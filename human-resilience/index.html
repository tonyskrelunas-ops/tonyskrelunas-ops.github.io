Pillar one · Human Resilience

Fear travels down a family. So does calm.

What happened to the grandparents shows up in the grandchildren’s bodies — the flinch, the bracing, the short breath learned before there were words to argue with it. It runs the other way with exactly the same machinery. This page is about being the one it turns around on.

The weather

A great many people are living braced.

This is the one place on this site where numbers belong. They are not about anybody here. They are facts about the world, and every one of them has a name and a year attached at the bottom of the page.

Gallup asked adults in a hundred and forty-four countries what they had actually felt the day before, and published it in October 2025 as the State of the World’s Emotional Health. Thirty-nine in every hundred said they had felt a lot of worry. Thirty-seven said stress. Twenty-six said sadness. Each of those is at least four points higher than it was ten years earlier.

That is not a mood. A body that spends its days braced is running a specific piece of old machinery — the one that floods you for a tiger. It is good machinery. It is built for ninety seconds and a sprint. It was never built to be left switched on through a Tuesday.

Then the second thing, which is the same thing wearing different clothes. In 2023 the US Surgeon General issued an advisory called Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation. About one in two American adults reported loneliness. The advisory states the physical cost without softening it: lacking social connection raises the risk of early death about as much as smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day, and is associated with a twenty-nine per cent higher risk of heart disease and a thirty-two per cent higher risk of stroke.

And then the figure in that advisory that is not a percentage at all, which is why it lands.

150 → 40 The same measure for fifteen- to twenty-four-year-olds. Two and a half hours became forty minutes. US Surgeon General’s advisory, 2023
1 in 6 People worldwide affected by loneliness — linked to about 871,000 deaths a year, near enough a hundred every hour. WHO Commission on Social Connection, June 2025

None of this is a verdict on anybody. It is weather. People did not get weaker. The conditions changed, and the practices that used to hold a person steady got set down in a shed and the shed got forgotten. Nothing here is lost. It was set down, and it can be picked back up. The rest of this page is about opening the shed.

Panic is not a mood. It is a failure of preparation.

The premise underneath the whole practice

What passes down

A child learns how to be in a body before it learns a single word.

This is the middle of the page and the heart of the argument. What gets handed down a family is not only land and stories and a nose. It is a nervous system.

A way of being in a body: how fast the alarm goes up, how long it stays up, how easily it comes back down. A child learns that in the first years, in the arms of whoever is holding them, off the face and the breath and the tone of voice of an adult, long before there is any language to argue with it. Then, thirty years on, without deciding to, they hand it on.

The record on this is not thin. In June 2023 the CDC published ten years of its Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System: 63.9 per cent of American adults reported at least one adverse childhood experience, and 17.3 per cent — about one in six — reported four or more.

And those things do not stop with the person who lived them. In 2023 Lixia Zhang, Joshua Mersky and colleagues went through sixty-eight studies in the journal Trauma, Violence, & Abuse and found nearly all of them showing the same shape: a parent’s childhood adversity turning up in the child’s physical health, mental health, thinking and social life. Mostly not by any mystery. By the ordinary route — through what the adversity did to the parent’s own steadiness, and the parenting that came out of that.

That is the hard half of it. Here is the half that never makes the headline.

It runs the other way on exactly the same wiring. The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard has been saying it for years in almost boring language: children who do well in spite of serious hardship nearly always have at least one stable, committed relationship with a supportive adult. One. A relationship in which the adult’s own calm is available to borrow, over and over, until the child builds their own. That is not a soft finding. That is the transmission running in reverse, and it means the whole line can turn around inside one generation, on one person who got steady on purpose.

Which is why the calm comes first and not last. Not the reward at the end of the rebuilding. The ignition at the start.

Only a regulated people can build at all. The calm, and then the house.

The Council Fire

You are not being asked to fix your grandparents’ century. You are being asked to be the one in the room whose breath is not short.

Where the handing-down stops

The tools, and they are not complicated.

None of these needs a licence, a subscription, a therapist or a good week. They are what three old peoples arrived at separately, which is the strongest evidence there is for anything.

Start here · free, no email

The Stone Breath

Hold a small stone in your hand, or picture one. Feel the ages it took to come smooth. Give thanks to the water that carried it. Then begin.

In four. Hold four. Out six. Hold four.
Five unhurried rounds. About three minutes.

The long exhale is the body’s own switch for standing the alarm down — a count the old runners kept ten thousand years before anybody named the nerve it runs on.

Do it before the hard conversation, not after it. And do it in front of your children, out loud, where they can see you. That is the actual lesson. Not the count — the demonstration. A child who watches a grown person bring themselves down on purpose has just been handed something their grandparents may not have had to give.

Every one of these is a place the line stops. Not because anything was broken and got fixed — nothing here was broken. Because a child in the room learns what a settled adult looks like, and that is the thing that travels.

Longevity

My grandfather lived to a hundred and three.

He did not do it with a supplement. He did it with a life that had a shape.

Up before the sun. Prayer facing east, every morning, without a day off. Walk out with the sheep. Real food, most of it grown or raised within sight of the house. Work with his hands until the light went. People around him the whole time — family, neighbours, somebody always coming by. A reason to get up that had nothing to do with him.

Nothing on that list is exotic. Nothing on it costs money. And every single item on it turns up again in the places on earth where people most reliably reach a hundred.

The path to a hundred and three is not a secret. It is a schedule.

The Blue Zones found the same house from a different direction

Researchers went looking for the places where people live longest — Okinawa in Japan, Sardinia in Italy, Nicoya in Costa Rica, Ikaria in Greece, and Loma Linda in California, an hour from Los Angeles. Five places with almost nothing in common: different foods, different faiths, different centuries of history.

What they share is the shape. Movement built into the day instead of scheduled into an hour. Eating mostly plants, and stopping before full. A reason to get up in the morning that survives retirement. Something that downshifts the nervous system every day — prayer, nap, a glass of wine with people, a walk. Faith or practice of some kind. Family close. And a circle of friends who have known you for decades.

Loma Linda is the one that ought to end the argument. It is in Southern California, on the same freeways and in the same economy as everybody else. What is different is a community that keeps a weekly day of rest, eats simply, and does not leave anyone on their own. That is it. That is the whole intervention, and it buys years.

My great-grandparents were running a Blue Zone on Black Mesa before anybody had a name for it. So were yours, somewhere, a few generations back. Nobody invented this. We set it down.

What the studies keep finding

The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed the same men — and later their families — since 1938. It is the longest study of its kind. It measured everything you would expect: cholesterol, income, exercise, education. The thing that best predicted who would be healthy and happy in old age was none of those.

It was the quality of their close relationships. Not how many. How good. Loneliness, in that data, behaves like a disease.

Which is the same finding the Blue Zones arrived at by walking around, and the same one my grandfather would have told you without a study, and the same one that sits underneath everything on this page. A nervous system settles in company. It braces alone. Over eighty years, that difference shows up in the body.

Tools for a long life turn out to be tools for a happy one. Nobody has ever found two lists.

The hope, and it is not small

They were supposed to disappear. Their young are among the happiest on earth.

Lithuania. Banned, occupied, deported, the language driven underground and the printing of it made a crime for forty years.

They carried the books through the forests at night anyway. When the books were banned the grandmothers kept the songs going in kitchens, under the noses of soldiers. After 1944 farmers and teachers and students walked into the trees with rifles and stayed there for nine years. That story is told properly elsewhere, in his own words, and it is his to tell.

They tried to erase a whole people →

Here is the part that belongs on this page.

In 2024 the World Happiness Report ranked countries by age for the first time. Lithuania’s under-thirties came first in the world — 7.76 out of ten — while the country as a whole sat nineteenth. The gap was the story. It has not closed: in the report’s 2026 edition, on data gathered from 2023 to 2025, Lithuania’s young rank eleventh in the world while the country overall ranks twenty-eighth. Years on, the young of that country are still far happier than the country they live in.

That is not despite what they went through. The passage through it, together, is what bonds the happiness.

Tony Skrelūnas

A people who go through something together and come out the other side still themselves are bonded by the going through. The grandmother who kept singing is the reason the granddaughter is happy. The happiness is not amnesia. It is the inheritance of having got through, handed forward in a body that knows the family did not break.

It happened here, too. In 1864 the Diné were marched off their land — between two hundred and fifty and four hundred and fifty miles, depending which route the soldiers took — and held four years at Bosque Redondo. One in four of the people interned there did not survive it. On the first of June, 1868, the treaty was signed. On the eighteenth they started walking home. They came back, and the songs came back with them, because the songs had been carried in throats, which is the one place nobody can search.

Two peoples who never met, on opposite sides of the world, with the same instruction: you do not survive by forgetting who you are. Which is why the numbers at the top of this page are a weather report and not a sentence.

You do not make the calm. You retrieve it.

Which is why it is practised on the ordinary days

The house itself

The atmosphere of a home is a practice too.

Everything above is something one person does with their own body. This last one is not, and it is the one that decides whether any of the rest holds.

To be a far messenger, and to raise far messengers, you cannot live in a home or a community with bad energy.

Tony Skrelūnas

He means that plainly. Not as a figure of speech. The actual atmosphere of a house — what gets said at the table, what tone the door gets closed in, whether the grievance runs the whole evening, whether a person can walk in at six o’clock and feel their shoulders come down. A small child reads that room the way an animal reads weather, reads it every day for years, and it becomes their baseline. You can teach a child the breath in the morning and undo the entire lesson in the hallway at night.

The novel treats this as a kind of contagion, and that is fair. Someone who spends their days tearing at other people is not strong; they are drowning, and the room takes the water on. But it says the other thing too, and the other thing matters more: a wrecked frequency can be mended, and it does not take very long. A few days of the old practices — the quiet, the breath, the fire, the circle, real food, real rest, real faces, the land under the feet again, the screen set down — and a person starts to remember what their own calm felt like. Once they remember it, they want it more than they want the wrecking.

Which is where this page reaches the edge of what it can do alone. A person can get steady inside a house that is not. Nobody stays steady inside a community that is not. That work runs on a different clock — three generations, six ceremonies, elders who keep the date — and it has its own page.

Where the rest of it is

A framework is forgotten. A story is carried.

This page is the shape of the thing. The books are the thing.

Stone Breath is the practice, built around a single stone held in one hand — the door a person goes through on their own. The Council Fire is what happens when a whole people does this together: the fear taken out of the body first, and the building afterwards, in that order, because there is no other order that works. The nervous system passing down a family, and the tools for stopping it, are laid out there as they actually happen — in a story, to people, which is the only place that kind of thing can be laid out properly.
