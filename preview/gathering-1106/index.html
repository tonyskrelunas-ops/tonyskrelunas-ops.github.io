Community Resilience · How decisions get made

How meetings can be run.

In The Council Fire, twelve keepers from twelve peoples spend four nights working out how to sit together. What they settle on is a meeting any family, neighborhood, town council, company team, school board, nonprofit board or tribe could run next week: a circle with no head to the table, elders in the room, the young in the room, children at the edges, a stick that passes when the matter is hard, and nothing decided the first night. This page sets that meeting out step by step so you can run it wherever you are. Then it shows the rooms it was proven in, from a workshop at the Farmington Civic Center in 1998 to the way the Commission that wrote the Local Governance Act held its own meetings.

I am Diné and Lithuanian. I was executive director of the Commission on Navajo Government Development when the act was written, I have led community and tribal planning meetings for most of the years since, and The Council Fire is my novel.

01

The circle the keepers built.

The keepers in The Council Fire come from twelve peoples on six continents: a coal engineer from the Ruhr, a seed keeper from the Andes, a Diné woman who hauled water up the mesa road through twenty winters, a navigator from Aotearoa, a crofter from the Scottish hills, a rice farmer from Thailand. When they finally sit down together on a hill in Lithuania, the first thing they have to agree on is not the water or the seeds or the money. It is how to sit. They spend four nights on it and argue about most of it, and what they settle on is small enough that the book hands it back to the reader as a set of principles rather than a template.

Sit in a circle. Have elders in the room. Have the young in the room. Have children at the edges. Open with a moment that grounds you—a prayer, a song, a silence, a sharing of who your people are. Have rules of respect on the wall. Pass the talking stick when the matter is hard. Let the elders remind the group of basic values when the discussion gets heated. Recognize the ones who are already doing the work, and let them speak in that work’s name. Do not let one person become the whole thing. Rotate. Rest. Come back.

The Council Fire, Chapter Twelve, How They Sat

The chapter says that was the whole set, and I wrote it that way on purpose, because every one of those lines came out of a real room. The circle with the officials sitting among the people, the rotating prayer, the sharing of clans, the rules of respect on the chapter house wall and the talking stick for the hard matters were all recommended by chapter officials, elders and young people at a workshop we held in Farmington on July 8, 1998. The stone breath, the ten slow breaths of silence and the Guided Return came out of the twenty years of meetings after that, watching which rooms held and which ones came apart by nine o’clock.

Many peoples found the same shape.

The book is careful to say that none of this belongs to one people. In Chapter Fourteen the elders at the fire name the ones who found it before: the Haudenosaunee, whose clan mothers named the chiefs and could remove them, and who opened every major meeting with the condolence ceremony because a person carrying grief could not decide for the whole people; the ayllu of the Andes, where authority rotated by turn and was carried, not held; the Knappschaft of the German coal country, a miners’ brotherhood that kept a common chest for seven hundred years; the neighborhood assemblies of Kyoto; the elder councils under the tree; the village committees of the rice country, which kept the terraces working for a thousand years without a campaign or a vote; the Allmend of the Alps; the subak of Bali; the palaver, held under the tree until the talking was done; the assemblies of the Basque valleys sworn under the old oak; and the Diné Nat’aanii, chosen because the community had already seen him.

There were more. There always would be. Everywhere people had lived together for long enough to notice what worked, they had built something like this. It was not the property of one people. It was a shape that many peoples had found, in their own languages, in their own places, over generations.

The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen

The book shows the range on purpose. Hamish, the crofter, tells the keepers about a wool co-op of eleven crofts on the Scottish coast, nineteen once the next township came in. For sixty years the township had sold its clip croft by croft to one buyer, who knew by the third house what the first two had settled for. When they put the clip together, the first thing the co-op wrote down had nothing to do with the price. It was that no one gets above himself, then if your name is on it, you are at the fank, and third, the rule about meetings that took them longest to learn: you bring a problem plainly, and then you bring it as work: here is the thing, here is who does it, here is when. The chapter ends his telling plainly. Nineteen crofts now, he said. We set the price. He comes to us. That is a company meeting, and it would hold in any company.

My favorite explanation of why it works is Chariya’s, the rice farmer. Her valley has held a season of water meetings every year for something on the order of a thousand years, and she warns the keepers that the people who come and film it call it ancient wisdom, and that they will build the wrong thing if they believe that. The book gives her mechanism in one paragraph. It is the reason a kitchen table, a town hall or a chapter house can do what a capital cannot.

She said what it actually is, is that the man at the top of the hill has to look at the man at the bottom of the hill, in a room, every year, for the whole of his life. That is the mechanism. Not virtue. Proximity, and repetition, and no door out of the room.

The Council Fire, Chapter Twelve

02

The imported meeting manufactures a loser, and in a small place the loser is your relative.

I want to be fair to Robert’s Rules first, because most of the criticism of parliamentary procedure is lazy and I have run plenty of meetings under it. It is an elegant answer to a genuinely hard problem: a large body of strangers with competing interests and no shared history, needing a defensible outcome inside a bounded amount of time. For that problem it is fast, it is auditable, it cannot easily be captured by whoever talks loudest, and everyone can see how the result was reached.

The mechanism is a motion, a second, debate through a chair and a count, and every cycle of it ends in a majority and a minority. Manufacturing a loser is not a side effect of the format, it is how the format closes. In a room of strangers that is fine, because the loser goes home to a different neighborhood and the institution carries on. In a township, a family business, a school district or a chapter of the Navajo Nation the loser is your mother’s brother, his land borders yours, his children and your children will marry, and he will be in the room again next month and the month after for the rest of both your lives. You have not resolved a question. You have opened an account, and the format will make a deposit into it every month for forty years.

Four things follow from that, and none of them show up in the minutes. The format rewards procedural literacy over knowledge, so whoever knows how to move to table has more power than whoever knows the flood history of the wash, and within two years the meeting belongs to the people who learned the rules. It compresses everything. Three minutes at a microphone is not how anybody in a story-based culture has ever explained anything that mattered, so the explanation gets abbreviated into a position, and a position is the part of a thought that is easiest to argue with and hardest to learn from. It requires you to contradict a person in public, which in a great many cultures is a serious social act with consequences that outlast the agenda item. So people go quiet, and a chair trained in the imported format reads silence as consent, writes down unanimous, and adjourns. And it produces decisions nobody implements. A fifty-one percent decision leaves forty-nine percent of the community with no reason on earth to make it work, and in a small place forty-nine percent of the people is the whole implementation capacity.

We wrote the same thing down in 1998, in the proceedings of the Farmington workshop: “The current practice of holding elections in which there are winners and losers is divisive. Those who lose an election often feel they have been rejected by the community and feel their honor has been hurt.” A meeting that ends in a count does the same work in miniature, every month, and I have watched a great deal of money spent on training people out of what is a design consequence.

“That man could have carried us for thirty years,” Kiprono said. “We had him. We had him for free. And we ran him through a machine that was built somewhere else, and it broke him in eighteen months, and now he is gone.”

The Council Fire, Chapter Twelve

What agreement is, when it is not a vote.

Consensus is not unanimity and it is not a vote. It is the exhaustion of standing objection. The circle keeps going round, and it is a circle, literally, which matters, until nobody with a legitimate stake still holds an objection they are willing to state. That is a higher bar than fifty-one percent and a considerably lower bar than everybody agreeing. It does not require anyone to change their mind. It requires that no one is left standing outside the decision, because the person standing outside the decision is the person who will not implement it. It is slower in the meeting, materially slower, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to sell you the method. It is enormously faster in the implementation, which is where a government either exists or does not. A community can hold three fast meetings a year and govern nothing, or two slow ones and build the road.

03

Run it, step by step.

Everything below comes from two places, the keepers’ protocol in The Council Fire and the recommendations the participants wrote down at the Farmington Civic Center on July 8, 1998, and each step says which. Print it and take it to your next meeting. Change what does not fit your people, because a meeting form that is not yours will be performed once and quietly abandoned.

The book’s own protocol, For Circles and Reading Groups →

Before the meeting

The meeting is mostly decided before anyone sits down. These four take a week and cost nothing.

  1. Call it when it is needed, give notice, and feed people.

    The 1998 room was plain about this: Call a meeting when, and only when, needed, and set it for an appropriate and convenient time and place for all concerned. Plan the agenda in advance and screen the items so that what comes to the room is ready. Give timely notice with a clear date and time, and provide an activity such as a feast, because a meal is the oldest way a community says the evening matters. Make sure the young people and their representatives are invited, and organize around what already gathers people, the senior citizens’ meal or the youth council.

    Farmington workshop, July 8, 1998
  2. See each person alone first.

    In the book the elders go to each keeper alone in the days before the first night, one at a time, and ask what they had carried in that they should set down before the fire, and what they had left at home that they should have brought. A person who comes carrying anger from a fight at home will speak from that anger, and the whole council will be shaped by it without anyone noticing. The keepers also studied the Japanese nemawashi, the gentle asking of each person’s view before the room, so that when the meeting is held the decision has already been half-woven. At a town council or a company this is the phone call the week before, and in a family it is the walk.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven and Chapter Fourteen
  3. Name who will hold the room.

    From the back of the book: When it gets heated, somebody brings the group back to the basic values. That is a role and not a mood, so give it to a particular person before you begin, and let it be the steadiest one rather than the oldest if those are different people. And the one who convened the evening is not in charge of it, and should say so out loud at the start so that nobody spends an hour waiting to be called on.

    The Council Fire, For Circles and Reading Groups
  4. Decide the rotation.

    Decide who offers the invocation this time and who offers it next time. In 1998 the recommendation was to begin with an invocation, with respect to all religions, by having a traditional Navajo prayer at one meeting, a Christian prayer at the next, and a Native American Church prayer at the next. The keepers rotate it through every people present, Not the same people twice in a row, so that every tradition in the room is honored in turn and no one tradition becomes the room’s.

    Farmington workshop, 1998 · The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven

The room

The shape of the room says who is above and who is below, so make it say no one.

  1. Sit in a circle, officials among the people.

    Instead of a classroom setting, Officials should consider organizing their meeting settings in a circle to facilitate equal communication and participation. Officials should sit among the people as part of the circle to provide an inclusive process. The book sets it as law in the same words: They sat in a circle. Officials among the people, not above them. Nothing at the head of the room, because there was no head. Elders in the room, the young in the room, and the children at the edges, watching, learning what a council actually was by seeing one, not by being told about one.

    Farmington workshop, 1998 · The Council Fire, Chapters Eleven and Fourteen
  2. Put the rules of respect on the wall.

    Write them yourselves. The 1998 room asked that they include listening to the views of a young person that speaks, or always having an elder remind the group of basic values when discussions get heated. These rules should be put on the Chapter House wall and be communicated often. Read them aloud at the first meeting, and then again only when the room needs calling back to them, which will happen. The book records what happens then: Every time the rules were read, the room settled. This was not because the rules were magical. It was because the room had agreed to them at the start, and being reminded of the agreement was enough.

    Farmington workshop, 1998 · The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven
  3. Have something to hold.

    A talking stick, a feather, a stone, whatever the room agrees on. It is only for the hard matters, and the book is not embarrassed about it: if that is hard in your group then use something to hold, the way the keepers used a talking stick and were not embarrassed about it.

    The Council Fire, For Circles and Reading Groups

The opening

A meeting without an opening is a room without walls. Anything can enter, anything can leave, nothing is held.

  1. The Grounding, at the door.

    A breath in, and a breath out. A hand on the frame. A word said quietly, in their own language, to remember why they had come. Then everybody comes down together before a word is spoken, with the stone breath from the back of the book, four in, four hold, six out, four hold, five rounds. The reason is plain: a tight body cannot tell a story and a tight body cannot hear one. Skip it and you will have a discussion. Do it and you will have a telling.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen · How This Record Is Told
  2. The Opening: three things, in order.

    First, an invocation offered by one of the peoples present—rotating from meeting to meeting, honoring the different traditions in turn. Second, a moment of silence, held together by the whole circle, for the length of ten slow breaths. Third, the sharing of clans and relations, so that each person named who they were, whose child, whose grandchild, whose parent. The 1998 room named the same openings in its own words: the sharing of traditional tobacco, the singing of a song, the sharing of clans and relations, or a presentation by an elder of important teachings. The keepers’ elders said the sharing of clans was the one that mattered most, so that no voice spoke from ego alone. Every voice spoke as someone’s grandchild.

    The Council Fire, Chapters Eleven and Fourteen · Farmington workshop, 1998

How people speak

The order people speak in decides what the room gets to know.

  1. Every voice once before any voice twice.

    No one speaks over another. Every voice is heard. And from the back of the book, Every voice is heard before any voice speaks twice, and Listen to the young when a young person speaks, and not indulgently either. In my own rooms I add one thing. The people with the least positional power speak before the people with the most, because once the person with authority has stated a view, the knowledge held by everyone junior to them does not disappear. It just stops being said out loud, which for governing purposes is the same thing.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven · For Circles and Reading Groups
  2. Pass the stick when the matter is hard.

    The 1998 proceedings describe it exactly: On a particular topic, the person with the stick would express their views on alternatives relating to the topic. The stick would be passed to the next person. If that person does not want to express an opinion, they would pass the stick to the next, and so on, until everyone has had a chance to talk. The talking stick is symbolic and allows those that may be shy in a public setting to participate. The book says what it does: This gave the shy their voice, and it kept the loud from taking the room. It was a small thing, and it changed everything.

    Farmington workshop, 1998 · The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven
  3. Keep the reports short, and in writing.

    Train whoever reports to give short, clear reports and hand them out in written summary too. Put the routine reports at the end of the agenda or at the start while the room waits for a quorum, take the action items first, and type the minutes during the meeting, as some of our chapters already did in 1998, so that nobody waits a month to learn what was decided.

    Farmington workshop, 1998
  4. The Guided Return, when the room tightens.

    When the meeting had gone on for a stretch, and the elders saw that the room was tightening—that voices were rising, that faces were hardening, that ego was starting to speak in place of truth—one of the elders would call for the Guided Return. The whole circle would set down what they were holding. The elder would guide them through a short practice. A slow breath, held together. A remembering of why they had come. A brief silence. A word of acknowledgment from each person of what they had heard from the others. And then, only when the room had softened, the work would resume. The person you named in step three calls it. Nobody argues with the call.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen

The hard matter

When something real is on the table, the circle has four tools the imported meeting does not.

  1. Ask the One Question.

    Will this repeat the cycles of collapse, or does it honor the great-grandchildren? Put the real thing on the table, a development coming to your area, a decision at work, a purchase, a plan the family has been arguing about since spring. Ask it exactly as the elders asked it: Not whether it is profitable, not whether it is legal, not whether it is what everybody else is doing. Then let the silence run. The silence is the exercise.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven · For Circles and Reading Groups
  2. Seat someone for what cannot speak.

    Every council seats a person whose whole task is the water, and another for the soil, and another for the animals — not as a gesture, but with the same standing as anyone else in the circle, and with the same power to stop it. And the young must speak first about anything that reaches past this year, and be listened to, and not be thanked and moved past — because they are the ones who will still be alive in the part of the future being decided.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven
  3. One voice can hold it, and the objection stays in the record.

    Te Rangi, the navigator, tells the keepers that on the waka one voice can stop everything, and that it costs everything every time. The navigator says no. That is the whole procedure. What it buys is the fact that in the entire history of that ocean, no one ever sailed to their death because a navigator was outvoted. The council builds the same thing into its own rules, Any one fire can hold it. Not a majority. Not a supermajority. One, and a thing that is held goes back. It is redesigned, or it is refused, and either is honorable. At a kitchen table, on a school board or in a city council this means one thing. When a person with standing says this will break something that cannot be put back, their objection is written into the minutes in their own words, with their name on it if they want it there, and the matter goes back to be redesigned rather than carried over them by a count.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven
  4. Nothing is decided at the first meeting.

    Nothing may be decided in the first meeting. That is a rule. The first meeting is only for hearing it and letting it sit, because the old world’s worst decisions were nearly all made in the first meeting by people who wanted to get to lunch. The 1998 room reached the same place from the other direction. It recommended a planning meeting at the end of every meeting and two shorter meetings a month instead of one long one, so that a hard matter is heard at one and settled at the next.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Eleven · Farmington workshop, 1998

How a decision closes

A decision that is not work, with a name and a date on it, is a mood.

  1. Bring it as work.

    Hamish, the crofter, tells the keepers what his township’s wool co-op took longest to learn: You bring the problem and you bring it plainly, and then you bring it as work: here is the thing, here is who does it, here is when. Nobody says if this fails. So the decision goes into the minutes as the thing, the person and the date. The 1998 room asked for the rest: that the officials track the progress of implementation for each action item made at the Chapter meeting, and that a report on those action items be a regular part of every agenda.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Twelve · Farmington workshop, 1998
  2. The Closing.

    Each person would say one thing they were grateful for from the day’s work. A prayer of one of the traditions would be offered—rotating from meeting to meeting, as at the opening. A moment of silence. And then, one by one, each person would rise, thank the circle, and leave. No one would slip out early. No one would leave without the closing. The meeting had to be officially, honorably closed, or its work would not settle.

    The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen
  3. Plan the next one before anyone leaves.

    Involve the people in the room in planning the next meeting, identify the key people to invite, and keep a stable date and time that is advertised. Give the young people real projects between meetings, a community assessment, a history paper on the community, the volunteer fire department, because a youth who has done work for the community comes back to the circle as someone with standing in it. Then the children at the edges have seen a council, which is how the next one learns to carry it.

    Farmington workshop, 1998 · The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen

If the circle is reading the book together

The back of The Council Fire asks that you not run a book club but a council, with the steps above. After one voice has read a section aloud, the room asks the same three questions every time, in this order, forever.

Where is this happening in our own country? Not the country in the book. Ours. This town, this family, this work, this year.

What is the sign we have seen? What you have noticed with your own eyes that nobody has named yet. Somebody in the room always has one, and most of them have never once been asked.

What do we know that has to be carried—and who is carrying it? Name the thing, and then name the person, out loud, because the naming is the handing.

04

The proof: where we have run it.

The steps above came out of real rooms, and these are the rooms. On July 8, 1998, at the Farmington Civic Center, the Commission on Navajo Government Development held a workshop called Improving Chapter Meetings and Governance with Respect of Navajo Traditional Processes, and I wrote up the proceedings. We put three questions to chapter officials, administrators, elders and young people: how community meetings can be improved using traditional concepts, how a community builds consensus and a sense of community, and how those concepts can be built into the forms of government chapters would be able to develop. The room began by writing the vision of the chapter meeting it wanted. It wanted a meeting with a fun atmosphere and open discussion, focused on creating solutions, with the community divided into districts with selected representatives for each district and for the youth and elderly, with rules of respect that include respect for a youth’s views, with a stable date and time, advertised, and run in a less formalized fashion. The sentence the vision ended on went into the novel almost word for word.

People will once again consider it an honor to participate in nahat’a—the work of governance.

The Council Fire, Chapter Fourteen · first written at Farmington, July 8, 1998

The recommendations the room wrote that day, before, during and after a meeting, are the ones in the steps above, and the Local Governance Act carried the larger idea as a right: a certified chapter may develop an alternative form of government of its own design. Forty-five of the Nation’s 110 chapters have certified under the act since.

The Commission practiced what it was writing.

We met in Albuquerque, Farmington, Durango and Flagstaff. We held prayers over the documents and we made sure we were part of offering circles, and we made room for Navajo, Christian and Native American Church ways alike, because the people in the room were all three. The commissioners would give us an assignment at five o’clock and we would work until one in the morning so the Commission could reconvene the next day. We worked weekends and nights for about a year on the questions that do not usually appear in a governance bill: how to structure a meeting traditionally, and how to select leaders the old way instead of holding a divisive election with winners and losers. Sampson Martinez, a young Navajo rancher from the Ramah chapter who was getting his law degree at UNM while he worked with us, was my partner in that. We went to ceremonies together and spent our weekends at community events surrounded by medicine people, which is where a great deal of the act was actually understood.

I learned how a room is held before any of that, at the Grand Canyon Trust, from Jim Ruch, its executive director. He led a meeting walking the room with a cup of coffee in his hand, and whole communities would anticipate him arriving. I still picture it, and sometimes I do it.

The Colorado Plateau gatherings.

In November 2009 we convened the first Inter-Tribal Conversations on the Colorado Plateau at Colton House in Flagstaff: two representatives from each of ten tribal nations of the Plateau, hand-selected the traditional way by a planning committee. We ran the three days in the form the ancestors of this region used to create many of the natural laws, through story, teaching, song and art, with Native youth writers and artists documenting it rather than a court reporter. The group named four priority areas, water, health, language and culture, and sacred sites, and out of its direction the Native America Opportunity Fund was launched to support tribal work in those areas across the Plateau. Rex Tilousi, a Havasupai elder, said at the end of it, before I came here I thought we were the only ones fighting for these things. I feel we are not alone.

Every one of those rooms, the chapter house in 1998, the Commission’s own table, Colton House in 2009, ran on the same handful of things: the circle, the opening, the rotation, the stick, the elders, the young, and nothing decided in the first meeting. None of them needed a budget for it. None of it is particular to the Navajo Nation either, which is the point of the twelve keepers. A wool co-op on the Scottish coast and a valley of rice farmers run on the same things in the book, and so can a school board, a design team, a neighborhood association or a family deciding what to do about the land. That is why the keepers send principles and not templates, and why the steps above are short enough to print and carry into the room.

The Council Fire

The Original Instructions for the World We Rebuild

Twelve keepers of their peoples’ oldest knowledge are drawn to one fire as the systems running the modern world reach their limits. What they build there, the circle, the One Question, the four capitals, the Great-Grandchildren Company, is the foundation of everything on this site. The circle on this page is the one they sit in, and the back of the book hands their protocol back to you so the book itself can be read the way it was made to be read.

So do not run a book club. Run a council.

05

Tell me what your circle keeps failing to decide.

Council design, facilitation, and training your own people to hold it, for a family, a neighborhood, a town council, a company, a school board or a tribe. The first meetings I hold myself, so the room can see the form work before it is asked to believe in it. The last thing I do is hand it over, because a process that depends on an outsider is not a process. Write and it comes straight to me.
