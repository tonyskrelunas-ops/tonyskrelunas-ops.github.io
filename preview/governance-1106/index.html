A government that fits.

In 1998 the Navajo Nation passed the Local Governance Act, a law I helped write. It gave each of our 110 chapters the right to plan and zone its own land, raise its own revenue, choose its own form of government, and select its leaders in the older way if it wished. Forty-five chapters have certified under it so far. This page tells what happened, what was built, what changed once the authority was real, and what any community, tribe, region or organization deciding how to govern itself can take from it.

I am Diné and Lithuanian, I was executive director of the Commission on Navajo Government Development when the act was written, and I have spent most of the years since helping chapters use it.

01

Before 1998 every chapter was the same chapter, copied a hundred and ten times.

On the Navajo Nation a chapter is the local government, the community itself, and there are a hundred and ten of them spread across a land it takes most of a day to drive. Before the act each one had a single coordinator, paid about eighteen thousand dollars a year, working under the same job description from Shiprock to Lechee. That coordinator answered to the council delegate rather than to the people in the chapter house. A chapter could pass resolutions, run tally counts and manage grazing permits, and that was the whole list. There was no zoning, no land use authority, no local revenue and no taxing power, because anything worth building was approved, financed and controlled from the center.

All the while the chapters could see what a town could do. Kayenta Township had hotels, restaurants and real community planning. Chapters all over the Nation wanted green belts, architectural standards around development, their own planners, their own attorneys and accountants, and a share of the money their own land was producing. They had no lawful way to do any of it, so they passed resolutions asking.

The Commission, the task force, and a year of nights and weekends.

The act came out of the Commission on Navajo Government Development, where I was executive director. Albert Hale had won the presidency in 1994 on local empowerment, and there were two proposals on the table that did not agree. One would send the money and the authority down all at once, and the other wanted careful, conditioned delegation. The Local Governance Act is the compromise between them. An inter-branch task force drafted it, Peter Deswood led for the executive branch, I led for the legislative branch, and the judicial branch sat in the room with us. Larry Foster chaired the Commission, an older community leader with a business education who served as chief of staff for the legislative branch and later for the executive branch. Around us were young lawyers doing the hard work, Michelle Dobson, Larina Bahe and Helen Webster, staff like Ben Silversmith and many others, and the guidance of traditional elders and religious leaders, among them the esteemed medicine man Sam Begay.

The most prominent lawyer among us was Sampson Martinez, a young Navajo rancher from Ramah and an apprenticing singer, who was getting his law degree at the University of New Mexico while we drafted. He went on to become a full medicine man and to build a successful law practice, and he was key in getting business site leasing delegated to the Navajo Nation, so that leases for businesses on our own land could be approved at home. I honor him here.

We ran a full public policy process, with summits, workshops and meetings with elders, youth, chapter officials and the Navajo Nation Council, and most of it happened at night and on weekends. Research out of the Harvard Kennedy School was telling us the same thing at the same time. A government developed by the community itself, one that fits the old way of governing and the old way of selecting leaders, does better over the long run than one imported whole. A community allowed to fail and allowed to succeed does better than a community that is managed. That sent us into about a year of questions a governance bill does not usually ask. How do you structure a meeting traditionally, how do you select leaders the old way instead of holding an election with winners and losers, how do you write ordinances with that in mind?

As these laws took shape we made ceremonial offerings at the sacred mountains, and we studied how our people governed long before there were chapters, which is where the alternative forms of government came from, the Council of Nat'aa among them. We also needed a foundation to stand on. Every history of our government written up to then came from a European or Western research perspective, so we organized an effort to write one from oral history, in the words of the people who remembered it, and that book has sold out at the government development office several times since.

Tabled, then passed.

I was the main presenter when the act went to the Navajo Nation Council, and the Council tabled it. They wanted to see exactly how it would work, what an ordinance would look like, what the staffing structure would be, how each authority would be exercised and by whom. So I went back and wrote a full implementation plan, authority by authority, and brought the act and the plan together the second time. It passed with very little debate. By then President Hale had stepped down and his vice president, Thomas Atcitty, was in the office, and I remember the chief of staff ushering me in and Atcitty signing the legislation right there. It was a tremendous victory.

Shonto certified in 2000, among the first four chapters along with Tuba City. It adopted a Council of Nat'aa form of government, in which the precincts select their representatives through traditional nomination and discussion and a strong manager, the Atsilisdai Executive, runs the administration. Forty-five chapters are certified today, out of a hundred and ten, and I would rather tell you forty-five than round it up. 02

Five older ways of governing, given a statute number.

I did not invent any of these at a drafting table. Every one of them was already at work somewhere in the community, in the ceremony, the family, the grazing dispute and the way elders settled a hard matter. The work of the act was to give each of them a legal form that a chapter could adopt on its own resolution.

  1. Selection by recognition.

    In the older process it was highly honorable to be selected by your family group or community to carry its views and experience. It went to someone living an honorable life by the teachings and values of the people, and you did not apply for it. A certified chapter can write that back into its government, as Shonto did, with each precinct naming its representative through nomination and discussion rather than a campaign. What I wrote after the 1998 workshop still stands: the current practice of holding elections in which there are winners and losers is divisive, and those who lose an election often feel they have been rejected by the community and feel their honor has been hurt.

  2. Precincts, with seats for youth and for elders.

    The community is divided into districts, each with a selected representative, and the youth and the elderly hold seats of their own. A chapter's business then no longer waits on the old quorum of twenty-five people who could make it out on a cold night.

  3. A respectful meeting process.

    Sit in a circle rather than in rows, with the officials among the people. Open with an invocation that rotates with respect across traditions, the sharing of clans, a song or an elder's teaching. Write the rules of respect and post them on the chapter house wall, and pass a talking stick when the matter is hard. These came out of a workshop the Commission held on July 8, 1998 at the Farmington Civic Center, called Improving Chapter Meetings and Governance with Respect of Navajo Traditional Processes. I wrote them up afterward for any chapter that wanted them.

  4. A form of government of the community's own design.

    A certified chapter may develop an alternative form of government. We wrote the act in generalities on purpose, so that communities could create their own innovations of local law, keep the old honorable ways of selecting leaders if they chose, and use traditional governance terms in place of Western ones.

  5. Certification, with the books in order first.

    Before a chapter holds the new authorities it adopts five management systems, for accounting, personnel, procurement, property and records, and runs them for six months. What we had wanted was an annual audit, read out to the community, once a chapter handled real money. The five systems came in late at the request of the Auditor General, and I have said for years that they were a little drastic and are the main reason adoption has been slow.

Not by campaign. By recognition. The way it had been done for ten thousand years before there were ballots.

03

Beedih, the tools to fulfill their dreams.

When responsibility travels down and the money and the authority stay up, a community has been handed a workload, so the act moved the instruments themselves. A certified chapter can develop a comprehensive land use plan, zone, and create its own ordinances. It can set protected zones with architectural standards, green belts and limits on what may be built next to what, issue and manage business site leases, and enact a local sales tax. It can form regional agreements with other chapters to develop a project together and share the costs and the benefits, or pool an accountant, a lawyer and a development office between them, take over a central government function by delegation, and retain its own legal counsel, accountants and managers.

We built the on-ramps at the same time. Every chapter that certified received a grant of about a hundred and fifty thousand dollars, and a Community Services Program with offices in Tuba City and Shiprock helped with land use and technical assistance. The local sales tax was created right after passage as an incentive, and we went to HUD and got permission to use housing dollars for chapter planning, which is how a lot of communities got their first real plan. I went to Congress myself and sat with the Assistant Secretary of the Interior and our attorney to secure business site leasing authority for the Nation. The intent was to push it down to the chapters so they could drive their own development.

Chapters that certified have done their own zoning and their own planning. Shonto took the risk first, in 2000, stood up its own corporations with the right assistance, and launched a renewable energy company that sells everything from small home systems to large commercial ones, with the revenue and the expertise staying in the community. The local sales tax is collected and kept. I have spent years since then leading chapter planning processes, to improve the economy and protect the land, and the act is the reason those plans have somewhere to go.

The act was the easier half.

All three branches agreed that the whole Nation would help chapters make full use of these authorities, walking a chapter through taking over business site leasing, helping it take on a park, transferring a program and staying until it ran. That assistance has never been fully realized. The start-up programs went away one at a time, no chapter has yet taken over a central government function, and communities that are certified still watch the fees from businesses on their own land go to the center. Twenty-eight years on this is still infant legislation, with most of its potential ahead of it. The chapters coming back together as an association of certified chapters, asking as a bloc and piloting the first takeovers, is the next move. That unfinished half is the work I do now, standing up the systems, training the managers, negotiating the first delegation, and staying until a community can run it without me.

04

Six things that made it work, for any community that governs itself.

I opened a summit on the future of our government in 2012 by asking what Barboncito and Manuelito would build if they arrived today. What drove those men to risk everything, life and family, so that we could live here inside the four sacred mountains, keep a language, know the land and have our clans? They would have looked at what was working and built the government to match. A body with the form of a government and none of the substance is a design. The same design turns up a long way from the Navajo Nation, in a county that answers to a state capital, in a village working under a colonial-era code, in the field office of a company run from a head office two time zones away. These six made the act work, and I carry them into every governance conversation I have now, with a tribe, a town, a region or a company.

  1. Listen to the community first.

    Grassroots input came before anything else. We held focus groups and meetings with elders, veterans, traditional people, youth and college students across the Nation, and what they told us shaped the law.

  2. Trust community governance over the center.

    Governance close to the people is far more effective, and centralized governance did not work for our chapters. A community that decides for itself, and is allowed to fail and allowed to succeed, does better over the long run than a community that is managed.

  3. Build on a foundation of your own history.

    Tie the work to the culture and to how things worked from ancient times. That is why we made offerings at the sacred mountains, studied the old forms of governance, and wrote a history of our government from oral history when every book available had been written from a Western research perspective.

  4. Use the best governance knowledge from all over the world.

    We studied home rule, the research coming out of the Harvard Kennedy School and every option we could find, and we kept what fit the people and the land.

  5. Be patient. It is not a rush job.

    It took about a year of nights and weekends, the Council tabled it once, and we came back with a full implementation plan, authority by authority. Taking that time is why it passed the second time with very little debate.

  6. Monitor, update, correct.

    Do not try to write the perfect document. Effective governing means not being afraid, watching what actually happens, and improving on what you observe. We wrote the act in generalities on purpose, and it is still young law, with room to be corrected as the chapters learn.

Will this repeat the cycles of collapse, or does it honor the great-grandchildren?
