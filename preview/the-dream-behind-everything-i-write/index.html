The Books

The Dream Behind Everything I Write

I started writing because of the climate crisis and a dream I kept having of what is to come. Here it is in my own words, and the call it became.

November 26, 2026

The reason I started this writing was the global climate crisis, and my own dreams and visions of what is to come. The hard grounds. And that I was created for this purpose.

The Creator guides everything in my life. Every book, every story, every article.

The dream

In 2020 I put it in a letter to an old friend in Peru:

I have had dreams in the past that we will survive through a crazy period when climate change really escalates and speeds up the world’s disasters. In my dreams it happens fast. There is a post-apocalypse elder council that I am on that now reviews new approaches and technologies, to make sure that they are used for the right reason and that we won’t repeat the mistakes of the past. I’m not sure what that means, but maybe that we are on the right track.

I have written it down many times over since. It became the opening of The Council Fire.

This book began in a dream I kept having. Not a gentle wish. A solemn, recurring thing that arrived with the weight of prophecy. In it I walked through a world both familiar and shattered, and then, out of the silence, a council fire was lit.

I was led into a great round room beneath a vast starry sky, and I took a seat among my ancestors. Hopi dry farmers. Sámi reindeer herders. Peruvian healers, Māori navigators, the keepers of deserts and forests and steppes. Scientists and engineers sat among us as humble listeners. Our whole charge was to weigh every new thing that wanted to be built against one question.

Will this repeat the cycles of collapse, or does it honor the generations not yet born?

I have been awake inside that question ever since.

The hard ground

We felt it first in the wind. The winds turned volatile, arriving off their old calendar, gusting from the wrong quarter, no longer the steady relatives we had known.

We felt it in the rain. The soft female rains, the gentle ones that come walking slow and sink all the way in, that the corn drinks for days, grew rare. In their place came the hard male rains: too much at once, drenching and violent, running off the baked ground and carrying the topsoil away down the wash. Anyone who farms with their hands knows the difference between a rain that feeds and a rain that wounds.

The numbers say the same thing in their own language. In November 2026 the Climate Action Tracker projected about 2.7°C of warming above pre-industrial levels by 2100 under the policies in place today, and about 2.1°C even if every pledge and long-term target is met.

We are entering a time when we must be resilient to cross: the hard deserts, the waves and oceans bigger than ever. And then the rebuilding.

What I was shown

What I was shown, over and over, was the rebuilding. Not the breaking. The fire already lit, and the room full of people who had come through.

A window. A single light. In the worst winter the world had known. And then another, and another: small lights scattered across the dark of the whole earth. A hogan in the high desert, smoke rising. A stone croft on a snowed hill. A butter lamp in a monastery above the failing snows. A low farmhouse in coal country, its pumps still turning. And a fire on a hill, and a song carrying across the dark.

The light was the point. Everything else, the collapse, the hunger, the silence, was just the story of what broke. The light was the story of what held. And the keepers did the thing that was the whole difference between an ark and a bunker. They opened the door.

The call

So I set the whole of it down as a call to the human family: A Call from the Council Fire. It carries the Original Instructions from the back of the book, word for word, and what I am asking of each of us. You can read it, or listen to it.

Which means the crossing has come to you. Not to a rare few. To all of us. We are all the far messenger now.

Begin on an ordinary day. Hold a stone. Count the breath. Find a keeper, someone who holds a song, a craft, a language, three hundred plants, and has been told it is worth nothing, and feed them.

Wherever you are, whatever is breaking around you: the stone is still warm. The fire is still lit. You are not alone.

Tony Skrelūnas is Diné (Navajo) and Lithuanian. He writes The Ancestral Watch — Stone Breath, The Amber and the Stone, The Council Fire and The Far Messengers — and the working reader, Living as a Far Messenger. He is a PhD candidate; his dissertation looks at human and organizational performance through Diné and Lithuanian ancestral knowledge and the Japanese practices he has long studied. He holds a faculty appointment at 1440 Multiversity, where teaching is the center of the work now. He runs at dawn and hikes with a corgi named Roper.
