Tony Skrelūnas · Diné (Navajo) and Lithuanian

I learned everything by lamplight, and by helping Masan.

I was raised between worlds. My great-grandmother Masan taught me to run at dawn with a stone in my mouth, on the high land of Black Mesa. My Lithuanian grandfather drove twenty-five miles of dirt road often in my childhood to sit in our hogan and pass on a tradition that had survived Soviet suppression. And by the light of a kerosene lamp, books and letters arrived from farther still — Switzerland, Lithuania, Japan — and a boy with no electricity read his way toward a wider world.

Three traditions. One lesson, told three ways: the old people knew how to live, and they left us the tools.

This is where I share what she gave me, and what my Lithuanian grandfather and the teachers I found in Japan added to it, with anyone who wants it: a person carrying something heavy, a family that wants to hand down calm instead of alarm, a company that has to cross hard ground, a community rebuilding itself from the inside out. I grew up on Big Mountain and I have spent thirty years working inside tribal nations, so that is where the proof comes from, but the tools were never only ours, and they were never only for us.

The two pillars

Two pillars. One word.

Everything I do sits on one of two pillars, and they share the word resilience because they are the same work at two sizes. One is the person and the family, the nervous system you were handed before you could talk. The other is the place, the governance and the economy a community lives inside. You cannot fix one and leave the other alone for long, which is the thing my grandparents knew and most programs forget.

Human Resilience

The person, the family, the nervous system.

A calm body or an alarmed one is learned before language and handed down the same way, so when I say intergenerational healing I mean something you can practice on an ordinary Tuesday: the breath, the dawn, real food, sleep, movement, and a home quiet enough to teach in. The second subject here is long life, because my grandfather walked six miles a day into his nineties and lived to a hundred and three, which used to be common, and the research on the longest-lived people keeps arriving at the habits the old people never dropped.

You do not make the calm out there. You spend what you already stored.

Community Resilience

The place, the governance, the economy.

The foundation of this pillar is The Council Fire, the third book, where twelve keepers from twelve peoples sit down at one fire and work out how a community governs itself and rebuilds after the systems it leaned on stop answering: leaders recognized rather than campaigned for, the elders at the center, the circle meeting where a hard matter waits until it holds everyone’s best thinking. I did not work that out at a desk. In the 1990s I ran the Navajo Nation’s government development office and co-authored the Local Governance Act, which 45 of the Nation’s 110 chapters have adopted since, so I know what it costs to move authority back toward the people who live with the result, and what it gives back when you do.

Leaders recognized, not campaigned for. Power held close—the local council, not the distant capital. Elders and culture-keepers at the center, holding the Sacred Veto. Decisions woven in the circle until they hold the best thinking of everyone, not voted to a winning half.

Take a Stone Breath.

The count the messenger used on the fourth day. Before a hard conversation, a big meeting, or a race — pause and breathe the way he did.

Hold a small stone in your hand, or picture one. Feel the ages it took to come smooth. Give thanks to the water that carried it. Then begin.

In four. Hold four. Out six. Hold four. Five unhurried rounds. About three minutes. The long exhale is the body’s off-switch for alarm — a count the old runners had ten thousand years before anyone named the vagus nerve.

On the fourth day, the well was dry.

He came down off the last dune to the place the water should have been, and the seep was cracked mud, pale, and there was nothing.

A frightened man does one thing here, and it is always the same thing. His heart bolts. He gets up and moves — fast, in some direction, because moving feels like doing. He runs in the heat and pours his own water out through his skin into the sand. And they find him the next morning a few hours' walk away with the panic still on his face, and the well he needed sitting half a day north of where he died.

This man did not do that.

He sat down.

I have thought about that for eleven years. Not the courage of it. Anyone can be brave once. It is the trainedness of it — that at the worst moment of a crossing, with everything in his body screaming, the thing he reached for was a practice he had rehearsed a thousand times on days when nothing was wrong.

He softened his eyes to the whole horizon — not to search it, to widen it, because a narrowed eye is a frightened eye and a frightened eye reads nothing. He closed his fist around a small river-worn stone he had carried since the first morning and pictured it resting on his tongue, cool and smooth, which brings water back into a dry mouth by itself; I have done this and it works and I still do not entirely understand why. And he counted his breath the way he had been taught as a boy, to a rhythm like a slow drum: in for four, hold for four, out for six, hold for four.

He let the fear stand there beside him and gave it no chair to sit in.

That is the line I could not get out of my head. It is not mine — it is how his descendants tell it, and I heard it from a woman whose family has kept the telling. The fear is not the enemy. The fear is a guest who will stay as long as you keep offering it somewhere to sit.

And then his heart came down. And when it did, his quiet mind did the thing his racing mind could never have done.

It remembered.

A seep, half a day back and to the north, in a place no sensible person would look for water. His grandfather had mentioned it to him once — once — years before, in the way of a man who said everything once and considered repetition a form of doubt. It gave water only in the years the great rains failed everywhere else. This kind of year. The dry-well year. The telling says he sat a moment longer, checking the memory the way you check a knot, and that what he felt was not relief but something drier and more familiar: of course it is half a day backward. It could not be half a day forward. That is not the kind of story my family is in.

He got up. He went north. It was there.

And here is the part I love, the part that convinced me these were my people even though they are not my people: he laughed. Standing over a miserable brown trickle in the middle of the largest emptiness on earth, with two days still to walk, he laughed out loud, alone. Because among the ones who trained this way, the laughter was never the opposite of the calm.

It was the calm, arriving. The breath coming loose in a body that had decided to live.

We are the far messengers now.

Everything you know reached you because somebody carried it. Every story your grandmother told you, every song, every remedy, every way of doing a thing right: none of it grew where you found it. Those were the far messengers, and every people on earth raised them, the ones trained to carry what mattered across hard ground and arrive with it whole. We are in that kind of crossing now.

The comeback

College handed me an identity crisis. The old practice is what got me through it.

I was raised by my great-grandmother in a hogan on Big Mountain, no electricity, four hundred sheep, a ceremony every dawn, and I learned to read by kerosene lamp, and by that same lamp I read the philosophy of Japan, a body of practice I have studied ever since and drawn on in my own work. Studied, not inherited. When Masan died I lost my footing and failed out of college, and what brought me back was not rest but work that mattered, done beside my people, until the turn came and I understood I could be who I was. Everything since has been built on what that year taught me: capacity is not inherited, and it is not a personality. It is built.

Where I am now: a PhD candidate in Sustainable Development Education, writing on human performance and intergenerational healing through Diné and Lithuanian ancestral knowledge and the Japanese practices I have long studied. I hold a faculty appointment at 1440 Multiversity, in the redwoods above Santa Cruz, where teaching this work is now the center of it, and I carry it further out, leading sessions with First Nations and in other parts of the world, lecturing in Mexico, and convening retreats for people who carry weight for others. I advise hospitals, a major health-and-wellness development, and a robotics firm. Four novels in The Ancestral Watch, two of them already out in the world, and a working reader beside them. I run at dawn, and hike with a corgi named Roper.

The braid

Three traditions. Not a blend, a braid.

Two came by blood and one by choice. Each carries a discipline the other two do not. Kept separate, kept whole, and put to work together.

  • By bloodDiné

    Dawn practice, corn pollen, the four sacred mountains, and a way of counting wealth that has never once been in dollars. Raised in it, in a hogan on Big Mountain.

  • By bloodLithuanian

    The forest, the song, and the stubbornness of a people who were supposed to disappear four times and did not. Endurance as an inherited technology.

  • By choiceJapanese

    Read by kerosene lamp at fourteen and apprenticed to ever since. Restraint, repair, and the discipline of doing one thing completely before starting the next.
