Community Resilience

A community that holds together, the way The Council Fire tells it.

Healing first, because there is no them; leaders recognized rather than campaigned for; the circle meeting, where the elders can stop a thing, the young hold a full vote, and nothing is decided the first time; and the One Question, asked for the great-grandchildren.

This page is for anyone deciding how a group of people will govern itself: a town, a region, a company, a board, a nation or a family.

01

Every culture facing a breaking world kept a council fire — elders in the circle, the young in the circle, the children at the edges.

When the systems running the modern world reach their limits, the keepers of many peoples' oldest knowledge are drawn to one fire to remember what actually lasts. Over four nights of arguing they work out how a people can govern itself once the old machinery has stopped working, how a meeting is run, how leaders are found without a campaign, and the one question the elders ask before anything gets built.

This is the fiction that sits underneath all of the work on this page.

02

Six ideas the keepers settle at the fire, and what was built with each of them.

The keepers did not send templates. They sent principles.

  1. Recognition, not election.

    The person did not choose the office. The office found the person, through the community's own long knowing.The Council Fire

    What was built

    Elders gathering and building consensus to name a representative. Districts and precincts with seats for youth and for elders.

    Getting away from elections

  2. The circle meeting.

    They sat in a circle. Officials among the people, not above them. Nothing at the head of the room, because there was no head. Nothing may be decided in the first meeting.The Council Fire. Four humble rituals, the talking stick, the elders' veto, the young with a full vote, the objection kept in the record.

    What was built

    On 8 July 1998, at the Farmington Civic Center, the Commission held a workshop called Improving Chapter Meetings and Governance with Respect of Navajo Traditional Processes. The proceedings read like a design specification, because that is what they were.

    Run the meeting, step by step

  3. The One Question, the Sacred Veto.

    Will this repeat the cycles of collapse, or does it honor the great-grandchildren? If the answer is no, it does not happen.The Council Fire

    What was built

    An elder council seated alongside the board. The anti-drift principle, written into the bones instead of the values statement.

    The charter

  4. The Four Capitals and the Four Repairs.

    We are not inventing a new economy. We are remembering an old one, and we are not the first to remember it.The Council Fire

    What was built

    Every decision spends from four accounts. Empty three of them and the fourth goes too — in a company, in a community, in a person.

    The four accounts

  5. The Great-Grandchildren Company.

    The Great-Grandchildren Company was a machine for making a viable community.The Council Fire

    What was built

    The stake is permanent and un-sellable. If the business grows, the stake grows. If it is sold, the buyer honors the stake — and if the buyer refuses, the sale does not happen.

    A business with soul

  6. Healing first. There is no them.

    There is no them. There is only us. Only a regulated people can build at all. The calm, and then the house.The Council Fire

    What was built

    A community that can name who it is can hold authority.

    Healing at community scale

03

How meetings can be run.

What they settle on is a meeting any family, neighborhood, town council, company team, school board, nonprofit board or tribe could run next week: a circle with no head to the table, elders in the room, the young in the room, children at the edges, a stick that passes when the matter is hard, and nothing decided the first night.
