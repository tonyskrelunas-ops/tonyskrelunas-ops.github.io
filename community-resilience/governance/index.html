Community Resilience · Governance

A government that fits.

This page is for anyone deciding how a group of people will govern itself: a town, a region, a company, a board, a nation or a family.

It starts with my novel The Council Fire. Twelve people from twelve peoples on six continents sit down at a fire in the long northern summer: a coal engineer from the Ruhr, a song keeper from Lithuania, a seed keeper from the Andes, a Diné woman from a chapter like mine, a machine builder from Japan, a keeper of old books from the Armenian Highlands, a monk from Tibet, a navigator from Aotearoa, a songline keeper from the red desert of Australia, a herder from the Rift Valley, a crofter from the Scottish Highlands and a rice keeper from Thailand. Over four nights of arguing they work out how a people can govern itself once the old machinery has stopped working, how a meeting is run, how leaders are found without a campaign, and the one question the elders ask before anything gets built.

The proof that it works is further down the page. In 1998 the Navajo Nation passed the Local Governance Act, a law I helped write, which gave each of its 110 chapters the right to plan its own land, raise its own revenue and choose its leaders in the older way, and forty-five chapters have certified under it so far. First, how it can be, in the book's own words. Then what we built, what changed, and what any of you can take from it.

I am Diné and Lithuanian, I was executive director of the Commission on Navajo Government Development when the act was written, and I have spent most of the years since helping chapters use it.

01

How it can be, in the book's own words.

The chapter called The Council Fire opens by showing the council a hundred years on, running, before it tells how it began, because the beginning sounds like twelve worried people on bad connections unless you already know what it turned into. Somebody somewhere has an idea, a mine or a canal or a machine that can do a thing no machine could do last year, and in the world we live in now that person needs money and permission, in that order, and the land gets no say and neither do the people downstream. In the book it goes to the fire first.

Not to a government. Not to a committee that meets in a capital and issues a finding. It goes first to the circle of the place it would happen in — the people who would live beside it, who would drink whatever it put in the water.

If that circle says yes the thing is still not built. It is sent out to every council that joined the covenant, on every continent, each one reading it in its own season with its own elders in the room, the herders for what it does to grass in the fourth dry year, the navigators for the sea, the rice keepers for water arriving in the wrong month. The vetting is slow on purpose, and three rules govern it.

Nothing may be decided in the first meeting. That is a rule. The first meeting is only for hearing it and letting it sit, because the old world’s worst decisions were nearly all made in the first meeting by people who wanted to get to lunch.

The young must speak, and be listened to, and not be thanked and moved past — because they are the ones who will still be alive in the part of the future being decided.

Someone must speak for what cannot speak. Every council seats a person whose whole task is the water, and another for the soil, and another for the animals — not as a gesture, but with the same standing as anyone else in the circle, and with the same power to stop it.

And the elders must be in the room, holding the ego down and the anger down, and reminding the circle of what it agreed to when nothing was at stake.

The next rule is the one most governments would refuse, and the book argues it is the whole architecture. Any one fire can hold it. Not a majority. Not a supermajority. One. A single people, anywhere, who can see that a thing will break something that cannot be put back, sends it home to be redesigned or refused, and the book is honest that this is the most inefficient arrangement imaginable and the only one that has ever kept a small people from being outvoted into ruin by people who will not have to live in it.

How a meeting is run.

The middle nights of the gathering are where the book is most practical, and they answer every meeting that was ever run by whoever showed up loudest. The elders at the fire build the container first and let the work happen inside it.

They sat in a circle. Officials among the people, not above them. Nothing at the head of the room, because there was no head.

When a matter was hard, the talking stick was passed. Illary had brought it—a piece of polished wood, carved with the marks of her grandmother’s people. The person holding it spoke; the others listened. If a person did not want to speak, the stick passed on. This gave the shy their voice, and it kept the loud from taking the room. It was a small thing, and it changed everything.

The rules of respect were written on the wall of the council house. Listen to the young when a young person speaks. When the discussion gets heated, an elder reminds the group of the basic values. No one speaks over another. Every voice is heard.

The elders are seated in the circle with the twelve keepers, and the book is particular about what they are there for. And the elders were in the room. This was the piece that made the rest of it work. Their role was not to rule. Their role was to notice, before anyone else noticed, when the room began to tighten. The young are in every discussion, with the children at the outer ring watching, which is how the next generation learns what a council is by seeing one, and when the keepers set it down as law they gave each side its own job. It was the brake and the compass both. The young were the engine. The elders were the brake. Neither ruled. They needed each other, the way the meat needed the corn.

When the keepers went home they did not send a template to the communities that asked how to do this. The keepers did not send templates. They sent principles. This is the whole set, and it fits on a chapter house wall.

Sit in a circle. Have elders in the room. Have the young in the room. Have children at the edges. Open with a moment that grounds you—a prayer, a song, a silence, a sharing of who your people are. Have rules of respect on the wall. Pass the talking stick when the matter is hard. Let the elders remind the group of basic values when the discussion gets heated. Recognize the ones who are already doing the work, and let them speak in that work’s name. Do not let one person become the whole thing. Rotate. Rest. Come back.

Four small rituals, humble on purpose.

At a later gathering the keepers spent a day on ritual, because a meeting without one is a room without walls, and they were careful about it. The sacred ceremonies of the Diné stay with the Diné, and the same for the Lakota, the Māori, the Ainu, the Aymara and the Sámi, so what they built were four small things that belong to no one people and can therefore be shared, and I have run meetings on all four of them since.

  1. The Grounding.

    When each keeper entered the council house, before sitting, they would take a slow moment at the door. A breath in, and a breath out. A hand on the frame. A word said quietly, in their own language, to remember why they had come.

  2. The Opening.

    When all had gathered, the meeting would open with three things, in order. First, an invocation offered by one of the peoples present—rotating from meeting to meeting, honoring the different traditions in turn. Second, a moment of silence, held together by the whole circle, for the length of ten slow breaths. Third, the sharing of clans and relations, so that each person named who they were, whose child, whose grandchild, whose parent.

  3. The Guided Return.

    When the meeting had gone on for a stretch, and the elders saw that the room was tightening—that voices were rising, that faces were hardening, that ego was starting to speak in place of truth—one of the elders would call for the Guided Return. The whole circle would set down what they were holding.

  4. The Closing.

    At the end of the meeting, before anyone left, the closing would be held. Each person would say one thing they were grateful for from the day’s work.

    No one would slip out early. No one would leave without the closing. The meeting had to be officially, honorably closed, or its work would not settle.

You have done well. These are small. They belong to no one, and so they can belong to all. They do the work. And they leave each people’s own sacred ceremonies where they live. This is how a council for all nations must be built. Not by taking. By making, together, something humble enough to be shared.

The eldest in the circle, when the four were agreed

One more line from the book belongs beside every chapter meeting I have ever attended. An elder from one of the northern communities, asked how her circle was going, says: We meet on Wednesdays. Everyone comes. My great-grandchildren come. The mayor comes—but she comes as a neighbor, not as the mayor. That is the difference. We do not have a mayor at the circle. We have a Marta.

Recognition, not election.

Nobody at that fire had put themselves forward. Käthe had been named by the miners' widows who watched her walk the canal every morning for a decade, Nizhoní by her chapter, which had watched her haul water up the mesa road through twenty winters, Vahan by the men who tended the springs, and the book builds the whole shape of leadership out of that long watching, the way the Nat'aanii were named here before there were ballots.

None of them had chosen the role. The role had found them, through the long watching of the ones who had already been carrying it.

The person did not choose the office. The office found the person, through the community’s own long knowing.

Vahan's grandfather had told him the reason when he was young, and it is the sentence I would put on the wall of every government development office. The old world chose leaders by who could make the most promises. We choose by who has already kept the most. A person who has kept the agreements for forty years does not need to promise anything. The promises are written in the land, in the relationships, in the songs. Terms are short in the book too, so that nobody holds a role long enough for the role to become who they are. Terms would be short. No person would hold a role long enough for the role to become his identity.

The cost of the other way is told by Kiprono, whose community once had a man everyone went to with a grazing dispute or a family matter gone bad, never elected to anything, and was then told to modernize, with ballots and a proper count, which meant making him stand up in front of the people who raised him and campaign against his own cousin.

“That man could have carried us for thirty years,” Kiprono said. “We had him. We had him for free. And we ran him through a machine that was built somewhere else, and it broke him in eighteen months, and now he is gone.”

Somebody asks, reasonably, how a person knows they have been recognized if nobody campaigns and nobody votes. “You find out,” Te Rangi said, “because the work arrives.” Not by campaign. By recognition. The way it had been done for ten thousand years before there were ballots.

Will this repeat the cycles of collapse, or does it honor the great-grandchildren?

The One Question, which the book also calls the Sacred Veto. The Council Fire

The One Question, and what a veto costs.

On the fourth night the keepers name the thing that holds everything else, and it is one sentence, asked out loud about anything of any size before it is built, funded or switched on. The quick reference at the back of the book puts the rest of it in four words. If the answer is no, it does not happen. Not delayed. Stopped. It is the elders and the culture-keepers, seated at the center of the circle, who hold it.

The book does not make this easy on anyone. The morning after they name it, a clean-handed firm offers Käthe's valley a data center, four hundred jobs and thirty years of solar sales on land that has buried its work, and the circle goes around and around on it, which is the most honest account of a council meeting I have read anywhere.

This is what governance actually looks like, under the stories about it: tired people who need the money, arguing in good faith, afraid of being the fool who said no to four hundred jobs.

Kiprono's grandmother sets the question down in the middle of the circle like a stone, and Käthe is the one who answers it, because it was her offer to kill. That was the Veto, working. Not a rule handed down from above. A circle, holding one question against a real temptation, until the answer showed itself. Then, and this is the part most councils skip, they find the good shape of the same thing, a smaller center the valley owns, sized to the water it can honestly return.

Te Rangi, the navigator from Aotearoa, is the one who tells the council what a veto costs, because on the waka one voice can stop a whole voyage and eighty people get off the hulls and unload and wait a year, and nobody has ever argued with that voice in the water. He wants them to sit in the cost before they admire it.

“That is the job,” Te Rangi said. “Nothing happened. Nobody thanks you for the storm they did not sail into.”

And the councils keep the objection. When three outside experts spend eleven months with the keepers and one of them leaves recommending against funding, her report goes into the record unedited, because the objection is kept at full strength, in the objector’s own words, forever, so that the descendants can judge for themselves whether the ancestors answered it or only outlived it. The line the keepers wrote the night the grid engineer broke their plan and then rebuilt it belongs at the start of every planning process.

An expert asked to bless a plan is a decoration. An expert asked to break a plan is an engineer. Ask early enough that the answer can still change something.

Run it at home.

The back of the book hands the keepers' protocol to any reading group, family or board that wants it. So do not run a book club. Run a council. No head to the table, which means whoever convened the evening is not in charge of it, and should say so out loud at the start so that nobody spends an hour waiting to be called on. Every voice is heard before any voice speaks twice. And nothing has to be decided the first night. The councils treated that as law, because efficiency was the disease. Then the room asks the same three questions it asks after every telling.

  1. Where is this happening in our own country?
  2. What is the sign we have seen?
  3. What do we know that has to be carried—and who is carrying it?

02

Where it comes from.

The book did not arrive out of nowhere. On July 8, 1998, in the middle of drafting the act, the Commission held a workshop at the Farmington Civic Center with Stephen Sachs of Indiana University, called Improving Chapter Meetings and Governance with Respect of Navajo Traditional Processes, and the people in that room asked for the circle instead of rows, for the officials seated among the people, for an invocation that rotates between a traditional prayer, a Christian prayer and a Native American Church prayer, for the sharing of clans, for rules of respect posted on the wall and for a talking stick when the matter is hard. I wrote it up afterward for any chapter that wanted it, and twenty-eight years later Illary carries the stick to a hill in Lithuania and the keepers write the same rules on the wall of the council house.

The research came the same way, from the Harvard Kennedy School work on cultural match, which found across many tribal nations that a government shaped to fit a people's own culture and its own way of choosing leaders does better over the long run than one imported whole, and from how our own people selected a Nat'aanii, who was considered wise, a good citizen, of good family standing and knowledgeable of important life skills, and who did not put his name forward. Then I kept finding the same shape in other places, the clan mothers of the Haudenosaunee who could name a chief and unname him, the ayllu of the Andes where authority rotated by turn, the subak councils of Bali that moved one mountain's water through ten thousand fields for a thousand years, the Allmend of the Alps older than any parliament in its own country, the palaver held under the tree until the talking was done, and the method of the book is the method of my research, which is to name the ancestral principle, show it turning up in peoples who never met, and then turn it into steps a chapter, a town or a company can use on a Tuesday night.

The evidence was not regional. It was human.

Even the four small rituals were made by study, the keepers looking at the architecture of the Japanese tea ceremony and at nemawashi, the quiet preparation that half-weaves a decision before the room ever meets, at the long silence of a Quaker meeting, at the libation poured to the ancestors before a gathering eats, at the Sámi joik that carries a person or a place into the room, and borrowing only what could be borrowed with permission and gratitude, which is the same care I learned in Japan as a student and have tried to bring to every chapter house since.

A novel let me show the whole system running at once, the meeting and the selection and the veto and the four kinds of wealth, in one place, through people who could argue with each other about it, which a policy paper never can, and the back matter turns it into a protocol anyone can run. The chapters of this story that follow are the part I can vouch for with dates and names, what we built in 1998, what it changed, and what it taught me.

03

Before 1998 every chapter was the same chapter, copied a hundred and ten times.

On the Navajo Nation a chapter is the local government, the community itself, and there are a hundred and ten of them spread across a land it takes most of a day to drive. Before the act each one had a single coordinator, paid about eighteen thousand dollars a year, working under the same job description from Shiprock to Lechee. That coordinator answered to the council delegate rather than to the people in the chapter house. A chapter could pass resolutions, run tally counts and manage grazing permits, and that was the whole list. There was no zoning, no land use authority, no local revenue and no taxing power, because anything worth building was approved, financed and controlled from the center.

All the while the chapters could see what a town could do. Kayenta Township had hotels, restaurants and real community planning. Chapters all over the Nation wanted green belts, architectural standards around development, their own planners, their own attorneys and accountants, and a share of the money their own land was producing. They had no lawful way to do any of it, so they passed resolutions asking.

The Commission, the task force, and a year of nights and weekends.

The act came out of the Commission on Navajo Government Development, where I was executive director. Albert Hale had won the presidency in 1994 on local empowerment, and there were two proposals on the table that did not agree. One would send the money and the authority down all at once, and the other wanted careful, conditioned delegation. The Local Governance Act is the compromise between them. An inter-branch task force drafted it, Peter Deswood led for the executive branch, I led for the legislative branch, and the judicial branch sat in the room with us. Larry Foster chaired the Commission, an older community leader with a business education who served as chief of staff for the legislative branch and later for the executive branch. Around us were young lawyers doing the hard work, Michelle Dobson, Larina Bahe and Helen Webster, staff like Ben Silversmith and many others, and the guidance of traditional elders and religious leaders, among them the esteemed medicine man Sam Begay.

The most prominent lawyer among us was Sampson Martinez, a young Navajo rancher from Ramah and an apprenticing singer, who was getting his law degree at the University of New Mexico while we drafted. He went on to become a full medicine man and to build a successful law practice, and he was key in getting business site leasing delegated to the Navajo Nation, so that leases for businesses on our own land could be approved at home. I honor him here.

We ran a full public policy process, with summits, workshops and meetings with elders, youth, chapter officials and the Navajo Nation Council, and most of it happened at night and on weekends. Research out of the Harvard Kennedy School was telling us the same thing at the same time. A government developed by the community itself, one that fits the old way of governing and the old way of selecting leaders, does better over the long run than one imported whole. A community allowed to fail and allowed to succeed does better than a community that is managed. That sent us into about a year of questions a governance bill does not usually ask. How do you structure a meeting traditionally, how do you select leaders the old way instead of holding an election with winners and losers, how do you write ordinances with that in mind?

As these laws took shape we made ceremonial offerings at the sacred mountains, and we studied how our people governed long before there were chapters, which is where the alternative forms of government came from, the Council of Nat'aa among them. We also needed a foundation to stand on. Every history of our government written up to then came from a European or Western research perspective, so we organized an effort to write one from oral history, in the words of the people who remembered it, and that book has sold out at the government development office several times since.

Tabled, then passed.

I was the main presenter when the act went to the Navajo Nation Council, and the Council tabled it. They wanted to see exactly how it would work, what an ordinance would look like, what the staffing structure would be, how each authority would be exercised and by whom. So we went back as a team and wrote a full implementation plan, authority by authority, and brought the act and the plan back together the second time. It passed with very little debate. By then President Hale had stepped down and his vice president, Thomas Atcitty, was in the office, and I remember the chief of staff ushering me in and Atcitty signing the legislation right there. It was a tremendous victory.

Shonto certified in 2000, among the first four chapters along with Tuba City. It adopted a Council of Nat'aa form of government, in which the precincts select their representatives through traditional nomination and discussion and a strong manager, the Atsilisdai Executive, runs the administration. Forty-five chapters are certified today, out of a hundred and ten, and I would rather tell you forty-five than round it up. The long version of this story, with the dates, the rooms and the people in them, is on its own page.

04

Five older ways of governing, given a statute number.

We did not invent any of these at a drafting table. Every one of them was already at work somewhere in the community, in the ceremony, the family, the grazing dispute and the way elders settled a hard matter. The work of the act was to give each of them a legal form that a chapter could adopt on its own resolution.

  1. Selection by recognition.

    In the older process it was highly honorable to be selected by your family group or community to carry its views and experience. It went to someone living an honorable life by the teachings and values of the people, and you did not apply for it. A certified chapter can write that back into its government, as Shonto did, with each precinct naming its representative through nomination and discussion rather than a campaign. What I wrote after the 1998 workshop still stands: the current practice of holding elections in which there are winners and losers is divisive, and those who lose an election often feel they have been rejected by the community and feel their honor has been hurt.

  2. Precincts, with seats for youth and for elders.

    The community is divided into districts, each with a selected representative, and the youth and the elderly hold seats of their own. A chapter's business then no longer waits on the old quorum of twenty-five people who could make it out on a cold night.

  3. A respectful meeting process.

    Sit in a circle rather than in rows, with the officials among the people. Open with an invocation that rotates with respect across traditions, the sharing of clans, a song or an elder's teaching. Write the rules of respect and post them on the chapter house wall, and pass a talking stick when the matter is hard. These came out of a workshop the Commission held on July 8, 1998 at the Farmington Civic Center, called Improving Chapter Meetings and Governance with Respect of Navajo Traditional Processes. I wrote them up afterward for any chapter that wanted them.

  4. A form of government of the community's own design.

    A certified chapter may develop an alternative form of government. We wrote the act in generalities on purpose, so that communities could create their own innovations of local law, keep the old honorable ways of selecting leaders if they chose, and use traditional governance terms in place of Western ones.

  5. Certification, with the books in order first.

    Before a chapter holds the new authorities it adopts five management systems, for accounting, personnel, procurement, property and records, and runs them for six months. What we had wanted was an annual audit, read out to the community, once a chapter handled real money. The five systems came in late at the request of the Auditor General, and I have said for years that they were a little drastic and are the main reason adoption has been slow.

People will once again consider it an honor to participate in nahat’a—the work of governance.

05

Beedih, the tools to fulfill their dreams.

When responsibility travels down and the money and the authority stay up, a community has been handed a workload, so the act moved the instruments themselves. A certified chapter can develop a comprehensive land use plan, zone, and create its own ordinances. It can set protected zones with architectural standards, green belts and limits on what may be built next to what, issue and manage business site leases, and enact a local sales tax. It can form regional agreements with other chapters to develop a project together and share the costs and the benefits, or pool an accountant, a lawyer and a development office between them, take over a central government function by delegation, and retain its own legal counsel, accountants and managers.

We built the on-ramps at the same time. Every chapter that certified received a grant of about a hundred and fifty thousand dollars, and a Community Services Program with offices in Tuba City and Shiprock helped with land use and technical assistance. The local sales tax was created right after passage as an incentive, and we went to HUD and got permission to use housing dollars for chapter planning, which is how a lot of communities got their first real plan. I went to Congress myself and sat with the Assistant Secretary of the Interior and our attorney to secure business site leasing authority for the Nation. The intent was to push it down to the chapters so they could drive their own development.

Chapters that certified have done their own zoning and their own planning. Shonto took the risk first, in 2000, stood up its own corporations with the right assistance, and launched a renewable energy company that sells everything from small home systems to large commercial ones, with the revenue and the expertise staying in the community. The local sales tax is collected and kept. I have spent years since then leading chapter planning processes, to improve the economy and protect the land, and the act is the reason those plans have somewhere to go.

The act was the easier half.

All three branches agreed that the whole Nation would help chapters make full use of these authorities, walking a chapter through taking over business site leasing, helping it take on a park, transferring a program and staying until it ran. That assistance has never been fully realized. The start-up programs went away one at a time, no chapter has yet taken over a central government function, and communities that are certified still watch the fees from businesses on their own land go to the center. Twenty-eight years on this is still infant legislation, with most of its potential ahead of it. The chapters coming back together as an association of certified chapters, asking as a bloc and piloting the first takeovers, is the next move. That unfinished half is the work still ahead: standing up the systems, training the managers, negotiating the first delegation, and staying until a community can run it on its own.

06

Six things that made it work, for any community that governs itself.

I opened a summit on the future of our government in 2012 by asking what Barboncito and Manuelito would build if they arrived today. What drove those men to risk everything, life and family, so that we could live here inside the four sacred mountains, keep a language, know the land and have our clans? They would have looked at what was working and built the government to match. A body with the form of a government and none of the substance is a design. The same design turns up a long way from the Navajo Nation, in a county that answers to a state capital, in a village working under a colonial-era code, in the field office of a company run from a head office two time zones away. These six made the act work, and I carry them into every governance conversation I have now, with a tribe, a town, a region or a company.

  1. Listen to the community first.

    Grassroots input came before anything else. We held focus groups and meetings with elders, veterans, traditional people, youth and college students across the Nation, and what they told us shaped the law.

  2. Trust community governance over the center.

    Governance close to the people is far more effective, and centralized governance did not work for our chapters. A community that decides for itself, and is allowed to fail and allowed to succeed, does better over the long run than a community that is managed.

  3. Build on a foundation of your own history.

    Tie the work to the culture and to how things worked from ancient times. That is why we made offerings at the sacred mountains, studied the old forms of governance, and wrote a history of our government from oral history when every book available had been written from a Western research perspective.

  4. Use the best governance knowledge from all over the world.

    We studied home rule, the research coming out of the Harvard Kennedy School and every option we could find, and we kept what fit the people and the land.

  5. Be patient. It is not a rush job.

    It took about a year of nights and weekends, the Council tabled it once, and we came back with a full implementation plan, authority by authority. Taking that time is why it passed the second time with very little debate.

  6. Monitor, update, correct.

    Do not try to write the perfect document. Effective governing means not being afraid, watching what actually happens, and improving on what you observe. We wrote the act in generalities on purpose, and it is still young law, with room to be corrected as the chapters learn.

Leaders recognized, not campaigned for. Power held close—the local council, not the distant capital. Elders and culture-keepers at the center, holding the Sacred Veto. Decisions woven in the circle until they hold the best thinking of everyone, not voted to a winning half.
