The promise

We don't send your leaders home rested. We send them home as Far Messengers — able to carry weight across hard ground, and arrive whole.

Why now

The ground is moving faster than your people can absorb.

Technology is accelerating past the pace any of us were built for. Systems that held for decades are under strain. Your best people are absorbing that load in their bodies, and calling it a busy quarter.

Rest is not the answer. Rest wears off by Wednesday.

Resilience is different. Resilience is trainable — and it has been trained, deliberately and successfully, by peoples who crossed hard country for thousands of years before anyone wrote a wellness budget.

The best teams do not run on pressure. They run on coherence — a nervous system that has remembered how to settle makes clearer decisions, hears people better, and creates from abundance instead of scarcity. That state is not a personality trait. It is trainable, it is measurable, and it is the quiet engine under every good decision and every person who chooses to stay.

The arc

Arrive. Awaken. Carry.

Day one

Arrive

We begin outdoors, on the land, with a land acknowledgement — never in a conference room. The first hours are spent settling the nervous system: breath, quiet, food, and a slow introduction of every person in the room to every other person in the room. Nothing is asked of anyone on the first day except that they land.

Day two

Awaken

The full day. Breathwork at first light. Sound and chanting. Movement — yoga, tai chi, qigong, or the land options your group chooses. The ancestral kitchen. And council: the oldest meeting format there is, where each person speaks once, uninterrupted, and the group hears what it has been too busy to hear.

Day three

Carry

Integration. We convert three days of experience into something durable: a personal practice each participant can run in ten minutes a day, and a written 30/60/90 plan tied to the work they are going back to. They leave with it in hand, not in a follow-up email.

The signature journey

The Far Messenger Path

Each person names, out loud, what they are here to carry.

A far messenger was the person a community sent when the distance was long, the ground was hard, and the message could not fail to arrive. They were trained for it — breath, pace, food, mind, and the discipline of arriving whole rather than merely arriving.

On the Path, every participant walks that role. They name their message. They walk it. They are witnessed naming it, which is the part that makes it stick. It is the hinge of the program, and it is the thing people are still talking about a year later.

Measured, not vibed

A documented rise, or we have not done our job.

Every participant takes the Far Messenger Diagnostic before they arrive and again thirty days after they leave. You receive an aggregate, anonymised report showing the movement across your group — so the investment is defensible to the people who approve it.

Your guide

Tony Skrelūnas.

Tony Skrelūnas is Lithuanian and Diné — raised in a hogan on Big Mountain, learning by lamplight after the chores were done, reading the philosophy of Japan by the same small flame, a discipline he would take as his own. College handed him an identity crisis and an inferiority crisis at once. He came through it, and finished at the top of his class.

Today he is a PhD candidate in Sustainable Development Education, and his doctoral research studies exactly what this work delivers: how the ancestral tools of breath, ceremony, and community hold up as a high-performance system for the modern world. He is a repeat commencement speaker, most recently to a doctoral graduating class, and the first person of Lithuanian and Diné descent inducted into a college of business hall of fame.

He knows how to move real institutions. A three-time executive and systems architect, he led the governance restructuring of a still-traditional nation — returning real ownership and self-determination to its local communities — advises companies and communities on four continents, and serves as development advisor to a major integrated health-and-wellness development. For a decade he convened Indigenous elders across the Americas in the old way — the living model this program is built from.

He is board chair of a Native-led nonprofit, retreat faculty, a runner and aspiring silversmith, and the author of the Ancestral Watch series.

Across all of it runs one conviction: the tools were never lost, only set down — and a person, a team, or a whole people can remember their power and rise into it. Money is only ever one instrument in that work; the real aim is wholeness that lasts for generations. For each engagement, Tony assembles and leads the practitioner team.

“Guides toward a deeper sense of belonging, purpose-driven action, and the courage to live from inner knowing rather than fear.” Emily Alicia Affolter, PhD · Associate Dean of Doctoral Studies

A note on the work he does elsewhere

Ancient tools, for the age of AI.

Tony advises technology and robotics companies. He is not romantic about the machines and he is not frightened of them. He is interested in the same question you are: what a human being needs in order to stay whole while building something that moves this fast.
