The invitation

Every one of us is carrying something we have not put down.

You are competent. You are needed. The days move faster than you do, and somewhere underneath all of it, you have gone quiet.

Where I come from, when the world gets too loud, you go down. Into the canyon. Where the walls hold the heat of the day, where the water still runs, where there is nothing above you but a long strip of sky and nothing asked of you but to keep walking.

For three days at 1440 Multiversity, we make that descent together. Then, on the last morning, we climb back out into the harvest — which is what the end of summer has always been for.

You do not change your life by adding more to it. You change your frequency.

The three days

A descent, a floor, and a climb into the harvest.

01
Friday evening

The Descent

We open outdoors, by the creek, with a land acknowledgement — then breath, fire, and the first story. Nothing is asked of you on the first night except that you set something down.

02
Saturday, all day

The Canyon Floor

Breathwork at first light. Meditation. Qigong and tai chi beneath the redwoods. A sound bath. A long, slow forest walk. The Ancestral Kitchen at dusk, then fireside and storytelling until the fire goes low.

03
Sunday morning

The Harvest

The climb out. We gather what the canyon gave you and name it out loud, so it survives the drive home. Closing circle, and you are on the road before the afternoon.

Whose ground this is

We open by the creek, and we open by saying whose land we are standing on.

1440 Multiversity sits on the unceded homeland of the Amah Mutsun, who have cared for these mountains, these creeks and these redwoods since long before any of us arrived, and who are here still.

On Friday evening we begin outdoors, at the water, with a land acknowledgement offered to the Amah Mutsun. We say their name first. We ask the land's permission before we ask anything of ourselves. Everything that follows in the three days rests on that.

The practices

Old tools. Used properly. For three days straight.

  • BreathworkThe fastest door into the body we have found. Twice daily.
  • MeditationGuided and silent, indoors and under the trees.
  • Qigong and tai chiSlow movement on the redwood deck, first thing.
  • Sound bathsBowls, drum and voice. You lie down; the room does the rest.
  • Forest walksLong, unhurried, mostly wordless. The trees do the teaching.
  • A seasonal healing dietSpecially designed for these three days and this time of year.
  • StorytellingTold the way it was told to me — at night, out loud, no notes.
  • FiresideThe last hour of each day. The part people remember.

The Ancestral Kitchen

Food is the oldest medicine on the mountain.

One night of the weekend belongs to Missy and Henry Webster — a Navajo family from Gray Mountain, Arizona, who cook the way their grandmothers cooked.

Blue corn. Juniper ash. Frybread pulled hot. They will tell you what each thing is for while they hand it to you, which is how the knowledge has always travelled.

Around it, all weekend, a seasonal healing diet built for late August: light, warm, alkaline, and designed so that by Sunday your body is running at a different speed than it was on Friday.

Your guide

Tony Skrelūnas

Diné and Lithuanian. Raised on Black Mesa by his great-grandmother, Masan.

Masan did not teach with lectures. She taught with the day: what time you rose, what you ate, what you said to the fire, how long you stayed quiet. Most of what happens at The Canyon Within is hers.

Tony has spent a decade convening intergenerational gatherings — cooks, singers, runners, elders, facilitators — and now brings that work to 1440 Multiversity, where he serves as faculty.
