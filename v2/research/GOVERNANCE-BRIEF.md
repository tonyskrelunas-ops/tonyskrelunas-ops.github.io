# 2. THE DIAGNOSIS — what was structurally wrong before the reform

## 2.1 One template stamped on a hundred different places

**[PUB]** His central image, from S1:

> "Before the act all 110 chapters had a common coordination that was paid about 18000 a year same job description, all reported to council delegate, chapters made resolution to requisition things from the government, it was resolution govern..."

And the slide version, S8 p.1 — already almost publishable:

> "Before – all chapters same, one salary for coordinator who works for delegate. All only make resolutions. No zoning, no local revenues, no incentives – power and authority centralized"

**Universalized:** *Before the reform, every local community in the nation was governed identically. One administrator, one salary, one job description, reporting not to the community but to the representative in the capital. Communities could pass resolutions — requests. They could not zone, could not raise a dollar of their own, and had no incentive to try.*

**[PUB]** He names the failure mode precisely in S1: **"it was resolution govern."** A government whose only instrument is the request. This is a superb, ownable phrase — *resolution government* — for a body that can deliberate but cannot act.

## 2.2 The accountability break — the real argument

**[PUB]** The strongest formal statement is S5 (the official strategic analysis, section D, "A New Strategy to Consider: *The Community as Decision Maker*"). Quoted verbatim, then de-identified:

> "Experience, over and over, has shown that local communities that are run by distant governments never really get going. The primary reason they don't get going is because decision makers in Window Rock, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, or someplace else who are making the decisions, never pay the costs of their mistakes. Instead, local communities pay the costs."

> "Worldwide experience has shown that once the community is the decision-maker, then decisions and results get connected. If the community makes the decisions, then the community pays the costs of its mistakes and reaps the benefits of its successes; as a result the community's decisions improve. The community ends up implementing projects more likely to work because of the support of its citizens."

**Universalized and ready to publish:**
> *Communities run by distant governments never really get going. The reason is simple: the people making the decisions never pay the cost of their mistakes. The community does. Once the community is the decision-maker, decisions and results get connected — it pays for its own mistakes, reaps its own successes, and so its decisions improve.*

**[PUB]** The compressed version he used on stage (S8 p.5): *"Local decision making results in long term effectiveness – own successes and mistakes."*

## 2.3 What the communities themselves said they wanted

**[PUB]** From S1 — a list of grievances that reads as a design spec:

> "Chapters really had a desire to do their own planning, they wanted to create loa laws [local laws], wanted the ability to tax rev locally, hire more qualified managers, wanted their own attorneys, accountants, and manage their own affairs. Many chapters differ and are not the same, many wanted to do green belts to protect from development, architect standards around economic development... communities warned to get away from the 25 voters so we researched government options."

Note the last clause: **the quorum of 25** — a hundred-plus communities, every decision requiring twenty-five bodies in a room. **[PRIV]** the number 25 as stated; **[PUB]** as *"a fixed quorum that could stall an entire community's business because two dozen people didn't come out on a cold night."*

## 2.4 The pressures the reform was answering

**[PUB]** S5 lists the challenges verbatim, and every one universalizes cleanly:

> Declining Revenues · Federal Budget Cuts and Welfare Reform · High Population Growth and Unemployment · **Decreasing Cultural/Traditional Awareness** · Increasing Social Problems · Lack of money for basic services: trash, water, sewage, roads · Lack of public facilities: ball fields, parks, picnic areas · **Inability to respond to opportunities**

The fourth and the last are the two that make this an essay rather than a policy memo: *the culture was thinning, and the government could not move fast enough to catch anything that came by.*

## 2.5 [PRIV] The power crisis that made reform possible

From S2, and it must stay private because it is a named scandal:

> "Around 1989, the chairman could serve for many years and that chairman got served with a lot of power. As a potential result, you could have misuse of power... There was an effort by the council to take chairman [X] out of office and there were investigations. During that process, the council hired a legal team and created a branch system modeled after the United States government with legislative, judicial, executive branches."

**[PUB] as a principle only:** *The national government had already been broken apart into three branches — a Western design, adopted under crisis, in a hurry. Local communities remained under the legislature. The reform we then designed was, in a sense, the correction of a correction.*

His judgment on that borrowed architecture is important context (S18 is a third party saying it, but S14 says it in his own voice): the three-branch presidential system "resembles little of our traditional community-centered, local governments of the past." **Do not quote S18 as his.**

# 3. THE PROCESS — how it was designed, who was consulted, how long, what failed first

## 3.1 Sixteen years, not one bill

**[PRIV] full timeline** (S7, the scanned history page — dates are identifying):

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1982 | Government reform becomes a political issue |
| 1989 | Amendments create a Government Development Commission |
| 1990–92 | Public hearings: desire for local authority, accountability, empowerment |
| 1992 | Government Reform Act presented — **TABLED** |
| 1993 | Agency Executive Council formed |
| 1994 | Reservation-wide chapter officers conference: revision of the plan of operation |
| 1994 | Development of enabling legislation on local governance |
| 1995 | Proposed Local Empowerment Act and enabling legislation |
| 1996 | Inter-branch task force develops the act; council adopts a resolution |
| 1997 | Act proposed after extensive public review |
| 1998 | Passage |

**[PUB] universalized:** *The law took sixteen years. Reform became a political issue in the early eighties. A commission was created seven years later. Public hearings ran three years. The first full bill was presented — and tabled. It took another six years, a wholly redesigned proposal, and a second presentation before it passed.*

**This is the single most useful fact in the archive for a governance essay.** The heroic version of institutional change is a bill. The real version is sixteen years, one public rejection, and a rewrite.

## 3.2 Who was in the room

**[PUB]** From S1, with names stripped:

> "there was a large component that was guided by the traditional elders, such as religious leaders... working on the development of several options such as home rule, came up with the concept of the local govern acts"

> "we did a public policy process that included to get input on the legislation, a public policy development process where we had summit workshop meetings with elders youth, chapter officials, Navajo Nation council."

**[PUB]** The slide compression (S8 p.1) is already publishable almost as written:
> "Major public policy process to gain vision – elders, youth, leaders. Deep study of old systems, culture, ceremony, work night/weekends."

**[PUB]** And the formal acknowledgment from S5, which is the warmest sentence in the whole official record:
> "The Local Governance Act was put together after a long-exhaustive process... Many discussions have taken place with [the] people of all walks of life, in chapter houses, at fairs, in homes, and workshops and conferences."

*In homes.* Keep that.

## 3.3 The cast [PRIV — never publish names]

Young lawyers (four named in S1), traditional elders and religious leaders (two named), a commission on government development, an **inter-branch task force** with a legislative-branch lead and an executive-branch lead, the department of justice, and the judicial branch consulted. **[PUB]:** *the task force was inter-branch by design — the legislature, the executive, and the courts each seated. A law that redistributes power cannot be drafted by only one of the parties losing it.*

## 3.4 The competing proposals and the compromise

**[PUB]** From S1:

> "at the same time the president of the Navajo Nation really touted the idea of complete local empowerment, there was two differing proposals, local employees were about giving all the dollars and complete includement of the people, the compromise was an inner naval nation task force"

From S2, sharper:
> "[The president] won because of his idea that chapters should have full on money from the central government and even out the authority... There was not a lot of accountability members and not a real careful delegation... One of the careful ways to compromise legislation was the Local Governance Act."

**Universalized:** *There were two proposals on the table. One was maximalist — send all the money down, immediately, and let the people decide everything. The other was cautious. What passed was neither: it was a compromise built by a task force seated from every branch. Authority would go down, but it would go down against a standard.*

**[PUB] This is the essay's honest hinge.** The compromise is what made it passable **and** what made it slow. Both halves are true, and he says both.

## 3.5 What failed first — and the fix

**[PUB]** This is the best process lesson in the archive (S1):

> "I was then main presenter and facilitator to present the first to the Navajo Nation council, they tabled it saying they wanted to see a full plan to see exactly how it would work, how ordinance would look, staffing structure, how it works described each author, development a full local governs act implementation plan and presented not a second time a delegation was passed with very low debate"

**Universalized and publishable nearly verbatim:**
> *I presented it to the national council. They tabled it. They said: we are not voting on a principle. Show us the full plan — what an ordinance would actually look like, what the staffing structure would be, exactly how this works. So we went away and built the implementation plan. We came back a second time and it passed with almost no debate.*

**The lesson [PUB]:** *A legislature does not reject an idea. It rejects an unfinished idea. The tabling was not a defeat; it was an instruction.* The document they demanded is S5 itself — the "Strategic Analysis" — which exists in the archive and opens: *"written… to help national decision makers, [local] leaders, and citizens learn about the benefits, options, and costs of strengthening their local governments."*

**[PRIV] flavor detail, too identifying to publish as-is:** the signing happened in a leadership vacuum — the president had stepped down and the vice president signed immediately: *"I recall chief of staff ushering me to the presidents office and him immediately signing, it was a tremendous victory."* Could be universalized as *"a change of administration mid-passage, and a signature the same afternoon"* if he wants the drama — but it risks pinning the era.

## 3.6 [GATED] THE STRATEGIC PUBLIC POLICY PROCESS — 7 steps

From **S6**, the flowchart. This is a complete, teachable, ownable methodology. It should be **named in the public essay and taught behind the gate.**

```
(1) ORGANIZE
      ↓
(2) ANALYZE EXTERNAL AND INTERNAL FACTORS
      ↓            ↘ (feeding: Community Values · Trends · Community Future)
(3) IDENTIFY KEY ISSUES
      ↓
(4) DEVELOP STRATEGIES
      ↓
(5) DEVELOP LOCAL GOVERNANCE PLAN OF OPERATION
      ↓
(6) IMPLEMENT LOCAL GOVERNANCE
      ↓
(7) MONITOR ────────→ back to (1)
```

The three ovals in the middle of the diagram — **Community Values, Trends, Community Future** — are fed by step 2 and feed step 3. That is the distinctive move: *the scan does not go straight to issues. It goes through values first.*

**[PUB] tease line:** *"We built a seven-step public policy process for it. Step one is not 'identify the problem.' Step one is organize. And the analysis does not run straight to the issues — it runs through the community's values and its picture of its own future, and only then to the issues. Skip that and you get a plan the community will not defend."*

## 3.7 [GATED] The parallel meeting-design workshop

**[PRIV] provenance** (S4): a one-day workshop, *"IMPROVING CHAPTER MEETINGS AND GOVERNANCE WITH RESPECT OF NAVAJO TRADITIONAL PROCESSES,"* July 8 1998, Farmington Civic Center, convened by the Commission on Navajo Government Development with a professor from Indiana University.

# 4. THE MECHANISMS

## 4.1 The seven-part structure of the act

**[PUB]** S5 page 2 lists exactly what the law does. This is the cleanest architecture statement in the archive. Universalized:

1. **Accountability first.** A community, *before it begins governing*, must adopt policies and procedures for: accounting · personnel hiring and employment · procurement of goods and services · property management · maintenance of records.
2. **Self-legislation.** Power to pass ordinances on both *community problems* (public intoxication, illegal dumping, curfews, uncoordinated growth) and *community vitality* (land-use zoning, architectural standards, tax revenues targeted at parks and recreation).
3. **Stability of governance and decision-making.** Separating and clearly identifying the responsibilities of *officials*, *administrators*, and *citizens*; a system of checks and balances; plus meeting reforms — 48-hour notice of meeting and agenda, alternatives to the fixed quorum (representative decision-making), guaranteed community education and input on every ordinance.
4. **Streamlining cumbersome processes**, such as approval of home and business site leases.
5. **A professional administration** — one community or several jointly: executive manager, accountant, planner.
6. **A community-based land use plan** — *"A road map to the future, a document upon which the local leaders can govern and be held accountable."* Approved by the members themselves.
7. **A framework for future improvements** — local taxes under a uniform local tax code; subcontracting with central programs for better service delivery.

**[PUB] The design principle worth naming in the essay:** items 1 and 6 are the load-bearing ones. *Before you may govern, you must be able to keep books. Before you may plan, the community must vote its own map.* Everything else hangs off those two.

## 4.2 The five management systems — and his own second thoughts

**[PUB]** Origin, from S1 — an unusually honest account of how a safeguard got heavier than intended:

> "In our development of the local governments act we first felt like it was important to give authority to the community, but we would monitor the accountability of the community through an audit, that if a community started managing large sums of dollars, that it would have to get audited. The auditor general... asked if we could incorporate a fine [five] management systems requirement, which includes personal management, records, financial accounting systems, property management, and it was requested by the auditor general... it also outlined that a chapter had to develop and adopt the five management system and the system had to be in use for 6 months before a chapter could seek certification, **that always was a really high bar that ensures this has been a really slow adoptions.**"

**[PUB]** And the retrospective verdict, from S2 — this is him marking his own work:

> "I think five management systems were a little bit drastic, it was really a last minute inclusion. The initial thoughts were that chapters would have to put in place accounting procedures, checks and balances, and there would be an audit requirement... There would be an annual audit that would be shared with the community. That was what we wanted, but the five management system requirements that lay out the system are really drastic and it has been **up to the interpretation of the auditor general.** It has made it really hard for chapters."

**[PUB] The universalized mechanism, and its lesson:**
> *We wanted one thing: if you handle real money, you get audited, and the audit is read out to the community. What we got was five full management systems — accounting, personnel, procurement, property, records — each written, each adopted, and each **in active use for six months** before a community could even apply. It came in at the last minute, at the auditor's request. It was reasonable. It was also the single biggest reason uptake was slow for a generation. And because the standard was written as a system rather than a result, the interpretation of it belonged to the auditor, not the law.*

**This is the most valuable single lesson in the entire archive for a public essay:** *the safeguard that kills the reform is never the one anyone opposed. It is the reasonable one, added late, by the person whose job is to be careful.*

**[GATED]** The five systems as an actual readiness checklist — accounting / personnel / procurement / property / records, each with the six-month in-use test — is a ready-made **Local Readiness Audit** product.

## 4.3 Precincts and nomination on honor

**[PUB]** His own slide (S8 p.2) lists among the big ideas:

> "Improve decision making – utilize effective traditional governance – **precints / nomination on honor**"
> "**Respectful meeting process**"

This is the phrase that already appears, universalized, in his author bio across the books: *"rebuilding representation on the ancient patterns of nomination and honor, down to how a meeting itself is held, in a way that nurtures rather than divides."* **That bio line is already public and already correctly de-identified. Match it.**

**The substance behind "precincts"** is in the workshop vision statement (S3/S4) — **[PUB]** with the one word swapped:

> "The community would be dissected into districts with selected representatives established for each district and for the youth and elderly."

So: *precinct* = the community subdivided into named neighborhoods, each seating a representative, **plus dedicated seats for youth and for elders** — not geography alone. That solves the quorum problem (§2.3) without holding an election.

**The substance behind "nomination on honor"** is in S3, and it is his clearest statement of why elections were the wrong instrument:

> "Another good example is how the early Dine' selected their Nat'aanii (leaders). The Nat'aanii that were selected were usually considered wise, a good citizen, having a good family standing, and knowledgeable of important life skills. **The current practice of holding elections in which there are winners-and losers is divisive. Those who lose an election often feel they have been rejected by the community and feel their honor has been hurt.**"

And from the vision statement:
> "When selecting leaders, the community would really evaluate the candidates using traditional methods instead of divisive elections."

**[PUB] Universalized, and this is essay-ready:**
> *An election produces a winner and a loser. In a community of a few hundred people who will still be neighbors on Wednesday, the loser has been publicly rejected by everyone he knows, and his honor is hurt. That cost does not appear on any ballot, and it never gets paid back. The older pattern did not have losers. A family group or a neighborhood **named** someone — and being named was itself the honor.*

**[PUB]** The full pre-contact selection sequence is in S14, and it is the most beautiful passage in the archive. Quote at length in the essay (nation-neutral already, except one word):

> "In selecting the participants, it was highly honorable to be selected by one's family group or community to represent the views and experience of your area. If you were living an honorable life following the teachings and values of the Tribe, it was truly an honor to be asked to represent. After selection, the participant was prepared in various ways depending on the issue to be resolved. The elders would talk and recommend appropriate courses of action. Various prayers, offerings, and sing were conducted. A sweat was conducted and the person would be highly respectful and mentally/physically/spiritually sound to discuss the issue."

Note what that describes structurally: **selection → preparation → briefing → cleansing → deliberation → report-back.** A modern legislature has only the first and the last.

## 4.4 [GATED] MEETING DESIGN — the complete four-phase protocol

Source S3 (with S4 as the original workshop record). This is a finished, teachable product. **The public essay should describe the *idea* — that a meeting is a designed object — and gate the protocol.**

**Phase 0 — the vision the community wrote for itself** (S3, quote in full, one word swapped) [PUB]:

> "In the future, we would like to see a [community] meeting that has a fun atmosphere, with open discussion and open-minded participants. The meeting would be focused on creating solutions to problems... There would be no bickering or fighting. The young people would be involved in all discussions and the elders would welcome youth involvement... A stable date and time would be set for meetings and meetings would be advertised... Harmony would be restored in the community and people would feel important... **People will once again consider it an honor to participate in nahat'a (governance).**"

That last line is the thesis of the entire body of work. **[PUB] universalized:** *"People will once again consider it an honor to take part in governing."*

**[PUB]** And the diagnosis it answers, in one phrase from S3: the community's own complaint was *"an argument always arises."*

**Phase 1 — BEFORE the meeting** [GATED]:
- Call a meeting **when, and only when, needed.**
- Agenda planned in advance; **agenda items screened to make sure items are actually ready to be discussed.**
- Timely notice, advertised, fixed time and date. **An activity such as a feast should be provided.**
- Educate the public *and the officials* on: (1) what a meeting is, (2) the difference between administrative and legislative functions, (3) types of meeting — substantive vs. routine.
- Officials trained to give short, clear reports; reports also submitted in written summary.
- Identify key people to invite before the next meeting.

**Phase 2 — DURING the meeting** [GATED]:
- **Seat the room in a circle, not a classroom. Officials sit among the people, as part of the circle.** (He repeats this three separate times in the source — it is the one he cares most about.)
- Open with an invocation, **rotating across faiths meeting by meeting** so no one is a guest in their own community.
- Open with a **symbolic bonding act**: sharing of tobacco, a song, the sharing of clans and relations, or an elder presenting a teaching.
- **Rules of respect, written by the community, posted on the wall of the meeting house, repeated often.** Example rules from the source: listen to a young person who speaks; an elder is charged with reminding the group of basic values when discussion gets heated.
- A **talking stick** — passed in turn; you may pass it on without speaking. *"The talking stick is symbolic and allows those that may be shy in a public setting to participate."*
- **Action items first.** Routine reports at the very start (while waiting for quorum) or at the very end.
- Split into **two shorter meetings a month rather than one long one** — with a dedicated "planning meeting" appended.
- Minutes typed live, in the room.

**Phase 3 — AFTER the meeting** [GATED]:
- **Track implementation of every action item, and make the progress report a standing agenda item.** (The accountability loop most community meetings never close.)
- Involve participants at the end in planning the *next* meeting.
- A newsletter if the community is active.
- Youth projects: community assessments, planning, writing the community's own history, volunteer fire service.
- Work through the schools: civic participation in the curriculum; **recognize community service the way athletics is recognized, with a letter.**

**[PUB] The framing line for the essay:** *We spent about a year studying how you structure a meeting traditionally. Not the law. The meeting. Because the law is only as good as the room it gets argued in.* (This is a light paraphrase of S1's *"we spent about a year studying how do you structure meetings traditionally, how could you instead of having elections, select leaders the old way?"* — which is itself publishable nearly verbatim.)

## 4.5 Certification — the gate

**[PUB]** Mechanics, from S1 and S2:
- A **certification process** stands between a community and the new authorities.
- Prerequisite: the five management systems adopted **and in use for six months.**
- On certification, a **start-up grant** was provided — *"they would give them a grant of about $150,000 which was significant at that time"* [PRIV: the figure; **[PUB]** as *"a substantial start-up grant"*].
- Support offices were opened in two regions — *"It was called the Community Services Program and they would offer help."*
- **[PUB] the honest half:** *"Once those programs went away, one at a time..."* — the grant and the help were front-loaded and then withdrawn.

## 4.6 [GATED] Alternative forms of government — three models

Source **S10**, the official policies and procedures. Definitions worth keeping [PUB, universalized]:

> *"'Alternative forms of chapter governance' means to give a new design, function or organization to the existing chapter government."*
> *"'Ordinance' means a local law, rule or regulation enacted pursuant to the [act]."*

**The three models on the menu:**

| Model | Structure | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Council–President** | Membership → Council (elected at large) **and** President (elected at large) → Administration under the President | The familiar mayor-and-council form. Two elected power centers. |
| **Commission–Manager** | Membership → Commission → appointed professional Manager | The city-manager form. Politics and administration separated. |
| **Council of Nat'aa – Atsilasdai Executive** | Membership → Council → Executive **appointed by the Council** → Administration | The culturally matched form. A single line of authority, and the executive is hired, not elected. |

**Detail of the third model** (the one that matters for the essay), all [PUB] with names swapped:
- Council of **5 to 9** members.
- The president, vice-president and secretary/treasurer are elected at large for four years; **the representative members are appointed by their respective councils or boards, by resolution, for two-year staggered terms.** *(This is the precinct/nomination mechanism in legal form — a body that is part elected and part named.)*
- **All powers of the community and the determination of all matters of policy are vested in the Council.**
- The Executive is *"chosen by the Council solely on the basis of executive and administrative qualification"* and **need not be a resident of the community.** (A deliberate anti-nepotism, pro-competence clause.)
- The **anti-interference clause**, which is the sharpest piece of institutional design in the whole archive:

> "The Council of Nat'aa shall act in all matters as a body, and **it is contrary to the spirit of this form of government for any of its members to seek individually to influence the official acts of the [Executive]**, or any other officer, or for the Council or any of its members to direct or request the appointment of any person to, or his or her removal from, office; or to interfere in any way with the performance by such officers of their duties. The council and its members shall deal with the administration **solely through the [Executive]**, either publicly or privately. Nothing herein... shall prevent the council from appointing committees... to conduct investigations. Any council member violating the provision of this section shall, upon conviction thereof in the courts..., **be disqualified as a council member.**"

**[PUB] Why this matters:** *In small communities the fatal disease of local government is the phone call — a council member ringing the manager about a cousin's job. We wrote a clause that made that a removable offense. The council may investigate anything, as a body, in public. It may not lean on anyone, privately, alone.*

**[GATED] The adoption procedure — a real five-step sequence:**
1. Registered members adopt a resolution authorizing the **study** of an alternative form.
2. Members appoint an **Alternative Governance Ordinance Development Committee** from their own number.
3. The committee **recommends the model** that best fits the community.
4. The committee **drafts the ordinance** — defining duties, responsibilities and functions of elected and appointed officials, **and the extent of authorities delegated from the membership to the alternative government.**
5. **120 days** from appointment to completed draft, unless the membership sets otherwise. Then adoption, then filing with eight named central offices, then a committee resolution approving the model.

**[PUB] The elegant part:** a community that invents a workable model gets it **added to the national menu** for everyone else — *"the Committee further amends the Alternative Forms of Governance Models Policies and Procedures to include the model attached hereto."* Local invention becomes national option. That is a designed innovation pipeline, and it deserves a paragraph in the essay.

**[PUB]** Also: the ordinance may be **amended or rescinded** by the same process. *A community can change its form of government, and change it back.*

## 4.7 Authority transfer — the mechanism nobody used

**[PUB]** Six statutory hooks, from S11/S15 (universalize the citations away):
- Enter agreements for the provision of goods and services.
- Enter agreements **with other communities** for a common goal or interest.
- Enter **intergovernmental agreements** with federal, state and other tribal entities.
- Enter **contracts or subcontracts with the national government for federal, state, county and other funds.**
- Enter **contracts or subcontracts for national general funds** with the appropriate divisions or programs for service delivery.
- Adopt zoning ordinances, regulatory ordinances, land-use plan amendments, and local fees.

**[PUB] The design intent, from S1** — this passage should be quoted at length because it is the reform's unrealized half:

> "we felt that it was important for the whole [nation] to assist the chapters with full utilization of all these authorities, that they would not only give funding but help a chapter take over business site leasing and help a chapter take over or develop a local park or take over management of park lands and enable that responsibility program transfer, it was always intended that there would be chapters that could come to together with [national] assistance to form regional governments or regional administrations, they could share accountants, they could share business development center, they could share lawyers, we envision that there could be joint inter chapter agreements, they could share revenue from an economic development project, **instead of fighting over the border of their chapter.**"

**[GATED] The transfer feasibility test** — S11 spells out the questions a community must answer before requesting a function. This is a checklist product:
> "what it would take to responsibly take over management of the [function], what lands such a delegation would encompass, what type of functions would be taken on, **how revenues, repairs, maintenance, and other functions would be paid for**, and how the areas would be managed and resources protected."

And the analogue that makes it legible [PUB]: *"As similar to the [federal self-determination] contracting process, where once a [nation] requests a delegation of federal functions a negotiating process ensues, the [national government] would most likely enter into a thoughtful negotiating process."*

**[PUB] Sequencing advice from the same source:** *transition it over a reasonable period; run the first one as an explicit pilot "to work out the kinks for other future delegations"; and once the delegation is clear, the first joint work product should be a management plan written by the managing parties together.*

# 6. RESULTS AND HONEST LIMITS

## 6.1 What actually happened

**[PRIV — hard numbers, all identifying]**
- 110 communities before the reform.
- **45 certified** as of 2022 (S9, stated publicly on a summit slide); *"Half Chapters LGA certified"* (S8).
- Four certified in the first two years; one named community certified in 2000.
- 27 years on, **zero** communities have subcontracted a central government function: *"As you can see, 30 years later, no chapter has subcontracted the local government responsibilities."*
- A council of certified communities formed — **and disbanded.**

**[PUB] universalized:**
> *About half the communities in the nation are now certified under the law. Not one has yet taken over a function of the central government — the authority the law was really written for. An association of certified communities formed early and then quietly disbanded.*

## 6.2 The roadblocks, in his words

**[PUB]** The slide version is a clean three-item list (S8 p.3, S9 p.5):
> "Roadblocks – staffing, full use of 5 mgt systems, [national] technical support"

**[PUB]** The long version, from S1 — the last sentence is the most important sentence in the archive:

> "we felt at that time that the [nation] was fully behind this idea, and they were, but the unfortunate thing was that attention, **that implementation assistance has never been fully realized and that is another obstacle to this day, maybe now more than ever.**"

**[PUB]** And the mechanism of that failure, from S2:

> "because that avenue was largely not supported and chapters themselves did not have the wherewithal to start those negotiations. You really need those skills to take over these responsibilities... Many of the divisions held everything. They still take care of hiring and still manage insurance, but **they don't really explore delegation of functions or contracting of central government services. There's very little qualified technical assistance. So, a lot of chapters have been left out on their own to figure things out.**"

**[PUB] The universalized diagnosis — this is the essay's hardest and best paragraph:**
> *The law passed. The authority was real. And then nothing much happened, for a generation. Not because anyone opposed it. Because the half of the design that was supposed to be an obligation — the central government's duty to help communities actually take these things up — was written as an intention. Intentions do not survive a budget cycle. The start-up grants ran out. The support offices closed, one at a time. The communities were left holding an authority they had no staff to exercise and no one to teach them. **A right you cannot afford to use is a right on paper.***

**[PUB] Second failure mode, also from S1** — the safeguard as chokepoint:
> "it also outlined that a chapter had to develop and adopt the five management system and the system had to be in use for 6 months before a chapter could seek certification, that always was a really high bar that ensures this has been a really slow adoptions."

**[PUB] Third — scale mismatch, from S2:**
> "things like business site leasing delegation has been difficult for larger communities. This still makes sense because you're basically taking over central government functions, but for smaller communities. Consequently, **it has not been worth it.**"

*i.e. the tool was uniform, but the communities were not. For a small community the compliance cost exceeded the benefit. A reform that abolished a one-size template ended up delivering a one-size on-ramp.*

**[PUB] Fourth — revenue never followed authority.** The most concrete grievance, from S2 [PRIV: names of the project and the community]:
> "I remember going to the [community] for approval. [They were told] we could promise all these things such as wells and there were all these agreements with families. But, none of those things have been fulfilled. So, a lot of the intent in all the discussions with communities was that these revenues would start going to the chapters. In reality, that hasn't been the case. When you look at [that community], they are LGA certified. They don't get revenue streams from [the major resort project], they don't have any participation in parks and recreation. You have [a world-famous canyon] and all these businesses in the community, but **all those fees are going to the central government.**"

**Universalized:** *One certified community sits beside a world-famous canyon, a major resort and a lake. It holds every governance authority the law provides. Not one dollar of the gate fees stays there.*

## 6.3 What did work

**[PUB]**
- **Certification itself.** About half the communities cleared a genuinely hard bar. That is real institutional capacity built.
- **Planning.** Federal housing dollars were unlocked for community planning; many communities got their first real land-use plan. *"A lot of chapters were able to do planning by using that money. So that was a good thing."* (S2)
- **Zoning.** *"Some of the chapters that have been LGA certified have done some zoning."* (S2)
- **Local sales tax.** Created and collected.
- **Community-owned enterprise.** Three live models in the archive [PRIV names]: a village-owned non-profit development corporation operating a hotel, a franchise restaurant and a travel centre; two community-controlled development corporations that keep their lease revenue and drive their own projects, one opening a hotel; a community that "gets the taxes locally" and runs a large annual fair professionally. *"This is hyper-local sovereignty in action, and it's professional."* (S17)
- **The alternative-government path is live.** S12 — a signed 2025 MOU in which a community operating under an alternative form of governance, holding leasing authority, is recognized by the central division as *"a sovereign entity and governing body"* that *"retains full authority over its land use and leasing decisions."* The division commits to *"facilitate support without infringing on SCG's decision-making processes,"* to approach *"humbly,"* and to *"support[] self-reliance by building [its] capacity to independently manage and oversee its economic projects from start to finish."* The community stands up a Business Management Commission and hires its own manager.

**[PUB] The closing beat of the whole story:** *Twenty-seven years after I helped write the law, I was the director of the central division on the other side of the table, signing a memorandum that recognized one community's authority over its own leases and promised — in writing — that we would help without interfering. It took that long. It is the first one that reads the way we meant it to read.*

## 6.4 His overall verdict [PUB]

From S2, verbatim except for one word:
> "So, 20 years later this is still **infant legislation that hasn't gone anywhere near its potential.** Much of the work needs to be done to educate the central government and ensure that you're not blocking chapters, but you're really helping in guiding them. The intent is not for the central government to totally tell the chapters what to do."

And:
> "Right now, stuff remains centralized. It's really an ineffective system, and it's slow to respond."

## 6.5 What he says should happen next [PUB]

From S2 — the forward agenda, and useful as the essay's constructive close:
- **Re-form the association of certified communities.** *"we need to come back together and form this Association of LGA certified chapters. There's a need to apply for funding. You really need to have them team up, and as a whole ask for central government functions to guide, oversee, and recommend different types of pilot projects like taking over central government functions. Helping other chapters think through regional issues is also important."*
- **Run pilots, not universal rollouts.**
- **Fix the technical assistance layer**, which is the actual binding constraint.
- **Let communities be unlike each other.** *"imagine an agency having their own economic development department and each one trusted with their own innovative selves and **they don't have to be alike.** You could get some cool stuff going on."*
