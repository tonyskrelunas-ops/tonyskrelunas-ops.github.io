## 2. THE FORM — HOW THESE GATHERINGS WERE ACTUALLY RUN

### 2.1 The theory Tony wrote down, and never revised

The two-page document `Gathering Background and History` exists in a 2009 version and a 2021 version. **The text is essentially unchanged across twelve years.** That itself is a fact worth using: he wrote the theory once and it held.

> "There was a process to create these teachings. A family or community group was made aware by a messenger of a problem. The problem may have been identified as such by careful observation by the Tribal observers. A gathering would be called to bring together several responsible and knowledgeable representatives from throughout the Tribe to discuss, recommend, and develop a solution. **The solution had to be crafted in a way that could be shared and identified with by Tribal members – thus song, teaching, law, art – were the mediums.**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

That last sentence is the load-bearing idea of the whole essay: **the output format is chosen for transmissibility, not for the record.** A decision that cannot be sung, told, drawn, or lived is not finished.

> "The meeting was designed with elements of prestige and honor using a traditional Native gathering process. It was conducted in ways that hearken back to the old methods that were used by Colorado Plateau ancestors to create many of the current Natural Laws—**using story, teaching, song, and art**."
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 2.2 Before the meeting: selection and preparation

This is the part modern meeting culture has no equivalent for. Two distinct mechanisms:

**(a) Selection is an honor, and the criterion is conduct, not rank.**

> "In selecting the participants, it was highly honorable to be selected by one's community. **If one lived an honorable life following the teachings of the Tribe, it was truly an honor to be asked to represent.**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "It was a high honor to be selected by one's community to participate in a gathering. **Only those who lived an honorable life and followed the teachings of the Tribe were asked.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report*, Executive Summary **[PUBLISHABLE]**

From inside the first gathering, the same point made about a modern government by contrast:

> "our traditional way of selecting leaders were based on **codes of behavior rather then economic status**."
> — participant, Breakout Session One, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE if unattributed to nation]**

**(b) The participant is prepared before arrival — mentally, physically, spiritually.**

> "After selection, the participant was prepared in various ways depending on the issue to be resolved. The elders would talk and recommend appropriate courses of action. Various prayers, offerings, and sings were conducted **so the person would be highly respectful and mentally/physically/spiritually sound to discuss the issue.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Tony re-states this as an operational requirement in his own after-action notes:

> "**Folks need to be in a good place .. spiritually, physically, emotionally …as would be required to represent and discuss issue of most importance. They should prepare in their cultural way to be of open mind, to not be overwhelmed, and clear of thought.**"
> — *Gathering III reflections* **[PUBLISHABLE — this is the most portable sentence in the archive]**

**(c) Recruitment was hand-selected, in person, and written to.**

> "Using a traditional approach and thoughtful criteria, the planning committee **hand-selected two representatives from each of 10 different Tribal nations**."
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "We conducted one on one meetings with folks and sent them detailed writings to prepare them for an active session. We provided the participants lodging, meals, and a traditional honorarium for their participation."
> — *Packard NA Gathering for Tony 8-2-10* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The invitation letter itself opens with the register it wants the meeting to have:

> "It is with **utmost honor and respect** that I write you to notify you of the upcoming third gathering… **Your commitment and wisdom has enabled significant progress** towards identification of the land protection needs of our tribal communities…"
> — *It is a privelidge to notify you…* **[PUBLISHABLE — quotable as an example invitation]**

### 2.3 The host, the space, and the food

> "Once at the gathering, a highly honorable space was provided. **A family or community would host the gathering. They would prepare long and hard – making meeting space, ensuring adequate food, calling on relatives to help with cooking, inviting elders to ensure honor and respect, starting the session with proper prayers and offering, and ensuring a great gathering.** Many of the representatives would arrive with the intent of working several days to solve and make progress on a matter. **They camped in the evenings. They had side discussions. A facilitator could be used.**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Note three design choices packed in there: (1) hosting is labour, and the labour is the welcome; (2) side discussions are *expected*, not leakage; (3) the facilitator is optional infrastructure, not the centre.

Space selection was deliberate every time:

> "The space was perfect for this meeting, offering **history and sense of place**, and in view of the sacred San Francisco Peaks. **Traditional foods such as Native teas, corn mush, and lamb stew were eaten for nourishment.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

At Gathering II, the host nation opened with a food ceremony:

> "Upon arrival, participants were greeted with **a traditional agave roast and interpretation of its meaning** by Hualapai traditionalist Drake Havatone. This practice has been passed on by the Hualapai elders **as a way of welcoming their visiting brother and sister tribes.**"
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "When we have a gathering of family/friends we invite them with a traditional agave roost, this demonstrates good hospitality, and offers others the essential nutrients needed from the sacred plant."
> — Drake Havatone, *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

By Gathering V the food itself was made into content — a chef cooked the theme of the meeting:

> "…local catering native Somana Suetopka reminded the participants of the wonders which come from traditional food… **the entire meal entrees and menu brought the gathering to an honorable level**…"
> — *Gathering_write_up_December_20* **[PUBLISHABLE, de-identified]**

### 2.4 Rotation of the host — every gathering moved

| # | Date | Host / place |
|---|---|---|
| I | Nov 8–10, 2009 | Colton House, Museum of Northern Arizona, Flagstaff |
| II | May 18–20, 2010 | Hualapai Nation, Peach Springs |
| III | Nov 17–19, 2010 | Moenkopi (Hopi Nation), Legacy Inn |
| IV | Jun 1–2, 2011 | White Mountain Apache, Sunrise Park Lodge, near Mt. Baldy |
| V | Dec 15–16, 2011 | Flagstaff (winter storytelling gathering) |

The host was chosen partly so participants would *learn each other's country*:

> "The location was selected by the elders **to ensure that each tribe's knowledge about each other's lands and cultures is revitalized.**"
> — *Gathering Story for E Newsletter* (Gathering IV) **[PUBLISHABLE]**

And chosen partly as a repayment of honor:

> "We should go to Havasupai and have a gathering there. **We need to respect the elders from there and go to them.** … **Honor the elders by having the next mtg. in Havasupai so the community can also be involved.**"
> — *2011 Fourth Gathering Minutes* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 2.5 Opening

Invariant across all five gatherings: **prayer first, by a person of the host or a designated elder, in their own language, before any business.**

> "As in every traditional gathering, **each day begins with traditional prayer. The prayer serves as a pathway for the daily activities**, it also gives appreciation to the holy elements for existence and blessings of life."
> — *2009 First Gathering Report*, Ch. 2 **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "He began his prayer first with his traditional cattail pollen as a blessing element to the presenters and participants… **At his invitation to use the pollen to do prayers, most everyone did so.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "Gordon prayed **for that there would be goodness in the food, that the Tribes would share their ideas, and that the meeting would accomplish its intent.**"
> — opening prayer, welcoming reception, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]** — note that the prayer states the meeting's purpose out loud.

Gathering V opened its second day off-site, before dawn:

> "The second day started with an early morning prayer service. **Gathering members caravanned to a location near the sacred San Francisco Peaks to conduct traditional prayers in several languages before dawn.**"
> — *Gathering_write_up_December_20* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Gathering IV opened with dancers:

> "our host, the White Mountain Apache, welcomed us with a group of young Apache Crown Dancers. **The elder leader of the group provided a blessing for us to achieve success with the gathering process**…"
> — *Gathering Story for E Newsletter* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 2.6 Introductions — the longest and most underrated block

At Gathering I, introductions consumed an entire morning:

> "As Jim Enote closed out his introduction, he invited the attendees to introduce themselves. **Many did so, in their traditional language. Eleven ancient languages echoed through one room**…"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

At Gathering IV the minutes record **twenty-plus** introductions in full, and they are not name-and-title. People introduce themselves by clan, by home, by what they are worried about, by what they have been doing with their hands that season:

> "Has been sheering sheep all spring with his 3 yr. old grandson who is just now learning. Now he's privileged to have a grandson and pass down that knowledge as a grandfather."
> — *2011 Fourth Gathering Minutes* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "is a runner. **Has celebrated his 54th year of planting. Knows when there are droughts. You can feel the land year after year and know when the right time to plant is.**"
> — *2011 Fourth Gathering Minutes* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "is feeling a bit under the weather because **throat is hurting from ceremonial singing over the weekend**."
> — *2011 Fourth Gathering Minutes* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The design point: introductions establish *standing* — why this person's judgement should count — rather than credentials.

### 2.7 The "Honorable Meeting Host" — the chair rotates to the host nation

Instituted at Gathering II and kept thereafter. **This is the single most transferable governance device in the archive.**

> "Most importantly, the Gatherings are intended to be traditional, with **ample time for prayer and free-flowing discussion. The first order of business was to select someone to guide the proceedings.** Drake Havatone was selected to serve as the first official **'Honorable Meeting Host.'** He proved to be a dynamic and quite deft host."
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "Phil, **in the way agreed upon by the tribes, took helm of leading the meeting discussion.**"
> — *Gathering Story for E Newsletter*, Gathering IV **[PUBLISHABLE]**

It appears as a standing agenda line: *"Welcome and select local Tribe honorable meeting guide"* (`Final Agenda for Gathering III`), *"Select a host tribe meeting person"* (`Agenda_Outline_for_Gathering_IV`).

At Gathering V the honor went to a long-standing participant rather than the host:

> "In the spirit of past gatherings, an honorable meeting guide was selected. Melissa Nelson, who has been with the gatherings from the start, took up the honor. **We proceeded with a traditional Navajo Mountain Blessing Song that is used to ensure good flow of thoughts and plans.** We then conducted a round of traditional greetings."
> — *Gathering_write_up_December_20* **[PUBLISHABLE]** — note the song is chosen *for its function*: good flow of thoughts.

Separately from the host, a **facilitation team** ran the content. At Gathering I it was three named facilitators, all Indigenous, from three different backgrounds, described as "professionally facilitated" and "culturally respectful facilitation." So the model is: **a host who holds the room, and facilitators who hold the work.** Two different jobs, two different people.

### 2.8 The role of elders

Not ceremonial garnish. Four distinct functions, all attested:

1. **Legitimating presence.** *"inviting elders to ensure honor and respect"* — listed alongside food and space as a host duty (`Gathering Background and History`).
2. **A separate voice from government.** > "The elected governments represent the Tribes, **they are not a voice for all of us, and we elders have our own voice. They need to hear our voices.**" — Jim Enote, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**
3. **Content authority.** > "**The Zuni Elders carry more weight than scientific viewpoint.**" — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**
4. **They set the agenda and disburse the money.** The 2011 funding subcommittee was five gathering participants (elders and cultural leaders), not staff — see §5.

The room also managed itself around them. When Havasupai elders arrived mid-introduction:

> "a contingent of highly esteemed Havasupai elders made their entrance. **The room grew silent.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

And elders were expected to *model* behaviour, not just bless it:

> "Several members felt strongly that elders, including those attending the Gatherings, **should model 'green' or sustainable behaviors for the youth.** This means, in part, bringing our own cups and water to future meetings, recycling and composting if possible."
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 2.9 The role of song, dance, and art

Three separate uses, and they should not be collapsed:

**(a) Song as a state change / transition device.** Songs are used at openings, closings, meal breaks and after heavy sessions — as punctuation between modes of thinking.

> "**At every opportunity, from the opening reception to the closing session, dancers and singers recited the ancient rhythms.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "Lloyd sang four songs that ranged from eagles singing, little children, grass dancing, and in most respect **he sang an Honor song that brought everyone to their feet in respect to all Honorable individuals of our lands.**"
> — Ch. 6, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

**(b) Round dance as a physical equalizer before hard conversation.** The clearest articulation in the whole archive of *why* a group moves its body together:

> "**The round dancing conveyed a simple message about the cycle of life and balance. Just being able to see each other's faces and smiles as we attempted to coordinate our feet in time to the beat of the drum brought a sense of balance to open up discussions.**"
> — Karen Francis, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — outstanding quote]**

**(c) Art as an independent record of the meaning of a meeting.** A painter, three writers, and photographers were *hired* to interpret Gathering I — not to minute it.

> "We secured three young writers and one artist to capture the discussion, create short story and poetry, and **create an art piece interpreting the meaning of the gathering.**"
> — *Packard NA Gathering for Tony 8-2-10* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**The meeting was documented and archived by Native American youth writers and artists as well as reporting and photography.**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The painting is then read back to the participants as a finding. The report devotes a full page to what the painting means — dark ground because "many stories told during the gathering were dark feelings of heartache and sadness"; a two-tone heart, half heartache and half "the hope of the attendees in the outcome of the gathering." **[PUBLISHABLE]** This is a genuine method: *commission a non-verbal summary of a meeting and let it tell you the emotional truth your minutes cannot.*

Songs were also sung *into* the deliberation itself — a participant blessed the Colorado River with sage smoke during a Gathering II site visit, and elders "offered songs, teachings, stories, and prayers to the sacred Colorado River at Diamond Creek." **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 2.10 The shape of a day

Reconstructed from the agendas (`Final Agenda for Gathering III`, `Agenda_Outline_for_Gathering_IV`) plus the reports. This is the recurring skeleton:

```
EVENING BEFORE   Reception. Food. Entertainment by the host's own people.
                 Welcomes from outsiders/hosts. No business. No decisions.

DAY 1  ~7:45am   Breakfast together
        8:00     Opening prayer (host nation, own language)
        8:15     Select the Honorable Meeting Host for the session
        8:30     Overview: why we are here, what we hope to leave with
        8:45     Introductions — long, unhurried, in own languages
        ~9:30    Session 1: BUILD THE BASE — the past.
                 "How was this done before?" (not "what should we do?")
       12:00     Lunch, with shared stories and song
        1:00     Guest speaker: an outside model — another nation that
                 has already solved a version of this
        1:30     Breakouts on case studies / prioritized issues
        ~4:00    Groups report back to full circle
        4:30     Break — deliberate long gap
        6:00     Dinner elsewhere, informal, side conversations expected

DAY 2  ~6:00am   Optional prayer walk / dawn prayer off-site
        8:00     Review of yesterday, out loud, before anything new
        ~9:00    Working sessions: subcommittees, funds, youth, projects
       10:00     Break AND "cultural sharing" (an agenda item, not a gap)
        1:15     Strategic discussion: the future of the process itself
        2:00     Closing comments — every person who wishes to speak
                 Adjournment
                 Closing prayer + closing song
```

Two structural notes worth making explicit in the essay:

- **"Cultural Sharing – Intertribal members" is a scheduled agenda item at 10:00am and again at 1:00pm** in the Gathering IV agenda. The relational work is *on the clock*, not squeezed into breaks.
- **Half a day of every multi-day gathering was spent outdoors, on the host's land.** Gathering II: a 20-mile trip to the Colorado River at Diamond Creek. Gathering III: a half-day sacred-sites tour with an anthropologist guide. This is not tourism; it is putting the subject of the decision physically in front of the deciders.

### 2.11 Closing

> "Before ending the meeting, **we asked participants to provide closing comments, an honor offered at traditional gatherings.**"
> — Ch. 11, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The closing round at Gathering I runs for pages and is the emotional payload of the entire document. It ends this way:

> "As in the beginning of the gathering Phil Stago gave a traditional closing prayer in the White Mountain Apache Language. **While giving the prayer, Lloyd Arrive sang a traditional closing song.** Everyone attending and all participants were at comfort and all was in beauty at this time."
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Note the two-at-once ending: prayer in one language, song from another nation, simultaneously. Gathering V ended the same way — "A closing song was conducted by Drake Havatone."

And the culminating gesture at Gathering I: an elder sang two goodbye songs, one in his own language and one in the language of another nation present, while his wife "danced around the circle in blessings for all to have safe travels." **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**We don't have such a word as good-bye in Supai.** We say 'Walk softly. Be careful. We will cross paths again, either in this life or in the spirit world. We will come together again.'"
> — Rex Tilousi, Havasupai, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — best closing line in the archive]**

### 2.12 What was deliberately *absent*

Worth naming, because absences are design decisions:

- **No formal resolutions, votes on positions, or press.** > "The elders wanted to ensure utmost honor, as **some of the types of projects to be funded are very culturally sensitive, thus no press coverage will be utilized.**" — *Gathering Story for E Newsletter* **[PUBLISHABLE]**
- **No governments in the room.** > "We made a decision **we don't want Tribal government there.** We had it (gathering). It was very successful." — Dale Phillips, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE, de-identified]** and > "**Keep this out of the Tribal councils**, sometimes some Tribal governments are sensitive." — closing recommendations, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE, de-identified]**
- **No pre-set agenda in the traditional model.** > "These processes happened naturally, **there was no agenda.**" and, in the list of features of a workable modern model: > "**No agenda, let process happen naturally**" / "**Pre-defined roles**" — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**. In practice the gatherings ran a *thin* agenda — Gathering II is described as: > "**A modest, focused agenda facilitated the free flowing discussion.**" **[PUBLISHABLE — the reconciliation of the two]**
- **One thing they wanted and didn't get.** > "**There should have been a fire here at this meeting.**" — participant, Ch. 5, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**. Tony logged it as a to-do in his private notes: *"Fire, no use of styrophome."* (`Gathering III reflections`) **[PUBLISHABLE — charming and true]**

## 3. HOW DECISIONS AND CONSENSUS ACTUALLY FORMED — THE REAL MECHANICS

This is the section the essay most needs, and the archive is unusually honest about it. **They did not run on pure consensus, and they did not run on voting. They ran on a sequence.**

### 3.1 The sequence, in order

**Step 1 — Establish the method before the content.**
The entire first morning of Gathering I was spent *not* on issues but on how decisions used to be made. The facilitators split the room into three question sets:

> "Questions for Group 1: **What was our traditional process for decision making? What were the conditions under which they were created? What were the strengths and weaknesses?**
> Questions for Group 2: **What are the current decision making processes? What processes do Tribes use to deal with matters today?**
> Questions for Group 3: **Do you know of a traditional modern model for decision making? What would a past and present balanced process look like?**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — directly reusable as a facilitation sequence]**

Framed at the top as:

> "**How can we utilize old ways and processes today? Why aren't we using the old ways today? Is it possible to use old processes today? If you created a solution, how would you share it?**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Note the fourth question. *How would you share it* is asked at the beginning, not the end.

**Step 2 — Surface everything, then condense, then rank.**

> "Ten classes of issues were identified and then condensed. **Three were ultimately prioritized for immediate discussion**: protection of water resources; protection of sacred sites; and promotion of health."
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

**Step 3 — The one moment of actual voting, and how narrow it was.**

> "Participants were then asked to **choose their top three issues out of the nine categories by casting their vote. Each participant had three pieces of paper labeled one, two, and three**, and each prioritized choices related to what is the most important to their Tribal community. **All agreed and voted.**"
> — Ch. 7, *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

This is the mechanic worth reporting precisely: **weighted dot-voting on three ranked slips, used exactly once, only to set priorities — never to decide a position or resolve a disagreement.** The vote narrows the field; the vote does not settle anything.

**Step 4 — Self-selection into the work.**

> "**The participants were then asked to participate in any of the work groups that appealed to them.** The work group conversation focused on fully discussing the issue, what is already being done, what is still needed, and **what could be completed over the next 6 to 12 months.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

**Step 5 — A closing conversation with a name, and a time-boxed question set.**

> "The final conversation, entitled **'Letting the Eagles Fly,'** focused on summary, next steps, and closure. **What did we learn over the last two days? How do we move forward with the keen insights and wisdom gained? What can we do in 3 to 6 months? What can expect to do in the next year?**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

**Step 6 — Between gatherings, subcommittees do the actual work; the gathering ratifies.**

> "Four subcommittees (Water, Health, Sacred Sites and Culture/Language Preservation) will select their top projects at meetings late this summer, and **final project selections will take place at the next meeting**… The process that will take place over the next five months includes **visits, research, and interviews with each tribe** to identify projects that embody challenges common to as many of the participating tribes as possible."
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The budget confirms this was resourced seriously: 2 full gatherings and **10 subcommittee meetings** planned for 2011 (`2011 Gathering Budget and SWOT Analysis`).

**Step 7 — Research is commissioned and read *before* deliberation, not during.**
For Gathering III, four researchers spent five months producing a baseline report on what all the nations were already doing in the four priority areas. The first afternoon of the gathering was spent reviewing it, section by section, with discussion after each.

> "We will spend the first afternoon reviewing and discussing this research **that will form an important baseline for the future work of this committee**."
> — invitation letter **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 3.2 What happens when the group does *not* agree

The most credible passages in the archive. Consensus was **not** forced.

> "Red Butte illustrated a **philosophical divide** that was displayed in several contexts. **One side, often the younger members, feels strongly that we should lean toward openness** to protect sacred sites… **Other members of the group are just as adamant** that drawing boundaries around sacred sites is antithetical to Native views on connectivity and non-ownership, and further exposes them to desecration."
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**Everyone agrees the project is an affront** to a sacred site and to Native people, **but not everyone agrees on the best way to proceed.**"
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The resolution mechanism is not a vote. It is **sequencing of action**, proposed by a peer:

> "He suggested Brittanni **start by promoting awareness within her own tribe, then invite Hopi help, with wider publicity of the problem used only as a last resort.**"
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]** — i.e., disagreement about *whether* is converted into agreement about *order and escalation*.

And the explicit statement that lack of traction is itself a decision:

> "Arizona bill SB1070 came up for discussion. One member felt that the group should make a formal statement opposing the legislation. **Though the idea didn't draw opposition, it didn't get traction either, and no statement emerged.**"
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — superb. Silence is not assent; enthusiasm is the quorum.]**

Same pattern for technology:

> "**These were not necessarily divisive discussions, but they underscored a need for a comprehensive education and outreach strategy** that will incorporate tradition and perpetuate it among the youth, even as it utilizes modern communication channels."
> — *2010 Second Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

And a note that disagreement *within* the group did not corrode it:

> "The dynamics between the Gathering participants are evolving, and **while in other processes such dynamics of exclusion, dispute, or even disregard may occur, there remains a deep sense of respect between the participants.**"
> — *Gathering_write_up_December_20* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 3.3 The group's own list of what a good decision process contains

Compiled by the participants themselves at Gathering I in answer to *"Do you know of a traditional/modern model for decision making?"* — reproduce close to verbatim; it is a checklist:

> - "No agenda, let process happen naturally"
> - "Pre-defined roles"
> - "Consensus of societies"
> - "**Protocols within tribes take precedent**"
> - "no exclusion of gender roles… respect religious foundations"
> - "Respect family process"
> - "**Immediate decisions may be more detrimental than not making a decision**"
> - "**Communicating — listening is better than saying**"
> - "**Believe in the power of our words**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report*, Ch. 5 **[PUBLISHABLE]**

Add to it, from elsewhere in the same session:

> "Individuals, groups gathered with roles defined. **There was no blame, no judgments.**"
> — Norman Lopez, Ute Mountain **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**It is important to take our time. Sometimes it took several days to come up with solutions**, and it was considered an honor by everyone to be a part of the process."
> — Tony (identified in the transcript only by his prior government role — **[PRIVATE: do not reproduce the identifying clause]**) **[the quoted sentence itself is PUBLISHABLE]**

### 3.4 Where authority actually sat — the answers people gave

The breakout on *"What are the current decision making processes?"* produced a map of real, working authority structures that has nothing to do with org charts. Useful as a global illustration that decision rights are usually social, not formal:

> "At Zuni we have the family level, matriarchal society. **The women on your father's side and the womenfolk basically make a lot of decisions. Major life decisions, I would ask my aunties what they think.** Village level, we're looking at religious level. **They have those all-night conversations. That is when things were under duress, really difficult.**"
> — Jim Enote **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**Go to immediate family. I would ask what should I do?** … The thing is to go to your aunties. They do the cooking the whole year."
> — Larson Gasper **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "Religious leaders are responsible in decision making processes." — followed by a concrete case in which religious leaders overruled a tribal council on a dispute with a tourist. **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**Hopi governance is about responsibility to others. Leadership is at bottom. Hopi say you assume the burden of the people.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — extraordinary line about inverted hierarchy]**

> "There's a lot of preparation going into the decision making process."
> — Dale Phillips **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 4.2 The "why" of the arc — Tony's own diagnosis

> "Over the past decade there have been numerous efforts by the conservation community to intercede in conservation issues on tribal lands. **Almost all of these efforts have failed for one universal reason – there has been a poor track record of engagement in Native American processes**, including but not limited to listening to and respecting the perspective and wisdom upon which all tribal cultural and ecological meaning is based.
> **Efforts to apply scientific and conservation technology and techniques are meaningless until there is first a willingness to listen, a respect for traditional perspectives, and a building of trust.** Only then can the integration of science, technology and cultural meaning begin to find common ground, purpose and application."
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE — the thesis paragraph of the whole archive]**

> "**The process will not be conventional, but instead will be built around a series of recurrent, culturally appropriate interactions to build a forum for trust, listening, and planning.**"

## 6. THE THEMES THEY WORKED

### 6.1 How communities historically cared for and sustained the land

The framing question, verbatim, used to open the very first gathering:

> "**How did our Tribal communities historically care for the land and what did the family and community units do to ensure it's sustainability?**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE — reusable as an opening question in any convening]**

> "**What are other Tribal Nations successfully doing to protect their landscapes across our Mother Earth and what can we learn from them that would improve efforts to protect our lands?**"
> — same **[PUBLISHABLE]**

The answer the group arrived at, in Tony's summary:

> "Almost all tribes have a similarity that ensured sustainability - **they placed intimacy with the land as vital to survival and regarded the land as a living being. All tribes had to know precisely which plants and animals were available at what time during the year.** They had very intimate knowledge of their land, seasons, and community. **This knowledge was important for sustainable harvesting of these resources, for survival of the future generations of people, and all living animals and plants.**"
> — *The Future of the Colorado Plateau Intertribal Conversations 12-14-11* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

And from participants:

> "**Observation is very important, it teaches you how to be honest.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**Nature and in life look at the trees, the leaves turn up. Observe the ants and you will learn a lot by looking at the land.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**My grandpa would only say things once. You are shown something one time because your life depended on it… Life depends on the listening skills.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE — devastating line about attention]**

### 6.2 Making decisions with honor

Beyond §3, the honor framing itself:

> "**The meeting was conducted with high honor. Traditional rules of respect, honor, integrity were used.** Songs were sung. Drawing and art to depict a solution were created. Various expert folks would be called in, perhaps even from another Tribe. **Full understanding of the problem and solution was the goal.**"
> — *Gathering Background and History* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "Eventually, the gathering resulted in a solution. **The results were shared back in full detail with each representative's community members. There was honor in the entire process because it ensured the society, Tribe, and people's survival.**"
> — same **[PUBLISHABLE — the closing move: you go home and report back, in full]**

> "In those days, **to be called to a gathering to solve an important issue was an honor. A solution was crafted over a long time.**"
> — *2009 First Gathering Report* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

### 6.3 Reinvigorating traditional foods, language, and health

> "Health: support better health through **the revitalization of traditional farming, foods, and exercise, and bonding these activities with conservation teachings**."
> — the group's own priority statement **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**A renewed connection with gathering, farming and traditional food preparation could be a cornerstone for the re-invigoration of culture, spiritual well-being and physical heath.**"
> — *Intertribal Opportunity Fund Brochure* **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**The old way of keeping and teaching the seeds is by using it.**"
> — *2011 Fourth Gathering Minutes* **[PUBLISHABLE — perfect compressed line about living knowledge]**

Health was defined by the participants in four dimensions — **spiritual, mental, physical, communication** — with "communication" including "expression of emotion, venting, release," "listening," and "post traumatic stress." **[PUBLISHABLE]** The self-definition they landed on: **"Positive affirmations of living — 'I am in balance.'"** **[PUBLISHABLE]**

> "**kids are coming back in droves to dance. But they do not know why they are dancing.. they are not growing corn anymore… They are not speaking the language. If they don't know the words, they don't know the context, the metaphors, that reveal these very deep and significant relationships with place… We have to keep the living context of languages alive… not just the dictionaries and the words.**"
> — quoted from Melissa Nelson, *Original Instructions*, in `The Future of the Colorado Plateau Intertribal Conversations 12-14-11` **[PUBLISHABLE with attribution to Nelson's book]**

### 7.6 The tension nobody resolved

## 10. SUGGESTED USE — how to turn this into the essay

**The through-line I'd propose:** *Modern meetings optimise for the record. These meetings optimised for transmission.* Every design difference falls out of that one inversion — the long introductions, the paid preparation, the hired artist, the song at the transitions, the requirement that grantees report back in person to the same circle, and the rule that a solution isn't finished until it can be sung, told, or lived by people who weren't in the room.

**Suggested structure:**

1. **Cold open** with the elder's line: *"I have seen a lot of these meetings. After I left nothing happened."* Then: here is what someone built to answer that.
2. **The inversion** (transmission over record) — §2.1.
3. **Before the room:** selection by conduct, preparation of the person, the invitation letter, the honorarium. — §2.2. Land the "in a good place spiritually, physically, emotionally" line.
4. **The room:** the rotating host, the separate facilitators, prayer, the long introductions, cultural sharing as a scheduled item, food as content, half a day outdoors. — §2.3–2.9.
5. **How they actually decided:** method before content; surface → condense → rank; the single narrow use of voting; self-selection into work; subcommittees between; research read *before*. And crucially: what they did when they disagreed. — §3.
6. **Honest section:** cost, pace conflict, unpaid checks, missing fridge in the elder's room, funder timelines, the fiscal sponsor that closed. — §7.
7. **What it produced:** $200k moved by an elder-run committee into eleven projects; springs restored; runs seeded; elders recorded; legal protection for graves. — §5.
8. **Close** on *"Walk softly. We will cross paths again"* — and the argument that a meeting is not an event, it is the beginning of an obligation.

**Global-travel rewrite guide** (apply throughout):
- "Tribe / tribal" → "nation," "community," or "people," depending on register.
- "Chapter" → "local community government." **[PRIVATE constraint]**
- Any clause identifying the nation whose government Tony ran → "a national government." **[PRIVATE constraint]**
- Named nations: fine to say *ten nations of the Colorado Plateau* and to name Coastal First Nations of British Columbia as an invited model; avoid a roll-call that makes it read regional.
- Keep the specific: a $300 honorarium, three paper slips, a 6:00am prayer walk, a fridge in the elder's room. Specificity is what makes it credible; regionality is what makes it narrow. They are not the same thing.

**Do not publish without further verification:** the "80+ participants" figure; the South America / Peru expansion (currently résumé-only); "New Mexico Pueblos" as a plural (only Acoma is documented). See §4.1.
