Every extract is tagged:

- **PUBLISHABLE** — safe for a public essay once the nation is unnamed. Suggested universalized phrasing given where needed.
- **PRIVATE CONTEXT** — background for the writer only. Do not publish. Includes all dollar figures, client identities, named counterparties, and live deals.

## 2. THEORY OF ECONOMIC LEAKAGE

This is the strongest, most consistently documented idea in the whole archive. He has been making the same argument for twenty-five years in four different registers: slide deck, funder narrative, strategy essay, and lyric essay.

### 2.1 The diagnosis

**S1 — PRIVATE CONTEXT (the numbers), PUBLISHABLE (the mechanism):**

> "According to research conducted by C.B. Richard Ellis, $1.2 billion is earned annually on the Navajo Nation, only 28.62% of this money earned was spent inside the reservation. That means for every dollar earned, about 71% or $870 million leaves the reservation."

**S2 — PRIVATE (numbers), PUBLISHABLE (the shape):**

> "Before COVID-19, there was already a leakage of revenue of over 70% to off-rez border towns. Now that we have a serious lockdown on the reservation because of the coronavirus, we would estimate the leakage to be over 80%."

**S6 — PRIVATE (numbers):**

> "the leakage of dollars is 73% and tourism is estimated at over 90% leakage"

> "We are getting organized to help the Navajo Nation think through major shopping center expansion strategy to capture at least 50% of the leakage"

**Reading of it:** the leakage figure sits around **70–73% of all earned income in normal conditions, rising past 80–90% under stress**, and **tourism leaks worse than retail** — over 90%. That last point is the sharpest and least obvious claim in the archive: *the sector everyone treats as the salvation of a post-extraction economy is the sector that leaks the most.*

**PUBLISHABLE VERSION (no nation, no figures):**
> "In one nation I advised, researchers found that for every dollar earned inside its borders, roughly seventy cents left immediately — spent in the border towns ringing it. Under stress that number climbed past eighty. And tourism, the sector everyone points to as the replacement for extraction, leaked worse than anything else."

If a number is wanted at all, use the ratio and not the dollars: **"about seventy cents of every dollar"** is publishable; **"$1.2 billion" and "$870 million" are not** — they identify the nation instantly via the CBRE study.

### 2.2 The mechanism — why it leaks

From S1, the cause is structural, not behavioral. Leakage happens because the retail and grocery infrastructure inside the community is **owned from outside**, so every transaction is a wire transfer out:

> "Having mainly off-reservation grocery store corporations on Navajo land creates several challenges. First is the dependence on few companies offers food insecurity... The other challenge is that off-reservation grocers are hard to creatively engage and support local sources of produce farm products and meats."

> "The 13 shopping centers are not able to retain much of the potential spend."

**PUBLISHABLE:** the phrase to lift is **"not able to retain much of the potential spend."** That is a clean, unattributable formulation of the whole concept.

### 2.3 The doctrine of capture — plug the leaks first

This is his actual method, and it is deliberately unglamorous. **S1:**

> "Such an approach would be aligned with one of the most important steps of a logical economic renewal process which is to first plug the leaks and improve what we already have. This approach is a smart, objective process to economic development for communities."

He puts the **minimum-risk move first** — add a grocery component to businesses that already exist and already want to grow — before any new construction. **PUBLISHABLE, high value.** This is the counterintuitive practitioner move: not "attract a big employer," but "stop the bleeding in what is already standing."

**S3 (slide, verbatim):**
> "Have to use innovative strategies to grow – capture leakage"

### 2.4 Grow the pie, don't split it

**S1 — PUBLISHABLE, strong:**

> "The opportunity shouldn't be viewed as a small pie. That is small thinking. The real opportunity here is to increase the pie by joining forces to build more and thus attract more."

> "An important consideration is that the communities should partner and plan complimentary developments on both sides. It is possible to increase the pie as all the developments contemplated here would attract more customer and visitors."

Context (PRIVATE — identifies the site): this is about two neighboring communities of different nations at a highway junction, each afraid the other will take its customers. His answer is that adjacency is an asset, not a threat.

**PUBLISHABLE:** "Two neighboring communities spent years each afraid the other would take its customers. Neither had noticed that both were losing their customers to a town seventy miles away."

### 2.5 Leakage of expertise — the extension of the idea

The most sophisticated version of the theory. He applies leakage not to money but to **advisory capacity**. **S15 — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "directly increasing tribal communities' capacity to negotiate and retain economic value from transition investments rather than exporting it"

> "urgent demand for locally-trained practitioners who can negotiate, manage, and retain economic value from those investments rather than exporting it to outside consultants"

**Suggested use:** this is the essay's turn. Money leaks, but so does *the ability to keep money from leaking.* When a community must hire an outside consultant to negotiate its own deal, the leak has moved upstream into the negotiation itself.

### 2.6 The lyric statement of the same idea

**S10 — already public, use verbatim:**

> "Keep the wealth home. Keep the wisdom home. Turn extraction into ownership, and ownership into care."

> "Stop mining human beings for labor, and start cultivating them for their genius."

**S11 — already public:**

> "The extractive economy asks a narrow question: *what can I take, and how fast?* The economy of the sheep camp asks a deeper one — the only one that finally matters: *will this make the great-grandchildren whole, or hollow them out?*"

## 3. FOUR CAPITALS

### 3.1 The canonical definition

**IMPORTANT CORRECTION TO THE RESEARCH BRIEF:** the task specified the fourth capital as *natural*. **He never writes "natural."** In all three places he defines it, the fourth capital is **ecological**. Use his word.

**S9 (SSIR) — PUBLISHABLE, the best single statement:**

> "Ancestral Economics measures success across four capitals: financial, relational, cultural, and ecological. A business that makes money but poisons relationships, erodes culture, and degrades land is not successful. It's extractive. The four capitals don't compete. They depend on each other. That's the whole point."

**S13 (Council Fire, Appendix I) — GATED (book text). Do not reproduce verbatim in a free essay; paraphrase or quote only the last sentence:**

> "**The Four Capitals.** A thing is wealthy only if it grows four kinds of capital at once — **financial** (the money), **relational** (trust and kinship), **cultural** (language, songs, ceremonies, seeds), and **ecological** (water, soil, the living land). A venture that grows the first by shrinking the other three is not a success. **It is a thief.**"

"**It is a thief**" is the sharpest four words in the entire archive on this subject. Worth building a paragraph around.

**S14 (Sovereignty Blueprint) — PRIVATE document, but confirms the naming:**

> "ANCESTRAL ECONOMICS FRAMEWORK. 3 Protocols: Guardian Network (governance) · Ceremonial Sanctity (tech) · Generative Reciprocity (economics). 4 Capitals: financial, relational, cultural, ecological."

### 3.2 The three protocols the four capitals sit inside

**S9 — PUBLISHABLE.** Ancestral Economics is a three-protocol framework; the four capitals are the measurement layer under protocol 3.

> "Over three decades working with Tribal nations, I've distilled what I learned into a framework I call Ancestral Economics. It rests on three protocols."

1. **Governance — The Guardian Network.** "In the standard model, corporations decide. Communities are consulted—if at all—after the decisions are made." Replaced by "a distributed Guardian Network: local elders and youth holding a sacred veto." The test question: *"Does this choice honor our relationships—with each other, with the land, with the seven generations yet to come?"* If no, "their word is final."
2. **Technology — Ceremonial Sanctity.** "The AI illuminates. It does not decide."
3. **Economics — Generative Reciprocity.** The best passage in the archive:

> "When Masani sent a lamb to a neighbor, she didn't invoice them. She knew the value would return—maybe in wool, maybe in help during lambing season, maybe in a story told about her for generations. **Value circulated. It didn't leave.** Some years she would not let the sheep have lambs—not because she was cruel, but because the land couldn't support them. Modern economics calls that degrowth. We called it survival. **Rest is productive.** Her goal was never the biggest herd. It was to still have sheep when the drought ended."

> "**A system that survives is wealthier than one that peaked and crashed.**"

**Note:** "Value circulated. It didn't leave" is the *leakage theory and the four capitals fused into one sentence.* This is the structural hinge of the essay — it shows that his hard-nosed retail leakage work and his grandmother's sheep camp are the same argument at two scales.

**PUBLISHABLE with light edit:** "Masani" is his great-grandmother and the reference is personal, not tribal-identifying — it is safe. The word "hogan" in the surrounding SSIR text is identifying; if that passage is used, substitute "a round house built for ceremony" (his own substitution in S11).

### 3.3 Four capitals applied — the traditional wealth audit

**S6 — PUBLISHABLE with universalizing, genuinely original:**

> "From a western lens, local wealth, intergenerational wealth are terms that have rarely applied. The challenges start with the inability to gain value of land and home, the inability to sell assets that are tied to Federal Trust land. From a traditional perspective, tribal peoples consider wealth as knowledge of songs, teachings, ceremony, language, sheep, farms, ability to build their own homes, and so on."

This is the four capitals arrived at *empirically*, from the ground, years before he named them. Worth flagging in the essay: the framework is a description of what he found, not a theory he imposed.

**S11 — already public, the fullest expression:**

> "the wealthiest people I know are the ones who still raise their own herds off the land, carefully, and process them with immense respect."

> "None of that shows up on a balance sheet. All of it is wealth."

> "Land held in common, language kept in the mouth, ceremony kept in the year, obligation kept between neighbors — these are assets that compound across centuries and cannot be foreclosed on."

That last line — **"assets that compound across centuries and cannot be foreclosed on"** — is the four capitals argued in the language of finance. Excellent bridge sentence for a business audience.

## 4. DEAL AND FINANCE STRUCTURES HE HAS ACTUALLY USED

This is the section that separates him from every other writer on Indigenous economics. He is not describing structures he read about. He wrote the enabling law for them.

### 4.1 The core list — from his own Economic Summit slide

**S3, verbatim slide text — PUBLISHABLE with terminology universalized:**

> **Strategic Partnerships through LGAs**
> Chapters can be a partner in development
> • What's the role of a chapter?
> • What if a chapter partnered with NNSC?
> • Public-Private Partnership
>
> **Creation of special development areas**
> • Bond finance – Tax Increment Financing
> • Equity positions - Unique financing not WR dependent
> • Local Owned Development Entity
> • New ordinances – i.e. housing, architectural standards, green belts, mixed used
>
> **Chapters to manage Business leases**
> **Collect sales taxes**
> • Incentive to chapters
> • Initial steps - Land use plan needed

Note "**not WR dependent**" — Window Rock, i.e. the central capital. **PUBLISHABLE as "not dependent on the central capital."** The whole point of the equity position is that it lets a local community finance a project *without* going to the national government for an appropriation.

### 4.2 The authorities the law created

**S3 / S4, verbatim — PUBLISHABLE with substitutions:**

> "Tools of LGA – chapter in charge of destiny – Innovative business site leasing, sales taxes, land-use planning, zoning ordinances, create revenue, bond finance, delegation of central government responsibility to the chapter(s)"

> "experienced chapter manager, effective governance (culturally compatible), have special committee, legal counsel, accounting, create sub units"

> "Intent – form partnerships to drive Larger development"

**S4 adds the inter-community layer:**

> "Partner among chapters – take over central gov functions"
> "Joint Administration for mgmt., accounting, econ development"
> "Joint Agreements – develop projects together – share costs and benefits"
> "Special Tax District Financing"

**PUBLISHABLE framing:** "I co-authored a law that gave local communities the right to zone, to lease, to levy their own sales tax, to issue bonds, to take over functions from the capital, and to enter joint agreements with each other so that two neighboring communities could develop one project and split the cost and the benefit."

### 4.3 Tax increment financing in his own plain words

The single most useful sentence for a business-literate reader, because he explains TIF without the acronym. **S6 — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "tools such as the user fee districts where future lease revenue and future tax revenue is pledged to to pay back financing"

And **S6** again:

> "We will help provide guidance on creative financing mechanisms such as special tax and fee districts, so they can finance larger developments."

**Suggested use:** "The mechanism is not exotic. A community draws a line around a district, pledges the lease and tax revenue that district will generate in the future, and borrows against it today. Cities have done this for a century. The difference is who holds the pledge."

### 4.4 The equity-position doctrine — the philosophical core

**S2 — PUBLISHABLE, one of the best passages in the archive:**

> "The way we did business 40 years ago is different from how we are capable of doing business now. Forty years ago, we were passive land owners. That means **we didn't want to own the business and we didn't want part of the deal.** We also did not have experienced business people. Nowadays, we have MBAs from Harvard and Stanford, and we have experienced business people. Now, we can own ventures big or small."

And the hard-headed reason why ownership matters more than royalties:

> "it is important for the tribes to create incentives and support tribal ventures and ownership **because that's where the money is. The lease revenue and tax revenue from renewable energy is minimal compared to the resource extraction industry.**"

**This is the most important single insight in the just-transition material** and it is contrarian: swapping coal leases for solar leases is a *massive revenue downgrade*. Renewables only work as a replacement economy **if the community takes an ownership position rather than a landlord position.** Quantified in **S7** (numbers PRIVATE):

> "one large 750 megawatt solar project, would create 200 to 400 jobs for about 1.5 years and then 25 long term jobs. The revenue at the most, from leases and taxes, amount to several million dollars per year at most."

**PUBLISHABLE without figures:** "A utility-scale solar project employs hundreds of people for about eighteen months and a couple of dozen forever. As a landlord, you collect a fraction of what the coal lease paid. Renewables replace coal only if you own them."

### 4.5 The gap he names — no joint ventures, no equity

**S6 — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "the banks don't finance small businesses. There are really no good relations between impact investors and local entrepreneurs. The Navajo enterprises, the managers, and the Navajo Nation are used to getting grants to develop. They have never used onsite financing to do major development. **There's very little partnership creation, meaning there's few large joint ventures where the Nation is a big equity partner** in large scale retail or any transition type venture."

**S6** on the grant-dependency trap — publishable and quotable:

> "the Nation usually applies for federal funding to complete these projects. However, over the years, federal funding has become less and less... **it's a really slow process in that it takes 20 years to actually develop a shopping center using that strategy.**"

**Twenty years to build a shopping center by grant.** That is the number that justifies the entire capital-stack argument, and it is publishable because it identifies nothing.

### 4.6 The community capital stack

**S8 — PUBLISHABLE almost verbatim; already written for a global audience:**

> "**The Investment Framework.** Translating these four principles into action requires patient, community-aligned, long-horizon capital.
> Core components:
> • **Joint venture equity** — allowing community contractors to partner with experienced larger firms on major contracts
> • **Equipment financing** — enabling small businesses to acquire the tools needed to compete for large contracts
> • **Bonding and insurance support** — removing the single largest barrier community contractors face
> • **Operating capital** — bridging the gap between contract award and payment
> • **Community governance** — the fund governed by people who understand both the financial mechanics and the human relationships that make community economic development work"

**"Bonding and insurance support — removing the single largest barrier community contractors face"** is the detail that proves he has actually done this work. Nobody theorizing about Indigenous economics from a desk names surety bonding as the binding constraint.

### 4.7 The mixed public/private/co-op structure

**S1 — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "a set of incentives created, a vision for the development with community input, and **creative business structure such as building a public/private model that allow partnership between a local coop, a developer, investment by nearby villages and communities.**"

> "**Investment options and equity should be made available to nearby communities and villages to ensure the most investment of dollars from the area first** and fairness to the region of benefits. Business options range from investment into the overall development or parts such as the restaurants, theatre, motel, and so forth."

This is a **local-first capital raise** — offer the equity to the neighbors before you offer it to outsiders. Clean, publishable, and it is the leakage doctrine applied to the cap table rather than the cash register.

**S1** closing the loop on why it compounds:

> "Once the wheel starts turning, the confidence is gained, the investments and streamlined leases are figured out, a game changer for tribes can be created."

### 4.8 Nonprofit-as-aggregator — the economies-of-scale fix

**S1 — PUBLISHABLE.** A structure he designed to give independent local merchants the purchasing power of a chain:

> "In comparison to large corporations such as Bashas, they have major food distribution networks with the ability to buy in bulk and secure lower cost agreements with producers. An individual Navajo entrepreneur does not have access to such a low-cost mechanism of buying grocery items to resell. **The Navajo Food Network can bring the same economies of scale.** As part of its start up expenses, grant dollars could be the means for providing expansive offerings. Then it can be bought in bulk for multiple locations."

> "This group could be launched as a non-profit entity under or with support and/or seed financing from the Navajo Shopping Centers, Navajo Nation, non profits, and foundations."

**PUBLISHABLE as:** "a nonprofit buying cooperative, seeded with grant capital, that gives twenty independent shopkeepers the wholesale pricing of a chain." Rename it — do not use "Navajo Food Network."

### 4.9 Community development corporation

**S6 — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "There are best practice success examples, such as the Shonto Chapter which has created a community development corporation... I helped them get secure grant funding and did their strategic planning for 5 years."

Also the village-owned nonprofit development company model: *"It is a Village owned nonprofit development company."* (S6)

**Universalize:** "one community formed its own development corporation and became the template everyone else studied."

## 5. SUSTAINABLE TOURISM THAT STRENGTHENS RATHER THAN COMMODIFIES

### 5.1 The doctrine — relational vs. extractive tourism

**S8 — PUBLISHABLE nearly as-is. This is the cleanest statement of the idea he has:**

> "The Japanese concept of *ichigo ichie* — one time, one meeting, treasure every encounter — is the foundation of an entirely different kind of tourism. **Not the extractive model where visitors consume a landscape and leave. But the relational model where visitors come to learn, to listen, to be changed by what they encounter.**"

> "The tourism economy built on ancestral land is not a consolation prize for losing an extraction industry. It is a **generational wealth engine** — one that, unlike coal, does not deplete. **The canyon walls do not run out. The stories do not run out.** The land, restored and cared for, becomes more valuable with every generation, not less."

> "The Kumano Kodo pilgrimage trail in Japan... brings visitors from across the globe to walk ancient forest paths, stay in family-run guesthouses, and eat food grown by the families who have farmed these valleys for centuries. **The tourism does not extract from the community. It sustains it.**"

> "Bhutan measures its economy not in Gross Domestic Product but in Gross National Happiness. It limits tourist numbers deliberately — charging a high daily fee that funds conservation and community development — and has built one of the most sought-after travel destinations on earth **precisely because it refused to be consumed.**"

**"Precisely because it refused to be consumed"** is the thesis of the section. Note the mechanism he is pointing at: **scarcity by design, priced, with the price ring-fenced for conservation and community.** That is a finance structure, not a sentiment — worth making explicit in the essay.

Also **S8**, on the Lithuanian Curonian Spit: **"The amber is not extracted and shipped away. It is experienced in place, by people who travel to be where it is."** That is the extraction/tourism distinction in one image, and it is his own.

### 5.2 Tourism leaks worst — the correction to the optimism

Cross-reference §2.1: **over 90% tourism leakage (S6, PRIVATE figure).** The essay should place these two facts next to each other deliberately, because together they are a real argument rather than a slogan:

> Tourism is the only post-extraction asset that appreciates — and it is also the sector that leaks the hardest. Both are true. Which means the tourism question is never "should we?" It is "who owns the guesthouse, who runs the tour, and where is the money at 6pm?"

### 5.3 Cultural difference as the economic engine

**S12 (already public) — PUBLISHABLE verbatim:**

> "Difference itself was the engine. Peoples depended on one another's distinct livelihoods and cultures — **a kind of cultural tourism woven straight into the economy.**"

> "The two were different in almost every way. One people's life was bound to farming; the other's to the health of their animals... And that very difference created a complement that pulled them closer... **It was a business connection and a social one at the same time, and neither could be pried loose from the other.**"

This reframes cultural tourism as **ancient and indigenous to the region**, not an import from the modern travel industry. Strong move for the essay: the relational tourism model is not a reform of mass tourism, it is the *older* form, and mass tourism is the deviation.

### 5.4 Practitioner detail — what he actually recommended

**S16 — substance PUBLISHABLE, all names and the hotel complaint PRIVATE:**

> "Creation of a Navajo Tourism Association that will enable networking, joint strategy, representation to the Navajo leadership to push legislation, projects, finance and outside leadership with a strong tourism focus."

> "Formulation of a Navajo specific hospitality institute... The intent would be to **improve customer service using best practices and Navajo values.**"

> "Companies still need help with the basics - accounting, human resources, training especially around hospitality, marketing."

> "Many of the tour operators are asking for more **respectful engagement** of the... parks and rec department... There are many concerns such as **reinvestment back into the facilities** such as paving the parking lots and building official bathroom facilities, work on roads and protecting the assets and scenery."

**PUBLISHABLE as:** "a sector association so the operators speak with one voice; a hospitality institute that teaches service through the culture's own values rather than a franchise manual; and — least romantic, most necessary — reinvestment of gate revenue back into roads, parking and bathrooms at the sites themselves."

**PRIVATE:** all individual and organizational names; the note that a host hotel's staff made racial comments; internal friction with a government department; payment problems with the fiscal agent.

**One publishable observation from S16**, from a state tourism director's testimonial — the detail is charming and safely generic: successful tour businesses in that nation were **"many founded by grandmothers."**

## 6. JUST TRANSITION — MOVING AN ECONOMY OFF FOSSIL DEPENDENCE

### 6.1 The reframe

**S8 — PUBLISHABLE, and this is the opening of the essay if you want one:**

> "In 2019, the Navajo Generating Station closed... With it went thousands of jobs, roughly $40 million in annual revenue... and an entire economic identity built around extraction.
> **The world called it a crisis. I called it a design opportunity.**"

> "The question is always the same: **when the extraction ends, what remains?** The answer, if you know where to look, is **everything.**"

**PUBLISHABLE VERSION:** drop the year, the plant name, and the dollar figure. "When the largest coal plant in the region went dark, it took thousands of jobs, a very large share of two nations' annual revenue, and an entire economic identity with it. ### 6.2 The four principles / four repairs

**S8 gives the essay version. S13 gives the compressed book version (GATED).** They are the same four moves, which is useful — it means the framework is stable across his work.

| # | S8 (essay, publishable) | S13 (book, GATED) |

**Principle One — PUBLISHABLE, the strongest single argument:**

> "In the Western economic model, environmental remediation is called 'liability.' A cost. Something to minimize. In the Diné world, it is called restoring Hózhó — returning the land to balance. **It is not a cost. It is an obligation.** A sacred one."

> "The practical reality: cleanup and reclamation work generates hundreds of skilled jobs over years and decades. **This work belongs to community contractors and local workers — not outside firms. The first economic opportunity of transition is not building something new. It is honoring what was taken by restoring what remains.**"

> "Germany's Ruhr Valley — once the most heavily industrialized coal region in Europe — has spent thirty years transforming slag heaps into parks, contaminated rivers into clean waterways, and abandoned mines into cultural destinations... **The restoration did not cost the community. It built it.**"

*Framing note:* "Diné" and "Hózhó" appear here. In a piece that must not name the nation, replace with "in the language I was raised in, it is called returning the land to balance." He does exactly this substitution himself in S11 and S12, so it is consistent with his own practice.

**S2 corroborates from the practitioner side — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "When a power plant is closed down, there are many years of decommissioning. In our area, a lot of assets are left behind. Everything from airports, pipelines, train lines, buildings, substations, and transmission lines are left behind."

> "on Navajo, there are many skilled welders, mechanics, engineers, and construction workers. We propose that there be a retraining of employees. A lot of them can work in decommissioning and reclamation... These companies could be re-expanded and offered business financing assistance to actually become a part of that decommissioning and reclamation work."

**Principle Two — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "the transmission infrastructure built to carry coal power to distant cities now offers a rare opportunity: the same lines can carry solar power, generated on tribal land, owned by tribal nations, to the same markets. **What changes is who owns the power — and who receives the wealth it generates.**"

> "**Corn growing under solar panels on Indigenous land is not a compromise.** It is Hózhó made physical."

Agrivoltaics evidence he cites: Japan post-Fukushima, "over 2,000 agrivoltaic installations" by 2023; India solar irrigation; Denmark community wind cooperatives; Aboriginal solar microgrids. **All publishable — public, non-client facts.**

**Principle Three — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "Every transition plan focuses on the big project. But **community wealth is built in the middle** — in the contractor who can bid on reclamation work, the baker who feeds the construction crew, the small hotel that houses the engineers, the accountant who manages the books."

> Mondragon: "Rather than attracting a single large employer, the community built an ecosystem of worker-owned cooperatives, each one supporting the others, all of them owned by the people doing the work."

> "The framework is the same everywhere: **identify what the community already has, organize it, provide the capital and support that small enterprises need to compete, and let the ecosystem grow from the inside out.**"

**S2 supplies the practitioner's version of "build the middle" — PUBLISHABLE and vivid:**

> "Let's say you have 600 workers for two years. That is a big impact. You're going to need hotels, camping areas, places to eat, and vendors. Workers are going to come in for five days and will need a place to sleep, to rest, and to eat. Then again, planning for how we maximize and take advantage of that opportunity is really critical."

That is leakage capture applied to a construction boom: *the construction spend is a temporary river running through your community; the question is whether you built anything to catch it.*

**S2 also names the community bank — PUBLISHABLE:**

> "it is important that we have a partnership between a wise mature nonprofit that can secure grants to help farmers stay whole and to help farmers get into business. **One thought is that we create a community bank and maybe even consider trading.**"

### 6.3 The dependency diagnosis

**S2 — PUBLISHABLE with substitutions:**

> "Tribes have become dependent on coal mining and power plants over the last 60 years. A lot of tribal governments were set up to approve mineral exploration and leases. **The federal government has always promoted resources extraction as a revenue and job source for tribes.** There have been policies that promoted development of that and quicker leases which has resulted in a situation today where the tribes are not finding a replacement revenue."

Powerful and publishable: **the governments themselves were designed as lease-approval machines.** The institution was shaped by the extraction economy, which is why it cannot easily produce anything else.

### 6.4 The human note

**S2 — PUBLISHABLE, unusually warm and worth keeping:**

> "What you learn from visiting these people is that we are very similar. It doesn't matter what color we are, we have a history. We are tied to the land. We want our families to grow up the same way we did. It is an honorable life. **People in the coal places are really honorable people. But, coal is not economical anymore and we have to find a way to replace it.**"

> "These communities are not going to move anywhere, everybody is going to stay there."

> "there are generational assets such as livestock, land, cornfields, hogans, stories, and connections to the land that pass from generation to generation. So, we have to protect that. **The people are not going to leave their homeland.**"

This is the answer to the standard economist's response ("people should move to where the jobs are"). Publishable; substitute "hogans" → "the old houses."

### 7.2 Retail and the food desert

**S1 — the three-strategy sequence. PUBLISHABLE and genuinely useful:**

> "we... have identified three strategic options for an expedited process to achieve grocery stores including: **(1) expanding within existing businesses** to offer fresh, healthy foods including locally grown produce, **(2) establishing a dynamic collaboration** between the Navajo Nation government, Navajo Shopping Centers, NGOs and entrepreneurs to open a new type of grocery store in a **ready and vacant space**, **(3) implementing innovative strategies such as a mobile grocery store** which could ideally reach our most rural communities."

> "The fourth strategy would be to go to regular grocery stores like Albertson's or Bashas. The challenge is that it takes many years to get the land approvals, secure tenants, and funding to build a shopping center."

**PUBLISHABLE as:** "a place where families drove two hours each way for groceries — and spent the money at the far end of the drive."

**On food as culture, not commodity (S1) — PUBLISHABLE, lovely:**

> "From a traditional Dine (Navajo) perspective, 'groceries' would be quite a unique list of items likely to be sourced only on Navajo or by Navajo people. The list might look something like this: 'blood sausage, mutton jerky, dried blue corn, juniper ash, and Navajo cushaw squash'. **To Dine, this food is medicine, not groceries.**"

Universalize the list slightly ("dried blue corn, juniper ash, mutton jerky, a squash that grows nowhere else") and the final sentence survives intact: **"This food is medicine, not groceries."**
