Health & Longevity

The Ancestral Code for Living to 100

Across the world, the peoples who reach a hundred are not lucky — they are living a code their ancestors wrote into food, movement, purpose, and song. We did not lose it. We stopped reading it.

"Walk in beauty. Live long. Die wise."
— a blessing-way teaching of my father's people
"Ikigai." A reason for being — where what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what sustains you all meet.
— Okinawan life philosophy, a Blue Zone principle
"Health is not only the condition of the body. It is the connection to the earth, to family, to song."
— folk wisdom of my mother's people

There is one code beneath these three sayings. It was written on four continents by peoples who never met. And most of us have stopped reading it.

Our ancestors knew how to live to 100. We forgot.

In the not-too-distant past, it was ordinary — not miraculous — for the elders of many land-based peoples to live past a hundred. Centenarians were not exceptions. They were the aspiration a whole life was built toward, encoded into ceremony, into blessing songs, into the very shape a life was meant to take. Among my father's people, the winter games count out a hundred and two stems as a quiet reminder: a hundred and two years is not a miracle. It is the destination.

My grandfather herded sheep every day of his life. He walked more than five miles daily, grew and harvested his own food, built and maintained his home, stayed woven into ceremony and community, and died at 103 in what I can only call ultimate happiness. In his nineties, at a ceremony held in his honor, he had the body of a much younger man. He was not an exception. He was the rule — before the rule was broken.

This is an exploration of why our ancestors lived so long — and why people in certain communities around the world still do. It is also a call. The wisdom that produced centenarians on the high desert is the same wisdom that produces them in Okinawa, in Sardinia, in the Baltic countryside. The code is global. It has always been global. We simply stopped reading it.

The Blue Zones: where the ancient code survived

In his landmark research, Dan Buettner identified five "Blue Zones" — the places on earth with the highest concentrations of centenarians: Okinawa, Japan; Sardinia, Italy; the Nicoya Peninsula, Costa Rica; Ikaria, Greece; and Loma Linda, California. He did not find five different secrets to a long life. He found one secret, wearing five different cultural faces.

When I first read his findings, I felt a deep recognition. Every principle he named — natural movement, a plant-rich diet, purpose, community and clan, spiritual practice, the honoring of elders — was something my own grandparents had been taught as children. Not as health advice. As the way life simply works.

The Blue Zones are not anomalies. They are survivals — the few places where industrial modernity did not fully sever the ancestral wellness code. They are windows into what was once universal.

The global ancestral wellness code: eight principles

1. Move naturally, every day

Run at dawn. Tend animals. Carry water. Gather wood. The body was made to move through the world, not to sit and watch it. Okinawan centenarians do not go to gyms; they garden, walk to their neighbors, and rise from the floor a dozen times a day. Sardinian shepherds walk more than five miles daily through mountains — not as exercise, but as life. Baltic farm life, which survived in the countryside well into the twentieth century, was built around the same constant motion: cutting hay, gathering forest foods, walking between villages, the body itself the primary tool.

The research is unambiguous. People who move naturally all day — not through structured workouts but through a life that requires movement — live longer and healthier than sedentary people, even sedentary people who exercise. The ancestral body was never designed for the chair.

2. Eat what your land produces

Corn, squash, and beans — the Three Sisters — formed the foundation of many ancestral tables, understood always in relationship to the land's seasons. The traditional Okinawan diet — sweet potato, tofu, bitter melon, seaweed, a little fish — is consistently ranked among the most health-giving on earth, paired with hara hachi bu, the discipline of eating until only eighty percent full. On the Greek island of Ikaria, where dementia runs at roughly a fifth of the U.S. rate, researchers point to wild greens, olive oil, legumes, herbs, and food grown on the island and shared in season.

Then there is Loma Linda — the only Blue Zone in a wealthy Western country. When I visited, I was struck by how independent it had stayed from the corporate food machine: no major chains, no fast-food corridors, just local stores stocked with plant-based food, locally sourced and community-supported. The Seventh-day Adventist community had simply refused to let industrial food replace ancestral food. On average, its members live ten or more years longer than their neighbors.

The pattern is global: communities that eat food grown close to home, in season, barely processed, and shared with others live dramatically longer than those fed by industry. This is not a diet tip. It is an observation about the bond between food sovereignty and health sovereignty — the difference between an economy that extracts from a people and one that is owned by them, keeping wealth and wisdom home.

3. Know your purpose — and live it

Ikigai — a reason for being, where what you love, what you do well, what the world needs, and what sustains you all meet — is among the most powerful longevity principles Blue Zone research has found. Okinawans with a clear ikigai live longer, heal faster, and hold onto their minds later in life.

Elsewhere the same truth wears different words. To "walk in beauty" is not a passive state; it is the active practice of living in right relationship with everything around you, of knowing your role in the web of life and fulfilling it on purpose, every day. The Baltic idea of farming as a sacred vocation gave whole communities a sense of stewardship tied to the land and to the generations before and after them. The farmer was not a laborer. They were a steward. The distinction is everything.

People with a clear sense of purpose carry a fifteen percent lower risk of death from all causes. Purpose is not a luxury. It is a biological necessity, encoded in our ancestral design.

4. Belong to something larger than yourself

The clan — the web of relational obligations that tells you who you are, who your people are, and what you owe to whom — is not merely social organization. It is a health system. When you belong to a clan, you are never alone; there are always people whose wellbeing is bound to yours, guaranteed by structure rather than by choice. Okinawa's version is the moai, a small circle of friends committed to one another for life, sharing money in hard times, grief in loss, and company always — a factor researchers name again and again in Okinawan longevity. In Sardinia, multi-generational homes and elders kept at the center of family life measurably benefit every generation: grandchildren of involved grandparents develop better; grandparents with involved grandchildren live longer. In the Baltic countryside, family extended outward to godparents and neighbors, weaving mutual-aid networks so dense that the isolation of modern urban life — now shown to harm health as much as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day — was structurally impossible.

Modern research confirms the ancestral insight: social connection is the single strongest predictor of a long life. Stronger than diet. Stronger than exercise. Stronger than any medical intervention. The clan is not a quaint tradition. It is health infrastructure. You are not alone; the circle holds.

5. Honor the elders. Learn from them.

In every Blue Zone, elders are woven into daily life. They are not retired from the community — they are promoted within it. Their knowledge, their stories, their place at the center of the family are not sentiment; they are the mechanism by which hard-won wisdom crosses from one generation to the next. Balance and right relationship cannot be learned from a book. They must be transmitted by someone who has lived them. The elder is not a museum piece. They are a living library.

Japanese research on ikigai found that elders who felt needed — who had a role, knowledge to give, people who depended on them — lived significantly longer than elders who felt purposeless. A community that honors its elders does not only preserve wisdom. It extends lives.

6. Pray. Sing. Keep ceremony.

Every Blue Zone culture holds a robust spiritual practice. Okinawans pray at household shrines daily. Adventists build their whole week around the Sabbath. Sardinians keep dozens of festivals a year; Ikarians their Orthodox rhythms; the people of Nicoya their faith communities that carry both meaning and connection.

In many traditions a ceremony is not religion in the Western sense — it is maintenance, a regular recalibration of the self in relationship to the whole. Like changing the oil in an engine, it keeps the human system running. The old Baltic path centered on sacred fires, sacred groves, and the singing of ancestral songs as a form of prayer — the dainos that a grandmother sings by lamplight in the forest, in the world of my book By Lamplight. These songs are not entertainment. They are a community's immune system: the way shared meaning, shared memory, and shared resilience pass from one generation to the next.

Across the Blue Zones, people with a regular spiritual practice — whatever the religion — live four to fourteen years longer than those without one. The mechanism appears to be stress reduction, connection, and purpose braided together. The ceremony is not separate from the health practice. The ceremony is the health practice.

7. Laugh. Often. Without apology.

In more than one tradition, a baby's very first laugh is cause for a feast — the whole clan gathers and everyone is fed. Not merely because it is custom, but because people understood, long before science confirmed it, that laughter is medicine. Okinawan centenarians are famously playful, teasing and light. Sardinians treat the ability to laugh at oneself as a mark of character, not weakness.

The research agrees: laughter lowers cortisol and adrenaline, raises endorphins and oxytocin, strengthens immunity, and helps the heart. A 2016 Norwegian study found that people with a strong sense of humor outlived those who laughed less — the effect most pronounced in cancer survival.

Your ancestors did not take themselves too seriously. Follow their example.

8. Grow what you eat. Eat what you grow.

Every Blue Zone culture grows food — not as a hobby or a trend, but as a fundamental practice that binds the body to the land, the season, and the community. Around the world, land-based peoples are now rebuilding that bond: regenerative cooperatives raising heritage lamb and grass-fed beef, traditional-foods cafés serving ancestral meals, farmers markets built around local growers, food systems that reconnect communities to the land-based diet that sustained centenarians for generations. In my father's homelands, cooperatives like Navajo Lamb and Navajo Beef are doing exactly this work — turning an extraction economy back into an ownership economy, keeping the wealth and the wisdom home.

The homestead garden survived Soviet collectivization in the Baltics precisely because families understood, at a cellular level, that their health depended on it. Even when the collective farms demanded all their labor, they kept their kitchen gardens. They knew.

Temple gardens in Japan, kitchen gardens in Okinawa, terraced vineyards in Sardinia — in every Blue Zone, the garden is not decoration. It is medicine.

The code is still here. We are the ones who must live it.

For a century, the ways that once ensured long, purposeful, healthy lives have been eroded — by displacement, by acculturation, by economic systems that prized labor over wisdom, production over relationship, efficiency over beauty. The result is written in the bodies of Indigenous, rural, and transitioning communities the world over: among the highest rates of diabetes, heart disease, depression, and early death anywhere.

But this is not destiny. It is a design flaw. And design flaws can be corrected.

The community at Loma Linda did not accept the health outcomes of the culture around it. It built an alternative — food systems, social structures, and spiritual practices all aligned with the values that support a long life. They did not let the difficulty stop them from making the vision real.

We can do the same — not by copying anyone, but by remembering what our own ancestors already knew. This is the through-line of everything I write, from Stone Breath to The Council Fire to The Far Messengers: that peoples and nations can transition, in a shifting world, from systems that hollow us out to systems that make us whole.

So put every choice through the oldest filter there is. Will this make the great-grandchildren whole, or hollow them out? Live the answer.

It is not too late. The fire is still lit. It is you.

Walk in beauty. Live long. Die wise. Let us celebrate growing past a hundred, together.
