The invitation

Every one of us is carrying something we have not put down.

You are competent. You are needed. The days move faster than you do, and somewhere underneath all of it, you have gone quiet.

Where I come from, when the world gets too loud, you go down. Into the canyon. Where the walls hold the heat of the day, where the water still runs, where there is nothing above you but a long strip of sky and nothing asked of you but to keep walking.

For three days at 1440 Multiversity, we make that descent together. Then, on the last morning, we climb back out into the harvest — which is what the end of summer has always been for.

You do not change your life by adding more to it. You change your frequency.

Who comes to the canyon

This weekend is for you.

The woman who gives everything

To work, to family, to everyone — and quietly wonders who refills her.

The one in a hard passage

A loss, a divorce, an empty nest, a career turning over — who needs a safe place to set it down.

The daughter finding her footing

Younger, at a crossroads, looking for ground to stand on.

The elder

With a life's worth of wisdom, ready to gather it and pass it on.

The seeker

Anyone who has been walking in the dark and wants to remember the way home.

You do not need to be fit, or a hiker, or experienced in any of this. Many arrive on their own and leave with a circle. Just eighteen guests, so no one gets lost. The oldest medicine is intergenerational — grandmother, mother, daughter, moving together.

Whose ground this is

We open by the creek, and we open by saying whose land we are standing on.

1440 Multiversity sits on the unceded homeland of the Amah Mutsun, who have cared for these mountains, these creeks and these redwoods since long before any of us arrived, and who are here still.

On Friday evening we begin outdoors, at the water, with a land acknowledgement offered to the Amah Mutsun. We say their name first. We ask the land's permission before we ask anything of ourselves. Everything that follows in the three days rests on that.

The practices

Old tools. Used properly. For three days straight.

The redwoods do half the work — standing beneath the Mother Tree, walking the creeks, eating food grown and blessed as medicine. Into that stillness we bring the tools, braided from three cultures: the Diné of the red-earth country, the Baltic song-keepers, and the Japanese discipline of stillness. Different tongues, one human operating system. Regulate first — then everything the ancestors left us becomes usable.

  • BreathworkThe fastest door into the body we have found. Twice daily.
  • MeditationGuided and silent, indoors and under the trees.
  • Qigong and tai chiSlow movement on the redwood deck, first thing.

Food is medicine

The Ancestral Kitchen.

Cooking is a lineage you can taste.

At the heart of the weekend is the Ancestral Kitchen, led by Missy & Henry Webster — a Navajo family from Gray Mountain, Arizona, who cook the way their grandmothers cooked.

They turn the harvest feast into a hands-on teaching: blue corn, juniper ash, frybread pulled hot. They will tell you what each thing is for while they hand it to you, which is how the old knowledge has always travelled — hand to hand, not page to page.

Around it, every meal is part of the healing: a specially designed, seasonal diet, every meal locally sourced, cooked and blessed as medicine, from Friday dinner through Sunday lunch.

It is built for late August — light, warm, and alkaline — and designed so that by Sunday your body is running at a different speed than it was on Friday.

Your guide

Tony Skrelūnas

Diné and Lithuanian. Raised on Black Mesa by his great-grandmother, Masan.

Masan did not teach with lectures. She taught with the day: what time you rose, what you ate, what you said to the fire, how long you stayed quiet. Most of what happens at The Canyon Within is hers.

Tony has spent a decade convening intergenerational gatherings — cooks, singers, runners, elders, facilitators — and now brings that work to 1440 Multiversity, where he leads retreats.

Before you come

The questions people ask.

Do I need to be fit, or a hiker?

No. The descent is inward. The walks are gentle, unhurried, and always optional.

Can I come on my own?

Yes — many do. With just eighteen guests, you will leave with a circle. It is a safe, warm container.

Can I come for just part of it?

Yes. Day passes are available for Friday evening, Saturday, and Sunday morning — join for the parts that call you, even if you cannot stay overnight.

Do I need experience with meditation or breathwork?

None. Everything is taught from the beginning. The first tool, Stone Breath, takes about a minute to learn.

Is this religious?

No. It is ancestral and human. Take what serves you and leave the rest.

What should I bring?

Comfortable clothes and layers, a water bottle, and an open heart. A journal and your stone are provided.
