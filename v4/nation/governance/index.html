Deep dive two · Nation-Building

Getting away from elections.

Campaign elections are one way to choose leaders. In a communal culture they are usually the worst one available. Here is what we built instead — and what it took to make the authority real rather than symbolic.

A Local Governance Act adopted by 45 communities Failed the first time. Passed on the second.
Start a conversation → See the first three steps

The before

One salaried coordinator, reporting upward. That was the whole local government.

Start with the failure state, because every conversation about governance reform that skips it produces a document nobody can use.

Before the reform, every local community in the system was structurally identical. One salaried coordinator, who reported to the central delegate. The community could pass resolutions. That was the extent of it.

No zoning. No local revenue. No taxing authority. No incentive to build anything, because anything built was approved, financed and controlled from the centre — and the people who approved it lived a long way from the people who would live next to it.

That is what centralization looks like from underneath. Not tyranny. Administration. A hundred communities holding regular meetings in order to produce documents requesting permission.

And here is the part that matters for anyone reading this from a very different place: the failure had nothing to do with the culture, the language, the remoteness or the poverty. It was a design. Power and authority had been placed entirely at the centre, and the local body had been given the form of a government with none of the substance of one. You can find the identical arrangement in post-industrial regions, in colonial-era municipal codes across three continents, and in the field offices of most large organizations.

Why elections misfire

A ballot is a mechanism, not a synonym for consent.

I am not against voting. I am against assuming a campaign election is the only way consent can be established, when the culture in front of you has three older mechanisms still visibly working.

  1. 01
    They reward self-promotion. In most communal cultures, declaring your own worth in public is a disqualifying act — it is the behaviour of someone who cannot be trusted with anything. So the election does not select the strongest leader. It selects the person least bound by that norm, which is a materially different group of people and usually a worse one.
  2. 02
    They split kin. A campaign obliges households to declare against each other in public. The election is over in November. The family is not. Where the same fifty households will be intermarrying and sharing water for the next century, a two-year cycle of forced public opposition is not a democratic exercise. It is a slow structural injury.
  3. 03
    They import a contest the culture never had. A winner with fifty-one percent inherits an organized opposition that did not exist until the campaign manufactured it. Then that opposition is available for capture by anyone with money, and now you have factions in a place that previously had families.
  4. 04
    They select for campaigning, not governing. These are two different jobs requiring two different temperaments. We have simply agreed to pretend that winning the first is evidence of competence at the second, and every level of government on earth pays for that pretence.
  5. 05
    Turnout gets read as legitimacy. Across a dispersed territory, turnout substantially measures the condition of the roads and who owns a working vehicle. A council elected on eighteen percent is then told, by everyone including itself, that eighty-two percent of its people did not care.

You do not put yourself forward. Others put you forward, and the honor is part of the pay.

On nomination as a selection mechanism

What we built instead

Four older mechanisms, given legal form.

None of these were invented. All of them were already operating in the community — in the ceremony, the family, the grazing dispute. The work was to give them a statute, a budget line and a filing cabinet.

  • Nomination and honor You do not put yourself forward. Others put you forward, and a substantial part of the compensation is the honor of having been named. It selects for standing already earned rather than for appetite, and it removes the campaign entirely — with it, the factions.
  • Representation by far-flung precinct Representation follows the true shape of the land and the people — kinship groupings, watersheds, the roads people actually travel — rather than a population-equal polygon produced by an algorithm. A community spread across two hundred miles of dirt road is several constituencies, whatever the map says.
  • Positions created from within the culture Instead of importing a committee chart, we asked which functions the community already performs — water, grazing, ceremony, the young, the burial, the dispute — and gave each of them a named position, a term and a line in the budget. The government then describes something that already exists.
  • A respectful meeting process, written into the law Not etiquette. Formal design: how the meeting opens, who speaks in what order, how a decision closes. It is in the act because a governing body that meets badly will govern badly no matter what authority it holds. The full argument →

And then certification, which is the part people skip

You do not hand real authority to a body that cannot yet hold it. That is not paternalism; it is the reason most devolution programmes collapse in their third year and get quietly reversed.

So communities became certified to govern. Certification meant meeting a defined standard, with genuine separation of powers, real checks and balances, and an annual audit once revenue passed a threshold. A community that had them held authority. A community that did not, worked toward them, with support.

About half of the communities achieved certification.

I want to say that number plainly rather than round it. The roadblocks were real and they were boring: staffing — small places cannot easily hire a manager and an accountant; partial adoption — a community would stand up the two easy systems and stop; and inconsistent technical support from the centre, which had agreed to devolve authority but had not fully reorganized itself to help anyone use it.

Half is not a disappointing number for a structural reform of this size. But I would rather tell you half than sell you a hundred percent, and any consultant who quotes you a hundred percent has not run one of these.

Authority has to be real

A local government without money is a suggestion box.

This is where most decentralization reform quietly fails. Responsibility travels down; authority and revenue stay up. The reform we wrote moved the tools themselves.

Alongside the governance act, business site-leasing law and the tax code were reformed so that communities gained the practical machinery to govern — local taxing and leasing authority transferred downward, not promised, not piloted. These are the tools that became available:

  • Business site leasing
  • Local sales taxes
  • Land use planning
  • Zoning ordinances
  • Revenue creation
  • Bond finance
  • Tax increment financing
  • Equity positions
  • A locally-owned development entity
  • Special development areas
  • Delegation of central responsibilities downward
  • Dispute resolution
  • Sub-units and joint agreements
  • Alternative forms of government

Using them requires supporting capacity, and there is no way around it: an experienced manager, legal counsel, accounting, and a standing committee with a real remit. A community that acquires the tools without the capacity will hold them and not use them, which looks from the outside like proof that devolution does not work.

And the first step is always a land use plan. Always. Nothing downstream — zoning, leasing, revenue, bond finance, a development entity — is available to a community that cannot yet state what its land is for.

What communities did with it

Once the authority was real, they used it in ways nobody had drafted for.

This is the argument for local decision-making in one line: the centre could not have designed these, because the centre did not know they were needed.

  • Architectural standards and green beltsCommunities set what their own places would look like, and drew the lines that would stay open.
  • Parks managed through delegationStewardship taken over from the centre by the people who live at the gate.
  • Special tax district financingRevenue raised locally against a defined area and a defined purpose.
  • Partnership between communitiesNeighbouring communities combining to take over functions of the central government outright.
  • Joint administrationShared management, shared accounting, shared economic development staff — the way small places afford competence.
  • Joint agreementsProjects developed together, with costs and benefits shared on terms both sides wrote.
  • Unique local ordinancesLaw that fits one valley, because one valley is what it was written for.
  • The whole economic development process, locallyNot a grant application to the centre. The process itself, held and run at home.

How it was built

It failed the first time.

The act was co-authored with a commission. Underneath it sat a major public policy process that gathered vision from three constituencies most reform ignores in favour of consultants: elders, youth and leaders. Alongside that, a deep study of the old systems, the culture and the ceremony — done at night and at weekends, for years, because nobody funds that part.

Then it went to the legislature and it failed.

We took it back, worked it again, and it passed on the second attempt — after which it was signed quickly. I say this every time, because the reform literature is full of clean stories and not one of them is true. A structural change that alters where power sits will be defeated at least once by the people it takes power from. Budget for that, politically and emotionally, or the first defeat will end the project.

If you are a community leader

Your first three steps, and you can start this month.

None of these require a budget, an outside adviser, or permission from anybody. They are the orientation work, and almost nobody does them before hiring someone.

  1. 01
    Write down the authority you actually hold. Not what you are told you hold. Read the enabling documents — the charter, the code, the delegation instruments, the leases. Most communities find they hold more than they use and considerably less than they were promised, and the gap between those two numbers is the entire strategy for the next five years.
  2. 02
    Make a land use plan. Always first, everywhere, without exception. Zoning, leasing, revenue, financing and any development entity are all downstream of a community being able to state what its land is for. A community without a plan is not underfunded. It is unable to receive funding.
  3. 03
    Name the functions you already perform, and give them positions. You are already governing water, grazing, disputes, ceremony and the young — informally, through people everyone can name. Give those functions a title, a term and a line in a budget and you have the beginning of a government that fits, built from what is already load-bearing rather than from an org chart somebody imported.

Beyond this point

The rest of this is done together.

What follows is the working method — the part that is genuinely a method rather than a principle.

  • The precinct mapping process
  • The nomination protocol, end to end
  • The certification criteria and the audit threshold
  • The authority-transfer sequence and its order
  • Ordinance and code drafting, and transition governance during handover

It is not a document. It is carried into a community and adapted to that community’s kinship, terrain and existing institutions, in the sequence that community can actually absorb. Handed over as a template it produces a binder. That is the only way it holds.

Working together

What an engagement actually looks like.

Governance design is a multi-year piece of work and I will say so at the first meeting. What follows is how it is usually staged.

One

Authority audit and vision process

What you hold on paper, what you actually exercise, and a real process — elders, youth, leaders — to establish what the government is being built to do.

Two

Design and drafting

Precincts, selection mechanism, positions, meeting process and the certification path, drafted into instruments your body can actually adopt.

Three

Transfer and capacity

Sequencing the authority that moves down, standing up the systems that let you hold it, and staying through the first defeat.

Who this is for. Indigenous and tribal governments, regional and municipal bodies inheriting a colonial-era code, newly autonomous territories, rural and post-industrial regions negotiating devolution, and any central government that has decided to push authority downward and wants it to survive the third year. Also the reverse case: a centre being asked to devolve, that needs a certification pathway it can defend.
