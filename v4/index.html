Tony Skrelūnas

I learned everything by lamplight, and by helping Masan.

I was raised between worlds. My great-grandmother Masan taught me to run at dawn with a stone in my mouth, on the high land of Black Mesa. My Lithuanian grandfather drove twenty-five miles of dirt road often in my childhood to sit in our hogan and pass on a tradition that had survived Soviet suppression. And by the light of a kerosene lamp, books and letters arrived from farther still — Switzerland, Lithuania, Japan — and a boy with no electricity read his way toward a wider world.

Three lineages. One lesson, told three ways: the old people knew how to live, and they left us the tools.

Before anything else

What is a far messenger?

Everything you know reached you because somebody carried it.

Every story your grandmother told you. Every song, every remedy, every way of doing a thing right. None of it grew where you found it. Somebody crossed the largest sand desert on earth on foot. Somebody lay in the hull of a canoe at night, reading the swells through the wood with their own body. They did that so you could know anything at all.

Those were the far messengers. Every people on earth raised them — the ones trained to carry what mattered across hard ground and arrive with it whole. Not the strongest. The steadiest.

The skill we are losing is not the stories. It is the carrying.

You do not make the calm. You retrieve it. The crossing is never made out on the sand. It is made beforehand, on the ordinary days when nothing is wrong and the practice feels pointless. Panic is not a mood. It is a failure of preparation, and you cannot build the container while the house is burning.

No messenger ever stood alone. Each one sat at the top of a whole civilisation organised around raising honoured people — the weather-reader, the seed-keeper, the one who knew which root fed you and which lied. In that world you rose by mastering something and serving your people with it. Your rising was not a threat. Your rising was your family’s glory.

We are in that kind of crossing now. And nobody is being raised for it.

Two hundred years from now, somebody will be sitting in the dark.

They will need exactly what we are holding right now. Where will their stories come from? From us, or from nowhere.

We are the far messengers now.

Two chapters, open

One is the crossing. One is the stage.

A messenger at the moment the water runs out. And what that training looks like carried into a hall full of ministers and founders. Press either.

On the fourth day, the well was dry. The Far Messengers · the opening +

On the fourth day, the well was dry.

He came down off the last dune to the place the water should have been, and the seep was cracked mud, pale, and there was nothing.

I want to stay in that moment longer than the story wants me to, because everything I have to say is inside it.

A frightened man does one thing here, and it is always the same thing. His heart bolts. He gets up and moves — fast, in some direction, because moving feels like doing. And they find him the next morning a few hours' walk away with the panic still on his face, and the well he needed sitting half a day north of where he died.

This man did not do that.

He sat down.

I have thought about that for eleven years. Not the courage of it. Anyone can be brave once. It is the trainedness of it — that at the worst moment of a crossing, with everything in his body screaming, the thing he reached for was a practice he had rehearsed a thousand times on days when nothing was wrong.

He softened his eyes to the whole horizon — not to search it, to widen it, because a narrowed eye is a frightened eye and a frightened eye reads nothing. He closed his fist around a small river-worn stone he had carried since the first morning and pictured it resting on his tongue, cool and smooth, which brings water back into a dry mouth by itself; I have done this and it works and I still do not entirely understand why. And he counted his breath the way he had been taught as a boy, to a rhythm like a slow drum: in for four, hold for four, out for six, hold for four.

He let the fear stand there beside him and gave it no chair to sit in.

That is the line I could not get out of my head. It is not mine — it is how his descendants tell it, and I heard it from a woman whose family has kept the telling. The fear is not the enemy. The fear is a guest who will stay as long as you keep offering it somewhere to sit.

And then his heart came down. And when it did, his quiet mind did the thing his racing mind could never have done.

It remembered.

A seep, half a day north, in a place no sensible person would look for water — which his grandfather had mentioned to him once, in passing, years before.

He got up. He went north. It was there.

And here is the part I love, the part that convinced me these were my people even though they are not my people: he laughed. Standing over a miserable brown trickle in the middle of the largest emptiness on earth, he laughed out loud, alone, with two days still to walk. Because among the ones who trained this way, the laughter was never the opposite of the calm.

It was the calm, arriving.

From The Far Messengers — Book Four

“Labas vakaras, Vilnius.” By Lamplight · The Scarf +

The black Mercedes sedan moved like a silent thought through the waking streets of Vilnius.

Inside, Tonas Yazzie existed in the calm eye of his own storm. On the seat beside him, a tablet glowed with the final slides of his keynote for the Vilnius Global Futures Forum. The title was sleek, modern: “Regenerative Systems for a Fractured World.”

He was the headline speaker. The boy from the mesa had arrived, in every sense the world measured.

That morning, in his suite at the Hotel Pacai, he had performed the old code. Move: a run along the frozen Neris River, his breath marking the dark. Order: his room an oasis of calm, notes sorted. Align: a silent prayer at the window, facing the direction of the sun’s return.

He was prepared. He was, as his assistant’s messages confirmed, “on brand.”

The car turned onto the cobblestone approach to the university. Students flowed through the gates, a river of scarves and backpacks against the Baltic cold. And then, the river parted.

There, on the sidewalk, a senelė was kneeling before her grandson. The boy, maybe seven, was a bundle of wool. The grandmother wore a scarf of deep forest green and maroon, its geometric patterns unmistakably ancient, hand-woven, real.

Her hands, red from the cold, were not just tying his scarf. They were arranging it. She pulled the wool snug around his neck, tucked a strand of hair under his hat, and said something into his ear. Her touch was not fussy; it was deliberate. It was the touch that installs armor.

The boy listened, his face grave, then stood a little taller, his small shoulders squaring as if bearing an invisible, honorable weight.

For Tonas, the world did not slow. It cracked open.

The Mercedes, the Italian wool of his suit, the keynote title — all of it evaporated into a silent, white flash.

He was not here. He was on a dusty bus, the vibrations a constant song in his bones. Masan sat beside him, her presence a mountain range of calm. Her hands, just as deliberate, were straightening the velvet collar of his shirt, her fingers brushing a piece of lint from his shoulder.

“They will see you,” she had said, her voice the only true north. “Let them see us.”

The car door opened. The cold air was a slap of reality. The senelė and the boy moved on, absorbed by the crowd. Tonas stood on the cobblestones, unmoored. The prepared speech in his pocket felt like a shell — accurate but hollow.

He walked into the vaulted hallway, toward the murmuring auditorium. An attendant gestured to the stage. “They’re ready for you, Mr. Yazzie.”

He entered the side stage darkness, the buzz of a thousand conversations washing over him like static. Then the introduction — his accomplishments a litany that felt suddenly like it belonged to someone else.

A path cleared for him. He walked to the podium, the lights warm and blinding. He placed his hands on the smooth wood, feeling the grain like a lifeline. He did not reach for his notes.

He looked out at the sea of expectant faces — ministers, thinkers, founders, students — and saw not an audience, but a council. He saw his children. He saw Masan.

He leaned into the microphone.

“Labas vakaras, Vilnius,” he said, his voice calm, clearing a space in the quiet. “Before we begin, I have a question for you.”

He paused, letting the shift settle.

“By a show of hands, how many of you feel that the systems we live by — our economies, our workplaces, even our personal lives — are somehow fundamentally broken? That they create efficiency but drain meaning. Generate wealth but erode health. Connect us digitally but leave us spiritually alone?”

A hesitant ripple, then a wave of hands rising.

“What if the future is not a place we invent? What if it’s a place we remember?”

The hall was utterly still.

Take a Stone Breath.

The count the messenger used on the fourth day. Before a hard conversation, a big meeting, or a race — pause and breathe the way he did.

Hold a small stone in your hand, or picture one. Feel the ages it took to come smooth. Give thanks to the water that carried it. Then begin.

In four. Hold four. Out six. Hold four. Five unhurried rounds. About three minutes. The long exhale is the body’s off-switch for alarm — a count the old runners had ten thousand years before anyone named the vagus nerve.

The comeback

College handed him an identity crisis. The old practice is what got him through it.

Raised by his great-grandmother in a hogan on Big Mountain — no electricity, four hundred sheep, a ceremony every dawn. He learned to read by kerosene lamp, and by that same lamp read the philosophy of Japan, a discipline he has been apprenticed to ever since. By choice, not by blood.

Then came the turn: he could be who he was. Everything since has been built on what that year taught him: capacity is not inherited, and it is not a personality. It is built.

Where he is now: a PhD candidate, writing a dissertation on human and organisational performance through three ancestral lenses — Diné, Lithuanian, Japanese. On faculty at 1440 Multiversity, and teaching this work in other parts of the world as a far messenger. Four novels in The Ancestral Watch. He runs at dawn, and hikes with a corgi named Roper.

The braid

Three traditions. Not a blend — a braid.

Two came by blood and one by choice. Each carries a discipline the other two do not. Kept separate, kept whole, and put to work together.

By blood

Diné

Dawn practice, corn pollen, the four sacred mountains, and a way of counting wealth that has never once been in dollars. Raised in it, in a hogan on Big Mountain.

By blood

Lithuanian

The forest, the song, and the stubbornness of a people who were supposed to disappear four times and did not. Endurance as an inherited technology.

By choice

Japanese

Read by kerosene lamp at fourteen and apprenticed to ever since. Restraint, repair, and the discipline of doing one thing completely before starting the next.

The tools for endurance, belonging, and purpose are not lost. They are dormant.
