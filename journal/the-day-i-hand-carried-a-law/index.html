The Day I Hand Carried a Law

It was not a hard question. They wanted to know exactly how this was going to work, and they asked us to show them a business plan.

November 17, 2026

The president's office in Window Rock is kind of regal, with mahogany doors and mahogany walls, the way an executive office should look, and one day in 1998 I sat in it with a law in my hands. The chief of staff, a man named Jeff Begay, had ushered me in. I had hand carried the Local Governance Act across from the Council chamber, where it had passed that same day. I think I was the only one in there with the president when he picked up his pen.

To tell you why that walk mattered I have to tell you how things were before. There were 110 chapters across the Navajo Nation and they were all the same. They functioned with a quorum of 25 members, some chapters really struggled to get one, and what a chapter could do was pass a resolution recommending something to the county or the state or the central government. They could not do any land use planning. They had no sales taxes, no management systems of their own, the same pay structure and the same positions everywhere.

So you had very large chapters, some with populations over 10,000, and very small ones with just a few hundred, some sitting on important high traffic intersections, and all of them were handed the same set of tools. It did not fit. I had done community plans by then, and I had read the research on how a government needs to match the culture of its people, and what I came to understand is that communities are closer to the issues, they are all different, and when there are mistakes it is better that they get addressed locally.

The Nation wanted to rethink all of this, and I applied to lead the work. I had to go before the Commission on Navajo Government Development several times, and those were my first serious interviews. They asked me about the policy process I would undertake and how I would go about things, and then they asked me the traditional questions, like what the sacred mountains are decorated with. I had to show I could speak Navajo, because most of the Nation at that time still did. I was hired.

I had a team. I want to say that plainly, because I was the one who had to be the main presenter and it can sound like one man did this. Our chair was Larry Foster, an older community leader who had worked off the reservation and went to United Nations meetings on indigenous rights in places like Geneva. We had young attorneys, and one of them was Sampson Martinez from Ramah, a rancher and a horse trainer who made sure he stayed out in the country the whole time he was getting his law degree. My grandpa was a horse trainer too, so we had a lot to talk about.

One of the things I loved about that Commission was that they were willing to work weekends and nights. They would give us an assignment at 5 o'clock and we would work until 1 in the morning to be ready when they reconvened. We met all over, in Albuquerque, in Farmington, in Durango, in Flagstaff. We had our prayers over our documents, and we respected the people who were Christian and the people in the Native American Church as well, because a law for everybody has to be carried in a way everybody can stand behind.

Then we took it to the Council, and the Council tabled it. There was a lot of politics around at the time, but I will tell you what the delegates actually asked us, because it was not a hard question. They wanted to know exactly how this was going to work. How would personnel work, what are the job descriptions, what kind of tax revenue could be generated locally? Show us a business plan.

They were right to ask. So we went back and put a whole implementation plan together. We made it professional, bound, with a hard cover in color. It had the bars and the charts and the graphs, the position descriptions, how an ordinance would work, what the revenues could be against the cost of salaries, how the alternative forms of governance would work, and how a chapter would do its own land use planning, which we were able to bring HUD dollars in for.

It also laid out what a chapter had to do to get certified, which was to stand up five management systems. I can give you four of them right off, accounting, personnel, property and records, and to this day I have to stop and think for the fifth one. It is procurement. I helped write the thing and I still lose that one.

Before we went back we held a study session for the Council at Northern Arizona University, and we held summits on governance and the economy where we educated the public. By that time nearly everybody was supportive. On the day itself Larry was supposed to present with me and something came up, he could not be there, and the Council called me up before them anyway. There was no debate. I cannot remember the count. It was overwhelming, and it felt like they were saying these guys have done a really good job.

Speaker Kelsey Begay, who had been part of the process since the beginning, signed it right there while the Council was still in session. Then I carried it across. President Albert Hale, who had run on what he called local empowerment, had stepped down by then, and Thomas Atcitty was the interim president. He told me this is very important for the nation, and he signed it. It became law almost that same day.

I was a young man and I thought I would have all kinds of big moments like that. What I think about now is the first vote more than the second. If we had walked into that chamber frightened and heard a no, we would have gone home and argued about who was against us. In The Council Fire there is a line about this, that a frightened people gets defensive and attacks, and "a calm people can sit in a circle and think together without it turning into a fight." The Council did its thinking out loud, and it handed us our homework.

Today 45 of the Nation's 110 chapters have adopted the act. Each of them did its own hard work to get there. This is not only a Navajo story. Any community, a village, a neighborhood, a small town with one stoplight, is going to be asked the same thing by the people it answers to, which is how exactly is this going to work.

So if a room sends your idea back to you, I encourage you to hear the question inside it before you hear anything else. Go and write the plan, put a cover on it, and bring your people with you when you come back.

Ahéhee'. Thank you.
