It Was Just Not My Day

Some days you're going to wake up negative, you just have those days.

November 27, 2026

In early October I recorded a voice memo to myself, thinking out loud about a day of leadership I was designing for a big room of directors and their staff, and somewhere in the middle of it I started talking about the interviews athletes give after they lose. I had not planned to. It came out because of something about people at the top of their game that has always stayed with me.

They don't look stressed. They don't look worried, even in defeat. A great player walks off after a loss in front of millions of people, a microphone comes up, and a lot of times they are just positive about it, almost light. It was just not my day. What you are watching in that interview is a nervous system trained to perform at a very high level, and it does not stop being trained when the score goes the wrong way.

Most of us do the opposite. We have a bad day and it becomes a verdict. The meeting went sideways, so maybe I am not cut out for this job. The kids fought all through dinner, so maybe I am doing it wrong as a parent. One hard day gets read as the truth about the whole life, and the body hears it and goes into fight or flight, and the next morning starts already braced.

What I have come to realize is that anything important in life is going to have days. I said it in the memo as a list, and I will leave it as a list. If you're a farmer, you're going to have days when it's really dry. If you're a runner, there are days you're hurting, you're sore, you're injured. If you're a parent, you have challenging days. If you're a couple, if you're a business owner, the same.

And some days you're going to wake up negative. You just have those days. The first thought is, oh, I don't want to be at work today, and there is nothing wrong with you for having it. I'm sure Phil Jackson had mornings like that, when he thought, oh man, I have to deal with Michael Jordan today, or I have to deal with Dennis Rodman. He still won six championships. The bad mornings were part of the six.

So the question I was really asking in that memo was never how to avoid the bad days. It was how a person approaches every day, no matter what, with the same kind of energy. I described the one I was looking for, the one who doesn't look at a hard day as, oh, I'm going to get fired, who really embodies that they belong there, that their work is enough, that they have done the hard work.

I think that sentence is the whole teaching. They belong there. Their work is enough. They have done the hard work. A good day cannot give you that, because the next day can take it away. You get it from the ordinary work you put in underneath all the days, good and bad, so that when the bad one comes it lands on something solid and does not go all the way down.

I also talked in the memo about the energy we hand to everyone else. If you read social media and how people bash each other, maybe you're one of the bashers or maybe you just read it, and either way it affects you, and you carry it into the room. Maybe it's somebody at home, and you can feel the energy the moment you walk in. You are causing an energy in the room, I said, and it is important to watch that.

Then I turned it around to the other kind of day, the one you have had as a manager or a parent or a teammate, when things just flowed and you didn't have to push. People wanted to sit with you at lunch. Somebody brought you a cup of coffee without you asking. You weren't wanting. That is the same nervous system the athlete has after the loss, and you have had it too, which means it is already yours to go back to.

Our ancestors understood this without any of the vocabulary. The people who carried on the songs and the language and the farming approached every day with a certain energy, a certain outlook, and they did not get to choose the weather. They had dry years and hard winters and long walks, and they kept that energy anyway, which is a big part of why we still have the songs.

In The Far Messengers I gave this to a navigator named Nalu. In a Swiss valley where the ice used to be, he stands up without notes and gives a room of ministers the whole shape of a crossing, "the loading, the long middle where nothing feels like progress, the bent swell, the beach and the grief and the hard, brave years after." The long middle is in that list on purpose. You can read the opening of The Far Messengers free.

So here is what I would hand you for the next bad day, and there will be one, for you and for me. When it comes, say the athlete's sentence to yourself, plainly and without any drama. It was just not my day. Then say the other three, out loud if you can. I belong here. My work is enough. I have done the hard work. Then go do one small, ordinary piece of tomorrow's work, because that is where the next good day gets built.

Ahéhee'.
