The Year I Failed Out of College

After we lost Masan, I failed out of college. My grandmother Ada let me rest a few nights, and then she put me to work.

November 14, 2026

After we lost Masan, I failed out of college. I have written that sentence before, in the opening pages of a book, and it still looks smaller on the page than it felt at the time, because she was not a grandmother I visited on holidays. She was my great-grandmother, she took me when she was sixty-four years old, and she raised me every day on Big Mountain, in a hogan with no electricity, a kerosene lamp, four hundred sheep and a cornfield that needed attention all summer long.

I want to be careful about how that sounds, because people hear no electricity and picture something it was not. It was a disciplined and abundant household. We were up before the sun, we prayed facing east, we swept the floor, and then we walked the flock. Every year we mixed red clay and re-rounded the hogan by hand until it was beautiful, and it was beautiful. At night there were stories and humor and relatives, and nobody sat alone.

So when she was gone, a great deal went with her that I had never needed to name, because it had always simply been there. The way I said it in the book is the truest way I know to say it. I forgot the stone for a while. I tried to become someone else. School did not survive that year, and for a while I did not know where I was supposed to be.

What saved me was not a counselor or a book or a plan. I went to my grandmother Ada's house. She let me rest a few nights, which I needed, and then she put me to work, which I needed more. The extended family was preparing a nine-day gathering and everyone was helping, every pair of hands already spoken for, and Ada did not let me sit in my grief. She handed me a task.

She did not explain what she was doing, and I did not ask. Nobody had ever sat me down and explained the practice when I was a boy either, because it reached me the way a recipe reaches you, from someone standing next to me who expected me to do it right. Ada was the same.

Looking back, I believe it was the oldest medicine she had. Not rest by itself, and not talk. Work that matters, done beside your people, with your hands busy and somebody next to you who expects you to show up tomorrow.

It came back to me while I was collecting herbs with my uncle. We were out on the land with our hands busy, gathering what was needed, and something I had forgotten returned to me there. It was not all at once, and it was not a vision or a revelation. It was just enough for me to remember who I was, and where I came from, and what I had been carrying the whole time without knowing it was in my hands.

I think about that a lot now, because so much of my work is about intergenerational healing, and that afternoon is the clearest example of it I have. What Masan put into me over all those mornings did not leave when she did. It was stored in my body, in the way I knew which plants to reach for, in the walking, in the rhythm of working beside family. Ada did not have to teach me anything new. She only had to put me somewhere it could wake up. It did.

There is a hard truth here and I will name it plainly. It is common, in families like mine, for a young person to leave home for school still carrying the loss of the elder who raised them, a long way from the land and the relatives who would have held them, and to lose their footing far from anyone who can see it happening.

This is a challenge among greater society too. Grief knocks young people off their path in every kind of family, in every country, and I suspect many of the ones who make it back had someone who handed them something to do.

I did go back. I finished at the top of my class, I was the Standard Bearer at graduation at Northern Arizona University in 1992, and then I went on to my MBA. What I remember most from that second run is that every door had a person behind it. A professor opened the door to the MBA, and the most intimidating professor I ever had was the one who nominated me to lead my graduating class. None of that happens if Ada lets me sit in my grief for a month.

I have come to believe that the comeback is not a return to who you were before. I was never going to get back the young man who had Masan beside him, and I did not need to. In The Council Fire I wrote that "the rebuild was never the recovery of a lost perfection." It was honest forward-building, by people with scars, and I was one of them. You can find the book at ancestralwatch.com/series/the-council-fire.

So if you have read this far, and someone in your family has fallen out of something this year, a school, a job, a marriage, a way of living, I encourage you to do what Ada did. Let them rest a few nights. Then hand them a task, a real one, and do it right beside them, out on the land if you can, with the radio off and the work in front of you. And if that someone is you, go find the people who are already busy preparing for something, and ask what needs carrying.

Ahéhee'.
