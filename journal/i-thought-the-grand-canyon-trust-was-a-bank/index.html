I Thought the Grand Canyon Trust Was a Bank

I just saw the way he moved, and the way he worked, and the way he lived, and I have aspired to it ever since.

November 17, 2026

It was the early nineties and I was working on my MBA at Northern Arizona University when one of my professors told me I should go and apply for a job at a place called the Grand Canyon Trust. It was kind of like an internship. I heard the word trust and I thought it was a bank, and in those days I believed the thing to do when a bank wanted to see you was to get a fancy suit. So I pulled together what little money I had, I bought the suit, and I went to the interview.

The interview was in Flagstaff, in an old two-story building everybody called the Homestead, and the person sitting across from me was not a banker. She was kind of a hippie lady named Fran, I still remember her name, and I sat there in my new suit while she asked her questions, and I got the job. It is a funny story to me now. I had dressed for the wrong building and it worked out anyway.

The Homestead sat on the property of the Museum of Northern Arizona. It was very old and crickety, and the floors were actually crooked, they sloped down as you walked across them. It is where the actor Ted Danson grew up, because his dad was the director of the museum. That is where the Grand Canyon Trust was getting started, with only about four employees, and I was the young one there, really the only young one.

It was a small organization and it did not have a lot of money, but it had some big wheels behind it. Bruce Babbitt was one of the founders, along with Jim Trees and Stewart Udall, the former Secretary of the Interior. Ed Norton, the father of the actor, was the president and worked out of Washington, D.C., and sometimes he would come in and just stay there with us. There was Roger Clark and a few others, a small fledgling crew, and we built something out.

And there was the executive director, Jim Ruch. Picture an avid outdoorsman, with the hair and the face and the eyes and the eyebrows, a man with a real presence who was also a writer. Whole communities would anticipate him arriving. I watched towns like Sedona wait on him, and I watched Native leaders do the same, and they were in awe of this guy, of his vision and his experience and the way he carried it.

What I remember most is the coffee. Jim always had a cup of coffee, and when he was working and leading a meeting he walked around the room with that cup in his hand, joking around, taking his time, and the room went with him. Nobody sat me down and taught me this as a lesson. I just saw the way he moved, and the way he worked, and the way he lived, and I have aspired to it ever since.

Then came my first real assignment. A tribe called the Kaibab Paiute had turned down a waste incinerator, and everybody who had advised them and encouraged them to turn it down just left them hanging afterwards. I was a young intern and I was given the job of figuring out something. I was able to recruit another master's student, a planner from Oregon named Blair Leist, and together we came up with a strategy built on community-based planning.

We held all kinds of meetings. We listened first. We went household to household with a set of questions, and what we were after was a future the community itself envisioned, the values the people wanted to protect, and then feasibility laid over the top of all of it so the plan could stand up. It became the first economic plan ever developed for that tribe, the state gave us a lot of help, and the nation adopted it. That was my first experience of what was possible.

I have thought a lot about why that work held, two students sent out from a small office with crooked floors. Part of it was the method, and I have been using that method for more than thirty years now. Part of it, I believe, was what I had been watching every day back at the Homestead. A man who walks a room with a cup of coffee is telling everybody in it that there is time, that nobody is in trouble, that we are going to visit first and then we are going to work.

My own people have a word for the front end of that. Woshdee means come in, but the actions around it have a deeper meaning. We are taught to greet in a happy manner, to welcome in, to offer food and coffee, to engage attentively in the visit, and part of it is to make the guest feel relaxed. Jim came from a different world than my great-grandmother did, and when I saw him cross a room I recognized it anyway.

I have seen the same thing in a lot of places since, in kitchens and chapter houses and boardrooms, wherever people have to sit down over something hard. I put it into Stone Breath, in a village in Lithuania, when a tired young man shows up at an old woman's door carrying a bundle he has brought a long way. She does not ask him one question. She points to a chair and says, "Sit. Eat first. Then we'll talk."

From that little office I went on to build a national program, and I have led more meetings than I could count for you, with tribes, with hospitals, with companies, with whole communities deciding their future. To this day I envision how Jim walked around as he was working, and sometimes I emulate that. I pour a cup of coffee and I walk the room with it before I ask anybody for anything. It still works.

So if you have a hard meeting coming, with your family or your staff or your community, I encourage you to try it the old way first. Welcome the people in. Feed them something, joke a little, and let them see by how you move that there is time. You do not need the fancy suit, and I can tell you that from experience.

Ahéhee'. Thank you.
