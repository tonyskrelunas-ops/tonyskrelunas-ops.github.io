The Council Fire Arrives With a Family on Its Cover

The Council Fire was released this past week with a new cover. Twelve keepers from twelve peoples, the letters their grandparents left them, and what a people grows back.

October 19, 2026

This past week The Council Fire was released with a new cover, and the cover came together over a single morning. I wanted elders with their backs to us and a horizon visible under the fire. I wanted the path in the red of Sedona, the hues of red rock, water, sky and clouds, with the mesa on one side and the mountains on the other. Then came one last change, and that was the one.

The figures on the path became a family: a man and a woman, with a child in gold walking between them and holding both their hands. Four elders stand in the foreground, two white-haired and two dark, in blankets of diamonds, stripes, dots and chevrons. The family walks away from us toward the river. Look closely and you will see the two grown ones wearing the stripes and the dots of two of the elders.

The title sits inside the great ring of light at the top, which I think of as the fire. Below the picture is the subtitle the book carries now, The Original Instructions for the World We Rebuild, and across the top, The Ancestral Watch Series. The book belongs to that series and stands on its own. You do not need to have read Stone Breath to sit at this fire.

What the book carries

Twelve keepers from twelve peoples, on six continents, and not one of them has heard the others speak. Käthe keeps the pumps under the Ruhr that can never be switched off. Illary carries her seeds up the Andes ahead of the heat. Nizhoní, who is Diné, carries a river stone her grandmother took from a seep that had gone dry. Each of the twelve carries one small object, and at the end a child holds them all.

They begin with their grandparents. For decades a retired miner in Germany, a seed keeper in the Andes, a song keeper in Lithuania, a land keeper in the desert and others like them wrote letters across oceans, about the water and the seed and what is owed to the land. When they grew old they put the bundles into younger hands and said some version of the same thing: “It is yours now. Finish what we began.”

That handing-over is the subject under everything in the book: what passes between generations, what was broken in the passing, and what a people can grow back. The first part, What Broke, is honest about the weather and the great rivers shrinking to threads. It is just as honest about the trance of comfort, and about the alarm that rang in people’s bodies all day and never shut off.

The book is careful to say the alarm did not make anyone bad. It made them unable to hear the warnings. Then it turns to the far messengers, the ones every people raised to carry what mattered across country that kills. A man crossing the Empty Quarter finds the far well dry on the fourth day, sits down in the shade of a dune, slows his breath, and only then remembers a seep his grandfather told him about once.

A 76-second film for The Council Fire. The far messengers of the deserts, the oceans, the mountains, and of time.

The second part, The Keepers, gives each of the twelve a chapter in their own country. Then they meet, at a fire on a hill in Lithuania on the longest day of the year. Readers of Stone Breath will recognize two of the elders who come down from the house at the top of the meadow.

Nizhoní will not call herself a keeper. She grew up in the city, came home at twenty-six to learn the rest, and found that the ones who carried the whole of it had stopped being replaced. Vahan tells her she is the most useful person at the fire, and Käthe tells her why: the rest of them are describing what they have, and she is describing what is coming for them.

The third part, What They Built, is the long middle of the book, a working manual carried as story. Every proposal is held against one question, whether it repeats the cycles of collapse or honors the great-grandchildren, and any one fire can stop it. Wealth is counted in four kinds, financial, relational, cultural and natural, and the ledger is read aloud to the whole community at the winter solstice.

The first company the keepers charter is a pump works, because the water under that valley will keep rising and the work can never leave. A seventeen-year-old on its board moves a deferred repair forward by asking who will still be in the room when it comes due.

The chapter I would hand to anyone working on healing is the one about the three a people had to grow back. A physician who has spent thirty years measuring what sustained fear does to a body, and then to that body’s children, says it plainly: “Fear is not merely painful. Fear is disabling.” Then she gives the generous half of the same truth. Settle the body first, then build, and give it three generations, perhaps four, one child and one family at a time.

That chapter is also honest about what turns sideways inside any people under long pressure, and it makes no one the enemy. Before the council designs a single thing for the ones who tear others down, the admission goes around the fire, keeper after keeper, that each of them has held that knife.

Let me say plainly that this is also a funny book. Käthe, in her kitchen with the river in the street, telephones the office and gets a recording saying it will reopen on Monday at eight. Two men from a ministry come out to register a Kalenjin herd that is walking north to stay alive, and the younger one writes nomadic in a little box. The keepers laugh in the councils and through the hard winters, because joy is how the surviving is done.

The keepers are invented. Almost nothing under them is. The last hard-coal mine in Germany did close four days before Christmas, and the water beneath the Ruhr does have to be pumped forever. The ways forward are real too: a community that owns its sun instead of renting it, wealth counted in four kinds instead of one, a council that asks one question of everything it builds.

In the fourth part, What Comes After, the twelve tell the crossing in their own voices. Then, gray and some of them walking with sticks, they come back to the place of the first fire and make a pact. The wires will come down and the mail will stop, so the story itself becomes the channel and the tokens become the proof. A last part, The Well World, sits a hundred years on.

Who it is for

The book is built to be told out loud. It opens by putting a stone in your hand, and the back of it holds the stone breath in full, a way to run a reading circle as a council instead of a book club, and a crossing to play standing up. Each section is sized for one evening and one voice, with three questions after: where this is happening in our own country, what sign we have seen, and what has to be carried.

It was written for the ones who already carry something for other people, the grandmother keeping the language at the kitchen table, the engineer who stays after the work has gone, the young one who came home to learn what the summers only showed them the edge of. It was written for the ones on their way to becoming that, too. If that is you, you will know it within a few pages.

The Council Fire runs about 110,000 words, with a glossary of the old words and working appendices at the back. It is direct on Payhip for $20 at payhip.com/b/GB2eg, and the book page at ancestralwatch.com carries an excerpt and the twelve keepers with their peoples.

The first voice in the book belongs to someone seven generations down, holding all twelve tokens, who says of the world the keepers made out of the broken one: “It is hard here, and it is good.” The reader this book was written for will hear everything that sentence carries, and will begin.
