What the Navigators Were Not Allowed to Miss

You're taught that your home is with you.

November 27, 2026

On an evening in October I sat in a circle with students at Prescott College and told them about the navigators of the Pacific, the people who found and settled almost every island in sixteen million square miles of ocean with no compass and no instrument of any kind. I have told the story of one master navigator before. That night I wanted to tell them about the children.

The first thing the children were taught, the way I understand it, was not the stars. They would cover the child's eyes and have them lie down in the hull of a canoe, out on the sea, and just feel it, the roll and the lift and the long pull underneath, until the body could read the water before the mind had a word for it. Then came the quiet.

That is the part I have thought about almost every day since. They learned silence, and they learned to control their minds, and the way I said it to the students was plain. You can't be longing for your mama out there, or your girlfriend, or your boyfriend. You can't be missing that TV show. You're taught that your home is with you.

The students laughed at the TV show, which I meant them to, and then a few of them got quiet, which I also meant. Every one of them had a crossing of their own coming. I wanted them to hear that the old navigators did not treat longing as a private sadness to be endured. They treated it as a leak, the kind of small thing that could quietly sink a voyage.

Here is why it mattered so much. From a canoe you can only see about three miles ahead, so by the time you see an island you are pretty much at the island. Everything before that moment is reading, the birds that go out in the morning and come home at night, the light under a far cloud, the swells bending around land you cannot see. Everything is a count. If your count is off, you are probably going to die.

You cannot keep a count like that with half your mind back on the beach. Longing is a beautiful thing at the right fire, but on the water it takes attention, and attention is the only instrument the navigator has. So discipline of mind came first, and right behind it came the body, the heart rate brought down slow, under sixty beats a minute the way I understand it, so he could feel the swells right.

I study a lot of the old traveling peoples, the crossers of the Arabian desert, the Hopi, a people in Australia whose whole map is carried in songs, and the number one thing in every one of them is controlling your nervous system. The ocean and the desert are different country, but they are honest in the same way. If you lose it out there, it's pretty much sayonara.

I described it to the students the way it happens in a body. Your heart rate goes up, your nervous system over-activates, you go into panic mode, your brain can't reason, your executive function goes away, you start sweating, and in the desert all that water just pours out of you. You are not going to make it very far. The panic does what the sun could not have done on its own.

But if you are trained, you are going to stop and calm yourself down, and no matter what happens you are not going to panic, and most likely you are going to find a solution. You're going to remember a song. You're going to reason. You're going to look at the clouds and the wind, at how the canyons run off a mountain, at the plants and the animals.

There was one more part, and it made the room laugh the hardest. You had to be humorous. Say we are all on a boat together, I told them, and I'm your navigator, and inside I'm thinking, I don't know where the hell we are. I can't say that to you guys. So the navigator makes a joke, and the crew laughs, and everybody's heart comes down a little, including his, and his count comes back to him.

That is not hiding the truth from the crew, as I see it. It is keeping the one instrument on board in working order, so that by morning he can tell them the truth and also where to steer. A frightened navigator and a frightened crew make each other worse with every hour, and a laughing one makes each other better, and the old peoples knew that long before anyone measured a heartbeat.

I wrote a version of this into Stone Breath before I understood I was writing it. Tonas goes as a boy from his great-grandmother's side to a working ranch school down the mountain, and the thing nobody expected, least of all him, was that it never felt like leaving. "He simply carried the same self twenty minutes down the mountain and set it loose in bigger country." You can read the opening of Stone Breath free.

Most of us will never steer a canoe across open ocean, but we all have our crossings, a new job in a new city, a hospital waiting room, a first year far from home, a long season of caring for someone we love. And most of us carry a little longing into every one of them. We are in the room and also back at the house, or ahead in next week, or inside a screen, and the count drifts while we are gone.

So here is what I would hand you, and you can start it today. When you notice you are longing for somewhere else, do not scold yourself, just notice it, the way a navigator notices a swell that does not belong. Then bring your heart down. Breathe in for four, hold for four, out for six. Then count something real in front of you, or remember a song, or find the one funny thing in the situation and say it out loud.

Your home is with you. It always was. The people you love are not less yours because you are fully where you are, and the ones who sent you out would much rather you arrived.

Ahéhee'.
