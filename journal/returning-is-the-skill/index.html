Human Resilience

Returning Is the Skill

A story for my cousins in their mid twenties, and for anyone going through the motions. What an old runner, a Lithuanian singer and ten people who were fired, turned away and counted out can teach you about tomorrow morning.

November 16, 2026

The alarm goes off at 6:40 and her thumb is on the phone before her feet are on the floor, eleven minutes of other people's lives before she has had one minute of her own, then the shower, then the same gray polo with the store's name over the pocket, then forty minutes in traffic, then eight hours selling phone plans in a strip mall between a nail salon and a tax office, then home, then the couch, then the phone again until one or one-thirty, then the alarm.

Her name is Dani and she is twenty-five. She is good at the job, customers ask for her, and if you asked her mother how Dani is doing her mother would say fine, she is doing fine, and it would be true the way a parked truck is fine.

What her mother has not been told is that under Dani's bed there is a pharmacology textbook, the $180 kind, from the second year of a nursing program she left three years ago after one exam went the wrong way, and that she told everyone she left because of money, and that she has not said the word nurse out loud since.

I should tell you that Dani is made up. I have many cousins in their mid twenties, and she is none of them and a little of all of them, built the way I build the people in my books, out of true things. I believe a lot of young people are living some version of her story, going through the motions, carrying an old hurt or an old failure they have never said out loud, with a dream set down somewhere along the way. What do you do when the dream got dropped and you were the only one who saw it fall? How long is too long before you pick it back up? Is twenty-five already too late?

So here is what happens to Dani. At a family cookout in October her great-aunt, who is eighty-one and sees everything, watches her check her phone four times during one plate of food, and when the plates are cleared she puts a small river stone in Dani's hand, gray and smooth and about the size of a quarter, and closes Dani's fingers over it.

"Carry this for one week," she says. "In your hand, never in your mouth. Before you touch that phone in the morning you hold this, you say thank you for three things, and you breathe the way I show you. Five rounds. Then you can go be busy."

Dani laughs, because what else do you do. But she puts the stone in her pocket.

I will come back to her. First I want to tell you where that stone comes from, because it is a real one.

The runner they called Sore Foot

My great-grandmother Masan took me when she was sixty-four and raised me on Big Mountain, with the sheep and the cornfield and a kerosene lamp, and when I was old enough she told me about her father. His name was Keh Yazh and they called him Sore Foot. He lived on Big Mountain in the late 1800s, he ran east every morning, and then he went to work, a large flock of sheep and cornfields that needed him all summer. When word came of a twenty mile race against Hopi, Zuni and Apache runners and men from the Plains who had traveled for weeks, he prepared the way he always prepared, his family rode with him for days, and he won.

He ran with a small stone to keep his breath steady when his lungs were burning. The old runners carried it in the mouth. I teach it in the hand, with the stone only imagined on the tongue, and that is the whole safety rule, the stone never goes in your mouth. He passed it to Masan, and Masan passed it to me.

In Stone Breath I gave Masan a scene by the fire with a seven-year-old boy. "The other runners thought they were racing each other," she tells him. "Keh Yazh was racing the land." The boy asks if running was his job, and she says that before the races there were people whose whole work was to carry, the word, the medicine, the agreement between two families, the news that could not wait, across country that would kill an ordinary person. They were trained from small in how to breathe, how to run, and how to be by themselves for four days without their own mind turning against them.

Anyone can leave with it. Arriving with it is the work.

That is Masan's line in the book, and it is the center of everything I teach. I call these people far messengers. The phrase is mine, it is not an old word, and I say so in the book. The work is old, and every people on earth once raised someone to do it.

Now look at your own week. Rent, a job, a family with opinions, a phone with two hundred more opinions, and maybe something from years ago that still tightens your chest at three in the morning. That is hard country and you are already crossing it. The open questions are what you are carrying, who it is for, and whether you will arrive with it whole.

Thanks first, then the breath

Here is the practice the great-aunt showed Dani, the way Stone Breath gives it. Hold a small smooth stone in your hand. Before anything else, give thanks, for the water, for the place, for being here at all. The old ones never took from a stream without giving thanks first. In The Far Messengers, book four of the series, a Lithuanian songkeeper named Rasa Vaitkutė does it as a plain inventory every morning, before the day can tell her what she is short of. Two legs. This work. My daughter asleep down the hall. Thirty seconds, out loud, and she admits she did it for weeks feeling like an idiot.

Then imagine the stone resting on your tongue, cool and smooth. Only imagine. Notice what your jaw does. It goes soft, because you cannot balance a stone on a tense tongue, and when the jaw goes soft the shoulders come down after it.

Then count. Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Breathe out slow for six. Hold for four. Five rounds, and Rasa's grandmother was firm about that number, five is where it takes.

The thanks is where the abundance mindset lives, and it is a long way from pretending everything is great. In the book Rasa says the old carriers did not get themselves full so they could feel good. "They got full because a full carrier reads country better, arrives intact, and delivers. It was equipment for a job."

A frightened mind sees less. A person who starts the morning by counting what is already in their hands walks into the interview or the family dinner with their eyes wide, and that person notices the door when it opens. Abundance is how a messenger prepares, the way you would fill a water bottle before a long run. Your ancestors crossed deserts on it. You can cross a Tuesday.

Sixty kilometers

Rasa is my favorite failure in the four books. She is a singer. She does not run. Her grandmother enters her in a race without asking, one hundred and sixty kilometers across sand in a country she has never seen, and when Rasa says I am a singer, I don't even run, her grandmother, who is peeling potatoes, says, "That is why."

She gets to about sixty kilometers and sits down in the sand and cries while a kind race official stands over her. Her legs were fine. What took her out was the counting, how far is left, how much water, whether she was a fool for being there, that voice, for hours, in a country that gives you nothing else to look at. Sitting there she understood it was the same voice that had kept her from holding her line in the old songs, only quieter at home because at home she had three jobs to drown it out.

Do you know that voice? I do. Most of us keep one job and a phone so we never have to hear it.

When she came home her grandmother taught her the breath. It did not fix her, she says. It gave her a handle, one thing she could do when the noise started, to "do something instead of obeying it." By the time we meet her she is fifty-one and has crossed sand eleven times, and she tells us the thing I most want my young cousins to hear, that you do not make the calm out there at the sixtieth kilometer or at the phone call in the night. You can only spend what you already stored, on days when nothing was wrong, in a practice that felt pointless at the time.

Now back to Dani. Day one she stands in the kitchen in her socks holding a rock and feels ridiculous. Day two she says thank you for coffee, for her little brother, and for a car that started, does the five rounds, and gets all the way to the freeway before she looks at the phone. Day three, the same. Day four she sleeps through it, grabs the phone, and is forty minutes deep in other people's vacations before she remembers, and that night she is up until two telling herself this is just who she is.

Day five she picks the stone up again.

That morning, day five, is the entire practice. The other voice in The Far Messengers is a Hawaiian navigator named Nalu Kealoha-Nakamura, whose teacher would not show him a single star for the first year and a half, because he was building the container before he poured anything into it. Late in the book Nalu is asked what happens when he loses his calm in front of his own son, and he says you repair it out loud, in front of him. The skill, he says, is "Returning. That's all it ever was." And Rasa adds that the old people had a word for someone who kept coming back to it. An adult.

It is okay to fail, and I can prove it

I will start with me. After we lost Masan, I failed out of college. I have told that story in The Year I Failed Out of College, so I will only say here that my grandmother Ada let me rest a few nights and then put me to work, that I did go back, and that I finished at the top of my class, was the Standard Bearer at graduation at Northern Arizona University in 1992, and went on to my MBA. The young man who failed out and the one who carried the standard were the same person.

Here is how we picture the road, and how it is actually walked.

Now the world. I checked every one of these, because many stories of this kind turn out to be legend, and you deserve the real ones.

Elon Musk. In 1996 the investors in his first company, Zip2, brought in a new chief executive over him. In September 2000 the board of his second, the one that became PayPal, replaced him as chief executive while he was away on his honeymoon. He was twenty-nine.

Steve Jobs. At thirty he was on the losing side of a boardroom struggle at Apple, the company he had started, and he left in 1985. He was back in 1997, and he later told a graduating class at Stanford that being fired was the best thing that could have happened to him.

Oprah Winfrey. Born into poverty in rural Mississippi to a teenage single mother. In 1977 she was taken off the six o'clock news desk at WJZ in Baltimore, where she had been co-anchor, and given smaller assignments. The next year the same station put her on a local talk show, and that was the door.

J.K. Rowling. She wrote much of the first Harry Potter book as a single mother on government benefits in Edinburgh, and twelve publishers turned the manuscript away before Bloomsbury said yes.

Soichiro Honda. People say Toyota turned him down for a job, and that part is legend. The record is better. In the 1930s he started a small company in Japan to make piston rings, and Honda's own history calls the early work a series of technical failures. By one account, of fifty rings he sent to Toyota, three passed inspection. So at thirty he sat in on night classes at a technical school and got them right. Then an air raid destroyed his factory, an earthquake followed, and in 1946 he started the small workshop that became Honda.

Jack Ma. By his own telling he failed China's college entrance exam twice, and when KFC came to China he was one of twenty-four people who applied. Twenty-three were hired. He went on to found Alibaba.

Billy Mills. Oglala Lakota, raised on Pine Ridge, orphaned at twelve. He went to the 1964 Olympics in Tokyo almost unknown, and in the last stretch of the 10,000 meters he came from third place past the world record holder and won the gold. No other runner from the Americas has won that race, before or since.

Viola Davis. She grew up in Central Falls, Rhode Island, in apartments she has described as condemned and full of rats. She went on to Juilliard, and she is one of the few performers to hold an Emmy, a Grammy, an Oscar and a Tony.

Arvydas Sabonis. My Lithuanian side will forgive me for leaving him this late. He suffered a devastating Achilles tendon injury in 1986, at twenty-one, had more surgery in 1988, and came back that same year to win Olympic gold in Seoul. In 1992 he helped bring a newly independent Lithuania its bronze medal, and he reached the NBA at thirty, on those same legs.

Eliud Kipchoge. Raised in Kenya by a single mother who taught school, he ran three kilometers to class every day. In 2012 he finished seventh at the Kenyan trials and did not make the Olympic team, so he moved up to the marathon. In 2017 he tried to run one in under two hours and came in twenty-five seconds over. In Vienna in 2019 he ran 1:59:40.

I have to be honest about what this list proves. Failing does not promise you anything. What the list proves is smaller and more useful. Being fired, being turned away, flunking the exam and growing up with very little are ordinary features of the road, they show up in the lives of the people we admire most, and so they cannot be evidence that you are on the wrong one. In the book Nalu says the danger was never failure, "failure keeps you sharp." The danger is comfort, the couch and the phone and the parked truck.

What other people think

When I first had some success I worried a great deal about how my own extended family would judge my decisions, and I have written about that before. I believe that worry holds many of our young people back from high achievement, and some take their own success apart with their own hands so they will never have to be judged for it. This is a challenge among greater society too. Every village, every office and every group chat has people watching.

Go back to Masan's line. Keh Yazh was racing the land. At those races he ran among strangers, sometimes among words that cut, and what he trained for was a clear mind that would not react to provocation. He did not look back.

The old carriers had a simple answer. Know who you are carrying for. A messenger with a message for one village does not stop to poll the other villages along the way.

The far messenger's daily kit

There are basic things to being healthy, and they are so plain that people are almost embarrassed to ask about them. I am a storyteller and a planner, not a doctor, and none of this is medical advice. It is what my grandfather did, he walked six miles a day into his nineties and lived to one hundred and three, and what Rasa lays out in the book when she is asked what her day actually looks like. Her whole list is "Sleep, movement, real food, real people, and a body that is not running from something."

Sleep comes first, because everything else leans on it. Rasa is in bed by half past nine and says she has never once regretted going to bed early, so pick an hour you can keep most nights and guard it. Morning light and water come next, outside in the first part of the day even for two minutes, the way Masan faced east every morning of her life, and a full glass before the coffee. Move every day, a walk at the least and a real sweat a few times a week, and eat real food, mostly things your great-grandmother would recognize, sitting down, with somebody else when you can.

Keep the phone out of the first minutes of the day and the last hour of the night, which Rasa does for one plain reason, she sleeps better. And get people and open sky into every day, because the old practices were never done alone in an apartment. They came with elders and a circle and somebody who would tell you the truth about yourself.

The old hurt

Some of you are carrying something heavier than a failed exam. A house that was not safe. A parent who left or drank or died. Something done to you that was never yours to carry. I will name it plainly, and I will say just as plainly that you are whole. In my family and in every family I know there are young people who lost their footing after a loss like that, far from anyone who could see it happening.

Rasa is asked what a person should do when the line they come from is broken, when it was cruel, or when it was empty. Her answer is the strongest sentence in the book. "Then you are the one it restarts with. That's not a consolation. That's an assignment."

The breath will give you a handle. It was never meant to carry all of it. The carriers did not cross alone either, there was always a person waiting at the far end and a person who had trained them at the near one. So if what you are holding is heavier than a morning practice, bring it to someone you trust, an elder, a friend, or a counselor or doctor whose work this is. Asking is what a messenger does when the load is real.

Dani, in January

By the new year she has done the five rounds more mornings than she has skipped, and she has gone back to it after every skip.

One Sunday she sits down at the kitchen table and tells her mother the truth about the exam. Her mother stirs her coffee for a while. "I know where the book is," she says. "I vacuum under that bed."

That week Dani writes to the nursing program. The answer comes in two days and it is not a parade. The next class starts in August, there is a placement exam in March, and she will need to retake one course. She reads it in the stockroom with her heart going, and she closes her hand around the stone, five rounds between the boxes of phone cases. Then she reads it again with a quiet mind and signs up for the exam.

I do not know if she passes in March. I made her up and I still do not know. What I know is that she is carrying something again, she knows who it is for, and she has a way to come back to herself on the mornings it goes wrong.

Tomorrow morning

Tonight, find a small smooth stone, or anything small and smooth, set it where your phone usually sleeps, and put the phone to charge across the room.

Tomorrow, before you touch the phone, pick up the stone and go stand outside, or at a window, facing the light. Say three things you already have, out loud, plain ones. Then the breath, with the stone in your hand and never in your mouth. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Hold for four. Five rounds. Drink a glass of water and walk for ten minutes.

Then do one small thing toward whatever you set down. One email. One page. One phone call. It only has to be small enough that you will really do it.

And tomorrow night, before the last hour, ask yourself the three questions Rasa's family asks at their table. What did I carry today? What did I learn? Who did I honor?

If you would like company in it, First Light, a short morning practice, is at Ancestral Peaks, the whole stone breath is at ancestralpeaks.com/practice, and the stories are in Stone Breath and The Far Messengers.

So if you have read this far, and you are twenty-five and tired and wondering whether the window has closed, I encourage you to let go of the idea that you are late. You come from people who carried what mattered across harder country than this, and they would tell you what Masan told me with a hand on my shoulder before dawn. The ancestors are walking. Don't let them find you asleep. Pick up the stone, give thanks first, and when you drop it, because you will, pick it up again.

Ahéhee'. Ačiū. Thank you.
