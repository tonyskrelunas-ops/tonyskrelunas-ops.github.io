Human Resilience

Returning Is the Skill

For anyone in the world, twenty to forty, who is struggling and feels they are not enough. Eight small daily practices that rebuilt my confidence from the ground up, and can rebuild yours.

November 16, 2026

There was a stretch of years when you could have found me out running by the Peaks in Flagstaff, Arizona, the mountains above my university, a student with a job and a young family, and if you could have seen inside my head you would have found me acting out a man who did not exist yet. I was visioning who I would be, and playing him, mile after mile. I want to tell you how I got to that trail, because I got there by falling.

My great-grandmother Masan took me when she was sixty-four and raised me every day on Big Mountain, in the high desert of the Navajo Nation, in a hogan, our round home, with no electricity, a kerosene lamp, four hundred sheep and a cornfield that needed attention all summer long. It was a disciplined and abundant household, up before the sun, the floor swept, the flock walked, and at night stories and humor and relatives.

Then we lost her, and I lost my footing. In Stone Breath I told it in three sentences. "I forgot the stone for a while. I tried to become someone else. I failed out of college after we lost Masan." The truest name I have for those wandering years is an identity crisis and an inferiority crisis, both at once. I had gone away to college and I began to mute the parts of me that felt too different, my background, my family's way of life, the rhythms of Big Mountain, until there was not much of me left in the room to do the studying.

I have told what came next in The Year I Failed Out of College. I went to my grandmother Ada's house, she let me rest a few nights, and then she put me to work. Out collecting herbs with my uncle, hands busy, enough came back for me to remember who I was and where I came from. I did go back to school. I finished at the top of my class, I was the Standard Bearer at graduation at Northern Arizona University in 1992, the one who carries the banner in front of the graduating class, and then I went on to my MBA.

People hear that and assume something big happened in between. What happened was small and daily. I was in college, starting a family, working, and I started with some practices. There were eight of them.

I am writing this for anyone, anywhere in the world, who is young and struggling, whether you are twenty or thirty-three or forty, in Nairobi or Kaunas or Manila or Phoenix. Maybe from the outside you look fine, the job, the phone, the commute, and on the inside a voice keeps saying you are not enough, and your confidence has come apart in your hands. I have family, and I see many younger people struggle, and I help some of them, often financially and with encouragement too. This is the encouragement, written down for anyone who needs it. I know that voice. It is the one I just named, an identity crisis and an inferiority crisis, both at once. What do you do when your confidence goes and you were the only one who saw it leave? How long is too long before you pick yourself back up? Is thirty already too late? Is forty?

Here is what I learned. I could not think my way out, and I tried. Confidence came back from the ground up, through small things done every day that proved to my body who I was, until the proof was too much to argue with. You were enough the whole time. The tools were never lost, they were set down, and the practices are how the body finds that out again.

These eight are what I actually did. See who you will be and act it out. Say the gratitude prayer. Stay grounded and go home. Always have one nice thing. Take care of all about you. Have hobbies. Choose friends you want to be like. Think positive.

1. See who you will be, and act it out

The first practice was visioning, and acting out what I would be, while I was out running by the Peaks. The acting out is the part people skip. It is one thing to picture a future from the couch, and another to carry yourself as that person for five miles, shoulders, breath, the way he speaks when it is his turn, until your body has been him for an hour and knows the way back.

I have used the same step for thirty years with whole communities. "You start with a vision of the future, and you do not let anyone stay in their head about it. You get them to feel it, how it feels to have arrived, the sounds and the smells and the people around them, and you have them put themselves there and act it out in their minds."

Why does it work? Because the body believes what it has rehearsed. Masan's father, Keh Yazh, the runner they called Sore Foot, did the other half of it. In Stone Breath I wrote that he "kept a museum of proof in his mind, every hard run, every time he pushed through." The vision looks forward and the museum looks back, and a person rebuilding their confidence needs both.

2. The gratitude prayer

The second was the gratitude prayer. Where I come from you give thanks before you ask the day for anything. Masan did it every morning of her life, and the way I set it down in the book is three steps: name what is real, give thanks, state the intention.

If you were not raised with a prayer, start with the stone breath. Hold a small smooth stone in your hand, never in your mouth. Give thanks first, out loud, for plain things. Then imagine the stone resting on your tongue and feel your jaw go soft. Breathe in for four. Hold for four. Breathe out slow for six. Hold for four. Five rounds.

The thanks is where the abundance mindset lives, and it is a long way from pretending everything is great. In The Far Messengers a Lithuanian songkeeper named Rasa Vaitkutė does it as a plain inventory every morning. Two legs. This work. My daughter asleep down the hall. She says the old carriers did not get themselves full so they could feel good. "They got full because a full carrier reads country better, arrives intact, and delivers. It was equipment for a job."

3. Stay grounded. Go home.

The third was being grounded, and for me that meant visiting my great-grandfather and Ada every other week when I could, all through college, starting a family, and working. Years ago I wrote it this way. "Aside from college, job, family, and running, I also spent my free time helping my aging grandparents."

I went to help, which is how Ada had brought me back in the first place, with a task in my hands. My great-grandfather built hogans and corrals and cornfields and trained horses and was the happiest man on that mesa. You cannot perform for people like that. They knew you before you were anything, and an afternoon of real work beside them puts you back in your right size.

So who is your Ada? Put the visit on the calendar and bring your hands. If home is not a safe place for you, an elder can be chosen, a coach, an auntie, a neighbor who has lived well.

4. Always have one nice thing

The fourth surprises people. Always have one nice thing, a car or an apartment.

Masan taught only abundance. As a boy I was, if anything, overdressed and probably overfed, and as I told a graduating class years later, "Our nicest piece of furniture was an all-wood kitchen table — and I was so proud of that table, especially when we had visitors." That table is the teaching. As a young father on a strict budget, mine was a truck. "With a young family I also spent our extra income on a dependable truck."

This is about self-respect and it has nothing to do with showing off. One thing, chosen with care, earned honestly, kept clean. Every time you step into it, it tells your body that you are a person who takes care of what you have. One is the number. It is not ten things, and it is not debt.

Somebody will have an opinion about it. I have written before that "I was concerned about how my own extended family would judge my decisions," and that this worry holds many people back from high achievement, in every society. Have the one nice thing anyway.

5. Take care of all about you

The fifth is to take care of all about you, your health, your clothes, and to honor yourself with jewelry.

In those rebuilding years I started dressing up, and I treated it as a ritual. The clothes were a signal to the world and a daily reminder to myself that I am someone who shows up prepared. We are taught that a person should be dignified with the decorations of mother earth and the sacred mountains, and as I wrote in 2019, "That is why its considered honorable to wear our jewelry as is the custom of most tribes." You are dressing the person your people prayed for, and your body hears it.

Health belongs here too, and it is plain. I am a storyteller and a planner, not a doctor, and none of this is medical advice. My grandfather walked six miles a day into his nineties and lived to one hundred and three. When Rasa is asked in the book what her day looks like, her whole list is "Sleep, movement, real food, real people, and a body that is not running from something." Sleep at an hour you can keep. Get outside in the morning light. Drink water. Walk every day. Eat food your great-grandmother would recognize, sitting down. Keep the phone out of the first minutes of the day and the last hour of the night.

6. Have hobbies

The sixth is to have hobbies. A life that is only work and worry has nowhere to rest.

On a student's budget with a family, "the only hobby I could spend time and money on was running," and running was enough. Later came golf and coaching baseball. Later still my daughter, in her first year of college, asked for help buying a motorcycle, so I bought two used enduro bikes and taught her to ride. Of course, I ended up crashing a lot more than her. These days I am an aspiring silversmith.

A hobby works because it is the one place where no one is grading you. You get to be a beginner and laugh, and your mind has somewhere to go that is neither the job nor the phone. Mine cost a pair of shoes.

7. Choose friends you want to be like

The seventh is to focus on friends who you want to be like.

When I went back to school I looked for the people who were going somewhere. "There were a few Native American kids in the College of Business who were really driven when I was starting my career, and a lot of professors looked out for me." What I remember most from that second run is that every door had a person behind it. A professor opened the door to the MBA, and the most intimidating professor I ever had was the one who nominated me to lead my graduating class.

You take on the pace of the people you run with, on a trail and in a life. You do not have to leave anybody to do this. You add. Find one person a few years ahead of you who lives the way you would like to live, and ask to carry something for them.

8. Think positive

The eighth is to think positive, and to stay away from negative conversations and thoughts.

My grandfather gave us one teaching and repeated it until it stuck. Think positive. I spent years assuming that was simple. It is a trained state, and he trained it every morning of his life. Masan's version was about the mouth. Speak only what you want built. She never asked anyone to pretend, our people are blunt, and you can say the true thing. Among the true things available to you there are several, and you are choosing which one to build with.

The other half is where you stand. You keep your distance from bitter people and bitter words, the talking bad about others, the rumors, the negative news that waits in your pocket every morning. When Keh Yazh raced among strangers there were sometimes words that cut, and what he trained for was a clear mind that would not react. He did not look back.

What a whole people can teach you

I am Lithuanian on one side of my family, and when a young person tells me they are not enough, I think about that small country on the Baltic Sea.

In 2024 the World Happiness Report ranked countries by the happiness of their people under thirty, and Lithuania came first in the world. Now consider what the grandparents of those young people came through. Their country was occupied in 1940. In one week of June 1941 more than seventeen thousand people were deported to remote parts of the Soviet Union, and by 1952 the number had passed one hundred and thirty thousand, most of them women and children, many sent to Siberia. Soviet rule lasted until 1990.

What do a people do through fifty years that give them no reason to hope? They kept small practices, together. They kept the language at the table. They kept the song festivals going, the Dainų šventė, thousands of voices in one field. In The Far Messengers Rasa says it this way. "They did not stop singing. In kitchens. Quietly. Grandmothers to granddaughters, for fifty years, songs about rye and about a girl going to a well — because the occupier could ban a nation and could not ban a harvest."

Then, on August 23, 1989, about two million people in Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia walked out to the roadside and held hands in one line, six hundred and seventy-five kilometers long, from Vilnius through Riga to Tallinn. They call those years the Singing Revolution. The following March, Lithuania declared its independence restored.

No single voice in that line could move an empire. Each one could sing its part, and the part, practiced daily, together, through years that offered nothing back, became a country whose young people are now the happiest on earth. That is the same arithmetic as the eight practices. Rasa says the young are coming back to the old songs today. "They arrive for anxiety and they leave with a language."

Japan teaches it with a bowl. In the craft called kintsugi, broken pottery is mended with lacquer and powdered gold, so the line of the break becomes the brightest part of the piece and part of its history. Nothing is hidden. If you feel broken open right now, that is where the gold goes.

It is okay to fall

The eight did not make me a person who never falls. They gave me a way back, and the way back is the whole skill.

In The Far Messengers a Hawaiian navigator named Nalu is asked what he does when he loses his calm in front of his own son. He says you repair it out loud, in front of him, and that the skill is "Returning. That's all it ever was."

We picture the road as a straight line, climbing. It is walked as a line that dips, at the exam, at the firing, at the injury, and keeps climbing anyway.

I am in good company, and so are you. I checked each of these.

Elon Musk was replaced as chief executive by the board of his own company, the one that became PayPal, in September 2000, while he was away on his honeymoon. He was twenty-nine. Oprah Winfrey, born into poverty in rural Mississippi, was taken off the evening news desk at a Baltimore television station in 1977, and the next year the same station put her on a local talk show.

Billy Mills, Oglala Lakota, raised on the Pine Ridge reservation in the United States and orphaned at twelve, went to the 1964 Olympics in Tokyo almost unknown and won the 10,000 meters from third place in the last stretch. No other runner from the Americas has won that race. Arvydas Sabonis of Lithuania suffered a devastating Achilles tendon injury in 1986, at twenty-one, had more surgery in 1988, and won Olympic gold that same year. Eliud Kipchoge, raised in Kenya by a single mother who taught school, finished seventh at the 2012 Kenyan trials and did not make the Olympic team, so he moved up to the marathon. In 2017 he tried to run one in under two hours and came in twenty-five seconds over. In Vienna in 2019 he ran 1:59:40.

What these lives show is that being fired, being turned away, flunking out and growing up with very little are ordinary features of the road, and so they cannot be evidence that you are on the wrong one.

The old hurt

Some of you are carrying something heavier than a lost job or a failed class. A house that was not safe. A parent who left or drank or died. Something done to you that was never yours to carry. I will name it plainly, and I will say just as plainly that you are whole. Rasa is asked what a person should do when the line they come from is broken. "Then you are the one it restarts with. That's not a consolation. That's an assignment."

I did not climb out alone, I had Ada and my uncle and a row of professors. So if what you are holding is heavier than a morning practice, bring it to someone you trust, an elder, a friend, or a counselor or doctor whose work this is.

Tomorrow morning

Do not start with all eight. I did not.

Tonight, find a small smooth stone and set it where your phone usually sleeps, and put the phone to charge across the room.

Tomorrow, before you touch the phone, go outside or stand at a window facing the light. Say three things you already have, out loud, plain ones. Then the breath, with the stone in your hand and never in your mouth. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Hold for four. Five rounds.

Then walk or run for ten minutes, and for those ten minutes be the person you are going to be. Each small thing you finish is one more piece of proof.

Before the day is over, pick one more of the eight. Call your Ada. Clean the one nice thing. Lay out good clothes for tomorrow.

If you would like company in it, First Light, a short morning practice, is at Ancestral Peaks, the stone breath is at ancestralpeaks.com/practice, and the stories are in Stone Breath and The Far Messengers.

So if you have read this far, whether you are twenty-two or thirty-eight, in whatever country this finds you, tired and wondering whether the window has closed, I encourage you to let go of the idea that you are late. You are enough to begin this morning, as you are. I was a young man who had lost his footing, and a few years later the same young man carried the banner in front of his graduating class. Nothing was added to me in between except eight things I did over and over. Masan used to wake me before dawn with a hand on my shoulder. The ancestors are walking, she would say. Don't let them find you asleep.

Ahéhee'. Ačiū. Thank you.
