Community Resilience

What a Million Community Plans Taught Me About One Life

The method I carried from community to community for thirty years works just as well on one person, and on the home around that person.

November 14, 2026

For most of my working life I have been the person at the front of the room with a marker in my hand, elders and young people sitting together, asking a whole community what it wants to become and then helping it get there. I have done that work in school gyms, tribal offices and conference rooms, on sustainable-development plans for the Kaibab-Paiute and for Cameron, and in more places than I could list for you. I tell people I have run it a million times. Some days it does not feel like an exaggeration.

Last year I left a well-paying executive position to finish my PhD, to write books, and to advise at what I can only call a whole other level. I chose the positive route out of that job and I do not dwell on the other side of it.

What I did not expect was that the method I had carried from community to community for thirty years would follow me into this new life, and that it would work just as well on one person, and on the home around that person, as it ever did on a town.

The sequence is simple, and I want to give it to you the way I actually run it. You start with a vision of the future, and you do not let anyone stay in their head about it. You get them to feel it, how it feels to have arrived, the sounds and the smells and the people around them, and you have them put themselves there and act it out in their minds. A community can do this. So can one person sitting at a kitchen table.

Then you ask about values, the ones that really matter. With a community I ask what values are so critical that you would never trade them away, and what you are proud of. With a person I ask what you are proud of in yourself and what you have already achieved, and I ask that second question on purpose, because so many of us are taught to carry our failures in the front pocket and our achievements somewhere near the bottom of the bag.

So the third step is what I call a museum of proof, and the one thing I have learned about it is that you have to give it a category or it never ends. What are your top five from the past? What are your top three from the last year? Write them down. A community that names its own wins before it plans anything plans differently, with its shoulders back, and a person does too.

The fourth step is the one people skip, and it is the one I care about most. Before you build anything, look at the future and ask what you want to keep. What unique things do you want to maintain, protect, carry forward no matter what? A community might name its language, its ceremonies, a piece of land. A person might name a morning practice, a friendship, a way of laughing that came down from a grandmother. You protect those first, and you plan around them.

Only then do you brainstorm, and I tell people to do it like a developer, which means you go after the highest feasible goals and not the timid ones. For a community that might be building a big resort, or finally taking on the housing crisis. For one person it might be finishing a PhD, or something that sounds smaller and is just as hard, like building real rest into every single day. Aim high there.

Then you develop a path. You pick what is truly doable, you build a plan that runs over several years, and you do your research on every avenue, because you have to research.

The last two steps keep the whole thing honest. You monitor and you adjust, the way any plan for a real place gets adjusted when the rain comes late or the funding comes early.

And you assess what you actually need to get there, which is the step that turns a vision into a budget, a schedule and a list of people to call. Nobody enjoys that step. It is the step people least want to take, and it is the one that decides whether the rest of the plan ever leaves the room.

When I build this out for a person I build it for their immediate home as well. If they have kids, the kids are in it. If they have a partner, the partner is in it. A plan made for one person alone, in a house where nobody else knows it exists, is a plan that gets argued with at dinner, and a good vision deserves better than that.

Here is the part that matters most to me. In community planning the hardest work is rarely the money or the engineering. It is the beliefs a place has inherited about itself, the stories that say we are not the kind of people who can build that, and a person carries the same kind of story. I'm not good with money. I'm too out of shape. I'm too old. I'm not smart. I don't have the education.

I have heard it said that when we are born we carry enough power to run a whole city for a week, and that we arrive with that power and no trauma at all.

Then life happens, and some of it happened long before we arrived, to our parents and grandparents in their own hard years, and the trauma teaches us that we have limits we were never actually born with. That is the intergenerational part of this work. The beliefs were handed down by people who were surviving, and they can be handed back, which is the healing.

So the change has to start in a realistic way, in the body and the mind together. Feel the progress you are making. Put yourself a year out, then two years, after you have made even more progress than you expected, and look around. What are you doing? Where are you? Which table are you sitting at, who is coming into your life, and what are you experiencing that you could not have imagined from where you sit now?

Writing my books has turned out to be the most healing work of my life, and I sometimes wonder where I would be if I had not taken these chances. In Stone Breath there is a young man in Lithuania whose voice shakes when he raises his hand in class, and Tonas gives him the simplest version of the museum of proof I know: get a notebook, and "write down every time you wanted to quit and didn't." It is the same exercise I have run with whole communities, shrunk to fit in one pocket. You can find the book at ancestralwatch.com/series/stone-breath.

So take the eight steps and use them without me. Do them at your own table with the people you live with, argue about the fourth one as long as you need to, and give your museum of proof a category so you are still on speaking terms by bedtime. A community is many homes deciding together what to become, and it was always going to start with one of them.

Ahéhee'.
