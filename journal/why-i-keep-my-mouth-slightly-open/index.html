Why I Keep My Mouth Slightly Open

Your job is not to worry, your job is to stay in the moment, and the body can be taught to do that one small piece at a time.

November 20, 2026

One morning in October I sat down early and recorded a short meditation, about eight minutes long, and I started it the way I start most of them, at a stream in the mountains or down in a canyon, with the water going by and the birds and the trees and the breeze, the morning dew still on everything and the sun barely coming up. In the picture I gave the listener, they had just finished their early morning run. They felt good. They felt powerful.

Then I asked them to walk the way a chief of the tribes would have walked a long time ago, lightly, feather-like, taking everything in, the clouds, the plants, the medicines, the aspen and the juniper and the pine and the oak, and the sweet smells that come up off a creek at that hour. That is how I was taught to move through country, and it is still the best instruction I know for a body that wants to rush.

You kneel down at the water and a pebble calls you, one out of all of them, and you pick it up and look at it. Think about the journey that rock has been on, thousands of years, maybe it came up out of the earth in the lava, maybe it broke off an old ledge, and then it rolled and it was shaped by the rivers and the storms for hundreds of miles, maybe thousands. And still it is here, this morning, in your hand. In the recording I hold it to my heart and I thank it, for taking the journey, for still being here.

The part I want to write about is what comes next, because it is small and physical and most people skip it. You imagine the pebble on your tongue and you have to keep it balanced there. You cannot lean forward, and you cannot throw your head back, so your shoulders find their level by themselves. You relax your jaw so you can hold it, you relax your temples, you slow your breathing down, and you leave your mouth slightly open.

In the recording I stop and ask the question out loud. You know what that's doing? It is slowing your heart rate, automatically, without you having to argue with it. It is keeping the moisture in your mouth, which matters to a runner out in dry country. And it is calming your nervous system. Three things, from one loose jaw and a small gap between your lips, and none of them ask you to believe anything.

I want to be careful here, because the stone is the oldest part of this and I teach it with one rule. In the practice I share, the stone stays in your hand and you only imagine it on your tongue. My great-great-grandfather, the runner I call Keh Yazh in my books, ran his races with a small stone held in his mouth, the old way, and he won races nobody expected him to win. You do not need the real stone in your mouth to get what he got. The body responds to the picture.

Then comes the breath. In through the nose for four, hold for four, out for six, hold for four, and again. The out-breath is longer than the in-breath on purpose, and that long, easy exhale is the whole mechanism, so I count it out every time.

One, two, three, four. Hold. Out, slowly, six. Hold.

A breath count on its own is easy to turn into a chore, so I pair it with the jaw and the temples and the shoulders, and I keep pairing it, every minute or so, the whole way through. When I teach it I say the same five words in the same order every time.

Temple. Jaws. Breath. Shoulders. Thoughts.

I do not vary those words and I do not dress them up. The last one surprises people, because they did not know you could ask a thought to relax the way you ask a shoulder to, and you can. You are not emptying the mind and you are not concentrating it. You are letting it go slack, the way a jaw goes slack, and after a few rounds the five words start to work like a switch you carry with you into the rest of the day.

Only after the body has settled do I bring in the gratitude, and on that October morning the list just came. You are here. You have wisdom, you have health, you have culture and ceremony, children and grandchildren, grandparents, elders, teachings. You have a passion, a job, a home you built with your own hands. You got educated. You are recognized for your work. Maybe you were raised by grandparents and got to see the honorable way of life up close, or maybe you are the one carrying it on now. You have responsibilities. That is a blessing too.

I put the gratitude after the breath on purpose, and before anything is asked for. A tight person who tries to be grateful mostly makes a list. A relaxed person feels it. The same words land somewhere completely different once the jaw lets go, and I would rather give you thirty seconds of real gratitude in a settled body than ten minutes of it in a clenched one.

Near the end of the recording I said something I keep coming back to. Your job is not to worry. Your job is to stay in the moment, to stay calm, to stay relaxed, at all times. Remember there is an eagle up there, flying, watching you every day, looking out in front of you and looking out behind you. Somebody has the long view. It does not have to be you, every minute, with your shoulders up around your ears.

I wrote Stone Breath around a man who teaches this exact thing for a living and still finds the breath turning into a technique in his own hands. Early in the book he takes eleven strangers down into a canyon in the dark, and one of them, a man from Tokyo, stops halfway down with his hands on his knees. Tonas gives him the stone and tells him to breathe into it, in through the nose and out long and slow. "You are not dying. Your body just thinks you are, and there is a difference." You can read the opening of Stone Breath free.

So here is what I would hand you, and you can do it before you finish your coffee. Find something small that fits in your palm. Hold it. Imagine it on your tongue, and let your jaw loosen until you could keep it balanced there, and leave your mouth slightly open. Breathe in for four, hold for four, out for six, hold for four, five times through. Then name three things you already have, out loud if you can, and go do the day lightly, feather-like, the way the old ones walked.

Ahéhee'.
