Global · One Operating System

Tibet, and the Unbroken Thread

August 7, 2026

A people is not its territory. It is the thread — the language, the prayer, the way of raising a child. Some peoples have carried that thread across the highest mountains on earth, and into exile, without letting it break.

I have twice been invited to speak with the Tibetan community at Machik Weekend — once in New York, and once online — a gathering of a people, many of them living in exile, thinking hard about the future of their nation. I came as a Diné man, a Navajo, whose own people were marched off their land within living memory. I did not come to teach them. I came to recognize them.

Because I know that face. It is the face of a people who were supposed to disappear — and did not. Tibetans carried their language, their prayers, their medicine, their Dharma up and over the Himalaya and set them down again in Dharamsala, in Queens, in Minneapolis. They kept teaching their children the old words in new cities. That is not survival. That is carrying — the work of the far messenger, who crosses the hardest ground and arrives with what the people need, whole.

Take the land, and a people can endure.
Take the thread, and it is gone. So they never let go of the thread.

What the exiled teach the comfortable

Here is the strange gift of a people who lost everything but the thread: they know exactly what a human being actually needs. Not the square footage. Not the title. The morning prayer. The elder's voice. The community that feeds you after the chant. The child who still knows who they are. Tibetans in exile, like the Diné after the Long Walk, like the Lithuanians under the ban — they ran the experiment the whole modern world is now anxiously running, and they came back with the answer: the operating system is portable, and it is enough.

I have sat in Buddhist halls and in hogans and in a Hare Krishna temple in Silicon Valley, and I keep meeting the same thing under different robes — the bell before the silence, the breath before the work, the bow to something larger. Compassion as a discipline, not a mood. This is the wisdom the world is starving for, and the people who nearly lost it hold it most tightly.

An offering of hope

So this is my offering to my Tibetan relatives, and to every people carrying a thread across hard ground: you are not behind. You are ahead. The world is turning back toward exactly what you refused to drop. Raise the children in it. Keep the language on your tongue. The far messengers are not only in the old stories — they are the grandmother teaching a grandchild to pray in a language the map tried to erase. That is the whole of it. That is a formula that works.

Tony Skrelūnas is Diné (Navajo) and Lithuanian. He writes The Ancestral Watch — Stone Breath, The Amber and the Stone, The Council Fire and The Far Messengers — and the working reader, Living as a Far Messenger.
