Reflections

One in a Trillion

Invited to Abundance Week at Rancho La Puerta to meet Tom Blue Wolf and Pat Mitchell — and the hour under the papel picado that came of it.

August 5, 2026

There is a mountain in Baja California that the Kumeyaay call Kuchumaa, and at the foot of it there is a lawn where, in the morning, people gather in a loose circle to be walked into the day. On the second of August I stood in that circle while a man played a wooden flute into the cool air, and I thought about my great-grandmother.

I have presented at Rancho La Puerta four times in the last three years, usually bringing a group and building the session with them. This time was different. This time I was invited — to Abundance Week, and specifically to meet Tom Blue Wolf and Pat Mitchell.

The invitation came out of the writing. Metamodernism, and how we prepare people to be far messengers: nervous-system control first, then the long work of building a future our seventh generation can stand in, with everything happening in the world right now happening anyway. That is The Council Fire, more or less. Rancho asked me to come and to sit down for Resonate, their broadcast, which is not a thing I take lightly.

That matters, because it changes what you see. A guest arrives at a place like this to be restored. I arrived to study how restoration is made — how a room is held, how a week is built, what happens to a person on the Tuesday after they leave.

What I did not have was any way to prepare for a lunch table.

The table

His name is Tom Blue Wolf. Muskogee Creek elder, founder of EarthKeepers, which he has run as a nonprofit since 1970 — fifty-six years, begun the year after he came home from Vietnam. He turns eighty-one in February. He is the only person I have met in a decade who does not carry a phone.

It was an honour to meet him. It was an honour to meet Pat Mitchell that week too, and to see her film.

We sat under the papel picado for the better part of an hour, and the conversation went where conversations go when nobody is performing. It started with names. How did Crazy Horse get a name like that? Tom explained: the real name was closer to one whose horse is crazy. And then he told the part I hadn't heard — that Crazy Horse once set out to find the white buffalo, was gone two years, came back empty-handed, and they took his name and gave him a new one. Little Worm. He was humiliated. So he turned around, went back out, and came home with a white buffalo robe. They gave him his name back.

I told him about my great-grandmother. She raised me. My great-grandfather was a Hogan builder and was rarely home, so it was her and me and the sheep. She taught me essentially everything I know. She had a kerosene lamp, and that lamp is where I learned to read. Years later I found the old table we used to sit at in that Hogan. I repaired it. It is my desk. I write on it every day.

Tom listened to all of that the way people listen when they aren't waiting for their turn.

Then he said the thing I have not been able to put down since.

"The chances of me and you sitting at this table are one in a trillion. Your ancestors and my ancestors avoided the genocide long enough for us to walk on the earth."

Tom Blue Wolf

He said he gets tears in his eyes when he thinks about it. So do I. Because the sentence does something precise: it takes a pleasant coincidence — two men from two nations meeting at a health resort in Mexico — and reveals it as the improbable output of a very long survival. Neither of our lines was supposed to make it. We are the part that made it. And therefore, as he put it, "we carry the torch, so we're going to be the ancestors of the future."

What he actually teaches

It would be easy to file Tom Blue Wolf under wisdom, which is a way of admiring something so you don't have to use it. He is more useful than that. He has a working theory of what went wrong and a practice for fixing it.

The theory: humans were designed to be the earth's immune system. Shepherds, stewards, caregivers. "When we abandoned that post, everything went to hell." He is not being metaphorical about the stakes. He cited the number that has been sitting in my chest since: forty years ago, roughly half of the animal life on earth was humans and our domesticated animals, and half was wild. This year the figure is ninety-six percent to four.

The practice is smaller and stranger and better. He takes young people — including kids sentenced to him out of an adolescent substance abuse program in Atlanta — into the forest, takes their devices, and teaches them what is food, what is not food, what is medicine. Then he teaches them the song of the food.

"Because plants will treat you different. Just like dogs — when you sing to them."

He explained the mechanism the way a physicist would, if physicists were allowed to be lyrical. Starlight enters the leaves. The tree carries it down as sap into the roots, and the roots hand the energy and the information to the mycelium, and the mycelium is the nervous system of the mother. Touch the earth anywhere, she feels it everywhere. Which is why ceremony works at all: you don't have to scratch the whole body to make the whole body feel better.

The coyote and the heart

The line I will steal, with permission and attribution, is this one.

Tom says we think the coyote lives in the head. The coyote is a good talker. He knows what you want to hear and exactly how you want to hear it. But the coyote does not care whether it is true.

"He's like AI. He don't care if it's true or not. He just tells a good story. The heart may not tell a good story — but it's always true."

I build things for a living — governance structures, mostly, and lately tools. I have spent this year with my hands inside systems that are extremely good at telling a good story. To hear an eighty-year-old man in the forest name the failure mode that precisely, without ever having owned a phone, was the single most clarifying thing that happened to me in Baja.

His practice, then, is to take a young person out of their head and drop them into their heart. Sometimes literally. He described tying a kid who was giving him static to a rock in the middle of a creek and leaving him there all day. "By the end of the day he's cooing like a dove."

I laughed. Then I stopped laughing, because he told me about Zimbabwe.

There is a village that has stood for a thousand years, where everyone knows everyone and everyone's relatives. One day a boy was fighting with another boy, knocked him down, and the other boy hit his head on a rock and died. It was the first death like that in a very long time.

So they took the boy, tied him to a chair in the center of the village, and blindfolded him. And then everyone came out and sang him his song — because there, you are given a song when you are born. They sang him his song. They told him about his ancestors. They told him about his mother and father. Hundreds of people, all day long. He cried like a baby.

By evening, in one of the huts, a girl was giving birth. They led him in, still blindfolded, and the baby came out of her and into his hands.

That morning he took a life. That evening he delivered one.

"He cried like a baby. He went into a fetal position, and became a priest."

Tom's summary: "That's rehabilitation. You could have put him in prison for ten years, he never would have come to that realization." Ours is a country with more people in prison than most of the rest of the world combined. Penalization, he says, doesn't rehabilitate anybody — you send someone away for twenty years, they come out better at corruption. "We think you need to be healed if you do something wrong."

Love is the healer. Forgiveness is the medicine.

The oak tree's timeline

On Monday night he gave the eight o'clock talk. At Rancho La Puerta that is the marquee slot — nothing else is scheduled against it, the whole house comes. The room is called the Oaktree.

He opened by saying he'd walked the property that day listening, and it had brought tears to his eyes. The foliage singing to the water, the water singing to the people. "I don't know if the people hear it, but the songs are awesome. They're celestial."

Then he said he found it interesting that he'd been asked to speak in a room called the Oaktree, because some time back he had traded his own timeline for an oak tree's.

He'd been thinking about light. They say it takes light from the sun eight minutes to reach the earth. "No," he said. "It takes us eight minutes to see it. It's here all the time."

So he asked the oak tree about time. And the oak tree said: Give it up, Tom. Don't measure your life by time spent. Measure it by distance traveled.

"Do you know how far I've come? In your world, I've been here three hundred years. In my world, I've traveled over a hundred billion miles. And as long as I have a community to care for, and as long as there's a community to care for me, I will remain here — until the distance is right."

The oak tree, as reported by Tom Blue Wolf

I have been trying to build institutions for twenty years, and I have never heard a cleaner statement of what an institution is for. Not duration. Distance. And the condition of continuing is mutual care in both directions — I have a community to care for, and a community to care for me. Take either half away and the tree comes down.

He added, almost as an aside, that it is probably no accident that somebody once decided to call this place the universe. It means one song. Which is why, when a child is born, you give them a song — because they don't have one yet, and these days there is so much static in the atmosphere that it's become hard to find the one inside you.

Somebody who knew somebody

Partway through he did the arithmetic that reorganizes the room.

His great-grandfather was born in 1837, on the Trail of Tears, on the way to what is now Oklahoma. He lived until 1954. He was a hundred and sixteen years old when he died. Tom was eight.

"So now you all know somebody who knew somebody on the Trail of Tears."

That is two handshakes from a forced march to a wellness resort in Baja, and he laid it down without any drama at all. Then he kept going: go back seven generations and you have twelve hundred ancestors. "So our people say you walk around with twelve hundred ancestors inside of you all the time. Guiding you. Protecting you. If you're able to receive."

And then the turn, which is the whole talk: "You're going to be an ancestor forever. You might only be here for less than a hundred years."

How do we stay resilient?

Near the end of the evening someone asked him the question I had come to Baja carrying: How do we stay resilient as we become the storytellers — as we become the messengers?

He answered by describing the sweat lodge. Four rounds.

The first is about waking up. "A lot of people don't realize some of us are sleepwalking." You wake up, and you become the one you dream of. "Heroes aren't out there. They're in there. We have to wake them up."

The second is healing, growth, transformation. The third is honoring the ancestors — and here he made the move he keeps making: when we honor them, we are also honoring the ancestor in us, because one day we will be one. It is not a backward-facing ritual. It is rehearsal.

And then, he said, the ancestors enter the lodge with us and give us strength and courage. Which he defined precisely, and not the way I expected:

Strength is knowing how to love somebody very deeply. Courage is knowing how to receive love.

"Some people can give you love but don't know how to receive it. Some people can receive your love but don't know how to give it. You've got to be both."

That is his answer to burnout among people who carry messages. Not discipline, not boundaries, not any of the vocabulary I would have reached for. The capacity to receive.

The rest of the week

I don't want to leave the impression that five days at Rancho La Puerta is one long conversation with an elder, though that would have been enough.

There was Dr. Sharon Horesh Bergquist making the case that the body is built to be challenged — that stress in the right dose, recovered from, is the actual mechanism of regeneration, and that three one-minute bursts of vigorous effort a day measurably move your mortality risk. There was Tracey Lukes asking a room full of accomplished people what actually gets in the way, and getting back: fear of conflict, time, and you forget how to enjoy.

There was a morning practice — circulation, activation, mobilization, meditation, manifestation — that begins by tapping your own body awake while saying out loud, every cell in my body is well, and which I have done nearly every morning since. It was the best experience of the week, and I did not expect that.

And there was Pandora Thomas, who runs EARTHseed Farm in Sonoma County, telling a room how her mother Frances was born into sharecropping in South Carolina, moved north at three in the Great Migration, and still taught her daughter to love the land that had been used against her family.

Pandora opened by asking us to turn to a neighbor and name an ancestor. Go back and fetch. That is what Sankofa means, from the Akan: go back and get it. The past isn't behind you. It's a resource you're allowed to use.

That is the exercise where I told a stranger about the kerosene lamp.

What I'm taking

Tom gave the room one concrete protocol, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. Remove gossip, rumors, criticism and judgment from your conversation. Replace them with dreams, ideas, visions and solutions. "That's a good start on altering the course of humanity. It's got to start somewhere. I would say it starts right here."

Kuchumaa was a gathering place long before it was a resort, and the people who governed here answered to a council. That is not decoration; it is the actual precedent for the thing I keep trying to build. Individual healing, on its own, will not save us. You need the container too. You need a fire that people sit around, and enough of them to constitute a council.

I went to Baja with a call sheet and a strategy. I came home with a collaborator — Tom and I are going to write together — and with a sentence I intend to live inside.

"The world is perfect. There's just a lot of people having a bad conversation."

He says it before ceremonies. Then he asks everyone present to make one vow to the universe: to raise the level of our personal poetry, so that everything we say to each other reflects what we hold dear about what it means to be the people.

One in a trillion, that table. I'm not going to waste it.
