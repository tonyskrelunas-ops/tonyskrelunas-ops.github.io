The 5K I Meant to Jog

I was going to use it as a training run, but my competitiveness really came out.

November 24, 2026

On the Sunday of Labor Day weekend I lined up for a 5K through the mountains around Flagstaff, at a gathering where hundreds of people had been camping since Thursday. I told myself on the way to the start that this one was a training run. I have been running a 5K every weekend, and the plan was to keep it easy, keep the breath smooth and save the legs for the next one. Then the race started.

My competitiveness really came out, the way it does, and somewhere between the start and the finish the training run turned into a race. I came in about fifth overall and first in my age category, right behind the top runners, and every one of them was thirty-two on down. I am not thirty-two. I have not been thirty-two for a long time, and I felt really good about that.

The next morning I sat down to breakfast with my coffee, looking at the sacred San Francisco Peaks, and recorded a meditation for the people who listen to them. I had been awake since right before five, when the coyotes started, and then the roosters, and a dog walked around me the whole time I was talking. I opened with the race, because I wanted to start with gratitude, and that was the freshest thing I had.

Then I widened it out, the way I always do, because the race was only my blessing for that morning. There's a lot to be grateful for, I said. It could be your health. It could be your wisdom, that you grew up around elders. Maybe you know some songs, or prayers, or you got very educated. Maybe you know how to cook so amazing, or you have humor, or a really good family. Everybody has a certain blessing.

I put health first on that list without thinking about it, and I have thought about it since. Most of us only notice our health when it is gone, the way you only notice a spring when you walk up to it and it is dry. A race every weekend is my way of checking the spring on purpose, while it is still running, and thanking it, and asking it for a little more than I asked last week. Most weeks it says yes.

I come from runners, so maybe none of this is my own idea. My great-great-grandfather, the one I call Keh Yazh in my books, ran his races with a small stone held in his mouth, the old way, and he won races nobody expected him to win. Those races at the big gatherings were spiritual things, my great-grandmother told me, and if you came unprepared they could wipe you out, so you prepared, the breath and the songs and the sweat.

She used to send me out running early in the morning, toward the east, and she told me the breathing and the discipline would make my mind stronger. I was a kid and I thought she was talking about my legs. Years later I ran a 4:20 mile as a freshman at Montezuma, a working ranch school where you moved at your own pace, and I still did not understand that she had been talking about my mind the whole time.

I also come from people who did not take the hint when the calendar told them to sit down. My grandfather lived to a hundred and three, walked six miles a day into his nineties and herded sheep into that same decade. My Lithuanian grandfather, Grandpa Al, built his own motorcycles and raced them up near-vertical hills into his seventies. Neither one of them ever explained it. They just kept showing up for the next thing, and the next. That was the teaching.

So when my competitiveness came out on that mountain course, I did not scold it. I think it is one of the healthiest things a person can still have at my age, the part of you that sees a younger runner up ahead and decides, without asking permission, that you would like to see how close you can get. That is the body telling you it is still in the race, and it is worth listening to. Mine was loud that day.

There is a warning in the old stories too, and I try to keep it in front of me. In The Council Fire I wrote the oldest grandfather of every people as a runner, crossing seventy miles of high desert to carry what he had seen, and his first words to the reader are about what he holds while he does it. "I run with a stone in my hand." He prepares for eleven days before he is allowed to run at all.

And in that same telling there is the other kind of young man, the one who decides the training is slow and he will simply go faster, and he is never the hero of any song. On a Sunday morning in Flagstaff I could feel a little of him in me. The difference, I hope, is all the other weekends, the ordinary runs nobody sees, which are where the race actually gets decided. You can read the opening of The Council Fire, with that whole run in it, free.

So here is what I would hand you, and you do not need a mountain or a gathering for it. Find a race you can enter, a 5K, a fun run, a walk around the lake with a clock at the end of it, and put it on the calendar. Train for it a little every week. Then on the day, let your competitiveness out, just once, and see what your body still has in it.

And the morning after, before you check the results or your phone or anything else, sit down with your coffee and thank the body that carried you there. Thank the people who ran before you, whoever they were, because somebody did. Then go find the next one.

Ahéhee'.
