Gifts Light Enough to Carry

They make you gifts, they make you food, they make you things that are light that you can carry.

November 20, 2026

Early on a September morning in Flagstaff I sat down and recorded a meditation that ran eighteen minutes, and partway through it I heard myself describe a people far to the east who are singing, singing the ancient songs for moisture and for abundance and for plentiful harvests. They work really hard. They still get up very early, they still teach their kids to read the skies and the winds and the animals and the earth, and they still know how to make a living in the desert, in the mountains, in the canyons.

What I asked the listener to do next is the part I have been thinking about since. You hear them singing, and you feel a call to make the journey, and the call is to carry a blessing to them, to carry gratitude to them, to share what you have. Maybe it is to teach music to the kids who want to learn. Maybe it is to share your wisdom and to learn theirs. You go to give first.

Most of the journeys we take in a life run the other direction. We go to get the job, to get the degree, to get the deal or the lesson or the rest we have earned, and there is nothing wrong with any of that, I have taken plenty of those trips myself. But the oldest kind of traveling I know of started with a gift in your hands and a song ready in your mouth.

My great-grandmother used to tell me about her father racing against other peoples at the big gatherings, where the Hopi and the Zuni and the Choctaw and the Chickasaw and the Plains nations came together and stayed for days. There was wrestling, there was dancing, there was food exchanged between the camps, and the races themselves were spiritual things. They shared a lot, and we loved coming together.

In October I recorded the ending of a much longer meditation, the one I made for driving and working out, and in it the traveler finally arrives. You can hear the singing before you see anyone, and then the drumbeat, and then the welcome. Welcome, granddaughter. Welcome, grandson. We are so happy you made this journey. We don't get visitors often, so we are happy to host you and share our wisdom so you can take it back to your family.

I love that line because it puts the host and the guest on the same footing right away. Nobody is the giver and nobody is the one in need. You are there, among them, and you see the beauty, the honor, the way they still live, the crops and the ceremonies and the poise and the teachings and the discipline, and the whole place runs on an honor code that nobody has to explain to you.

Then it goes both ways, the way it is supposed to. You rest, you are fed, and you are taught songs and prayers, and when you are ready you give back. You share your knowledge, your stories and your songs, you share your prayers, your gifts, your special talents. On the recording I said it plainly, there has been a really good trade, there has been a real happiness, and I still think that is the best description of a visit I know.

And then this, which is the line I keep returning to. They make you gifts. They make you food. They make you things that are light, that you can carry.

Light enough to carry. The people who made those gifts understood something about a traveler that we forget when we pack for a trip, which is that the road home is long and every ounce counts by the second day. A gift that weighs a person down was never really for them. The best things I was ever handed, a song, a way of breathing, a joke I still tell, the habit of saying thank you before my feet touch the floor, weigh nothing at all, and I have carried them my whole life without once setting them down.

I think that is true for families too, and for what one generation hands the next. We tend to imagine an inheritance as something heavy, a house or a business or a long list of obligations, and some of it is. But most of what keeps a family resilient across a hundred years is small and portable. A morning prayer. The way an uncle laughs when things go wrong. A recipe. The habit of showing up. The young ones can only carry what we make light enough for them to lift.

There is one more detail near the end of that recording. Some of the people walk with you as you leave, and the animals and the birds walk with you, and the wind and the waters and the clouds, everybody happy, because you are on a good journey and this is the meaning of life. You may never take that journey again, so you are grateful, you watch everything, and you carry it home to share the abundance with your own house.

I wrote The Council Fire around exactly this kind of carrying. Twelve keepers on six continents, none of whom has heard the others speak, and each of them holds one small object. Those objects come down seven generations to a single descendant who finally sits with all twelve in hand, a lump of coal, an amber bead, "a smooth river stone worn by many thumbs." None of them is heavy. All of them made the crossing. You can read about The Council Fire and its twelve keepers, and the opening is free to read there.

So the next time you visit, whether it is your parents, a friend's family across the country, a new team at work, or a gathering where you know nobody yet, decide on the way there what you are bringing that is light. Your song, your prayer, your gratefulness, your good energy, your smile and your laughter all fit in a pocket. Then watch for what they hand you, and carry it home.

Keep the good journey going.

Ahéhee'.
