The Second Meeting – Nemawashi

The Second Meeting – Nemawashi

The next day Tonas did something no one expected. He didn't call another big meeting — instead he visited people one-on-one — Old Jonas in his kitchen, Marija in her bakery, the carpenter in his workshop, the teenagers where they hung out behind the mill, smoking. He didn't pitch. He asked questions, and he listened. In Japan they call this nemawashi, preparing the roots before you transplant the tree: you talk to everyone privately before you bring them together publicly, you find out what they really think, and you address their fears before they become fights. By the third day he knew who was afraid of change, who worried about money, who didn't trust outsiders, who wanted credit, and who just wanted to help. He also knew who the natural leaders were — not the loudest, but the ones everyone listened to when they spoke quietly.

The Third Meeting – The Circle

A week later they gathered again, same room, same people, but this time Tonas arranged the chairs in a circle. "No head of the table," he said. "No back row. Everyone can see everyone." He placed a small stone in the center of the circle. "This stone represents what we're building. It doesn't belong to any one of us. It belongs to the circle." He asked the four questions again. This time, the answers were more focused. "What do we love?" – The river. The forest. The old recipes. The children. "What is hurting?" – The empty café. The loneliness. The young people leaving. "What do we have?" – Ona's recipes. Rytis's hands. The carpenter's tools. The baker's oven. The old building. "What's the smallest thing we could do in six months?" – Clean out the old café. Paint it. Put in a few tables. Start with one night a week – a community dinner, cooked by volunteers, using food from local gardens. Then add a small market table for people to sell their bread, eggs, pickles, mushrooms. Then, if it works, open two nights. Then three.

The Garden

The Garden

The next morning Rytis showed Tonas a patch of land behind the old café. “This used to be a garden,” he said. “My grandmother grew everything here. Potatoes, carrots, beets, dill. The café used to buy from her.” “We could do that again,” Tonas said. “A communal garden. Anyone who wants to help can plant. Anyone who needs food can take. The café buys what's left.” Rytis nodded. “The old way. Before supermarkets. Before everyone forgot how to grow.” They spent the morning turning the soil, and the teenagers showed up, not many but a few, and they complained about the work and stayed anyway, and by noon they had planted potatoes, carrots, and a row of sunflowers just because. “Sunflowers don't feed anyone,” Rytis said. “They feed the soul,” one of the teenagers said. Rytis looked at Tonas. “She's right.”

The Market Table

The Market Table

The first Saturday they set up a table outside the old café, and Marija brought bread, Rytis brought mushrooms, Old Jonas brought smoked fish, a young mother brought eggs, and Jurgita brought pickles made from her grandmother's recipe. Only a few people came. But they bought. And they talked. And they said: “We'll come back next week.” The second Saturday more people came, and the third Saturday someone brought honey and someone else brought wool socks and a teenager sold handmade candles, and by the end of the first month the market table had become a market. Not big. Not fancy. Just a few tables on the sidewalk, people selling what they made, people buying what they needed. “This is how it used to be,” Old Jonas said. “Before the big stores. Before everyone forgot each other.”

The Grant and the Fundraising

The Grant and the Fundraising

The grant application was rejected. “Not enough demonstrated community support,” the letter said. Tonas read it twice, then called a meeting. “They're wrong,” he said. “We have community support. We just need to prove it.” They planned six events over two months — a bake sale, a raffle, a community dinner, a talka to paint the café, a sweat-equity day where volunteers earned meal vouchers, and a silent auction with items donated by local businesses. Old Jonas donated a hand-carved walking stick, Marija a week's worth of bread, Rytis a mushroom-foraging walk, and the teenagers sold handmade candles. Then, a break. A wealthy businessman from Vilnius read about their efforts in a local newspaper and called Tonas. “I grew up in a village like this,” he said. “I'll match whatever you raise. Euro for euro. Up to fifty thousand euros. But it has to be sustainable — an endowment that pays for operations in perpetuity.” Tonas hung up and called another meeting. “We have a matching donor,” he said. “Now we need to raise the rest.” By the end of the second month they had raised eleven thousand euros, the businessman matched it, and the endowment was seeded — enough, with the grant, to carry the café through its first years without a landlord at the door. Tonas called the foundation. “We have the money. We have the community. We have the plan.” They sent a new proposal, and this time it was approved.
